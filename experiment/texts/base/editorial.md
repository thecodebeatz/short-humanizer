# Mufasa Should Have Lived

Mufasa should not have died in *The Lion King*. Not because children’s movies must be cheerful, not because fictional animals deserve immunity from tragedy, and not because the scene fails. It succeeds almost unbearably well. The stampede, Scar’s betrayal, Simba nudging his father’s motionless body: few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force.

But emotional force is not the same thing as narrative necessity. Mufasa’s death is so effective that it has largely escaped a more interesting question: Was killing him actually the best choice? I would argue no. Keeping Mufasa alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up—without sacrificing suspense or giving Scar a softer landing.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. The father’s removal creates the son’s journey. It gives Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. Without that loss, the argument goes, there is no movie.

There might, however, have been a better one.

## A Father Is More Than a Formative Tragedy

Mufasa is compelling because he is not merely powerful. He is affectionate, occasionally exasperated, playful with his son, and serious about the obligations of leadership. His lesson that a king must understand the balance of the kingdom suggests an ethical framework larger than royal privilege. He wants Simba to recognize that authority means service, not simply getting his way.

Then the film removes him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation.

That is a familiar bargain in children’s storytelling: a parent becomes most useful when absent. Death clears the stage, grants the young protagonist independence, and turns complicated family relationships into motivating memories. It is efficient. It is also limiting.

Growing up does not always require losing a parent. Sometimes it requires discovering that a beloved parent is fallible, disagreeing with that parent, and accepting responsibility while that parent is still watching. Those experiences are less tidy than inheriting a dead father’s wisdom. They are also recognizable to children whose central challenge is not bereavement but learning how to become themselves.

Simba could have matured alongside Mufasa rather than in the enormous shadow cast by his death.

## Let Scar’s Betrayal Have Consequences Beyond a Body Count

Keeping Mufasa alive would not mean eliminating Scar’s attempted murder. The stampede could remain. So could the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. But Mufasa could survive—gravely injured, stranded beyond the Pride Lands, and unable to return.

Scar, believing his brother dead or determined to finish the job, could seize power. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie. The kingdom could still suffer. The central conflict would remain intact.

What changes is the range of dramatic possibilities.

A surviving Mufasa would have to confront the fact that he failed to recognize the danger within his own family. The confident ruler who could face down hyenas would discover that strength does not make someone invulnerable to manipulation. He might need help. He might no longer be capable of reclaiming his position through physical dominance.

That would give the film something its original structure largely avoids: an older authority figure forced to change.

Instead, death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He becomes an ideal, available to offer guidance but never required to reconsider anything. A living Mufasa could still be wise. He would simply have to be a character rather than an answer.

## Simba Needs Agency, Not an Empty Throne

The strongest objection is that a surviving Mufasa would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

Because leadership should involve more than being the only available heir.

Imagine Simba learning that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. Returning would then require more than accepting his place in a succession. He would have to act without the comforting assumption that Mufasa could handle everything.

Perhaps Mufasa’s injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps his return would expose him to immediate capture. Either way, Simba would have to build alliances, persuade a frightened pride, and take risks on behalf of others. He could not merely wait for his father to restore order.

That is a strong test of adulthood: recognizing that the person who once protected you now needs your help.

It would also allow Simba to establish a leadership style distinct from Mufasa’s. The question would no longer be only whether he can live up to his father. It would be what he can contribute that his father cannot.

## Grief Is Valuable. It Is Not Mandatory.

None of this diminishes what Mufasa’s death has meant to audiences. For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. That value deserves acknowledgment.

But a film’s capacity to console does not place its creative choices beyond criticism.

The defense that “children need stories about death” is true in general and insufficient here. Children also need stories about disability, recovery, reconciliation, and the changing relationship between parents and children. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all four.

Nor would survival erase consequences. Mufasa could lose his health, his authority, or his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family. Scar’s rule could still devastate their home. Survival need not mean restoration to an untouched past.

A happy outcome is not automatically an easy one.

## The Braver Story Would Let Him Stay

Mufasa’s death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound. It helps make the movie memorable, and arguing against it means questioning one of the film’s greatest artistic successes.

Still, unforgettable is not synonymous with indispensable.

The more challenging choice would have been to let Mufasa live and deny both father and son their familiar roles. The father could not remain an invincible protector. The son could not remain a protected child. They would have to discover a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

That story would retain betrayal, danger, moral urgency, and a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. What it would lose is the assumption that a loving father must become a memory before his child can become a leader.

Mufasa should have lived—not so Simba could avoid growing up, but so the film could show how much growing up remains possible when a parent survives.

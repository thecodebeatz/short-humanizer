# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa’s death in *The Lion King* works almost unbearably well. The stampede, Scar’s betrayal, Simba nudging his father’s motionless body: few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. I still think the film should have let him live.

Keeping Mufasa alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, with plenty of suspense and consequences for Scar. The success of the death scene makes that possibility easy to overlook.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. His death gives Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. Without that loss, the argument goes, there is no movie. But an injured, absent Mufasa could have set much of the same story in motion while leaving both father and son more room to change.

## Growing up with a living father

Mufasa is affectionate, occasionally exasperated, and playful with his son. He also takes leadership seriously. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom asks Simba to look beyond royal privilege and consider what he owes the creatures he will govern. Simba, who mostly likes the prospect of getting his own way, has a lot to learn from him.

The film removes Mufasa before those lessons can become a sustained conversation. It makes a familiar bargain in children’s storytelling: a parent’s death clears the stage for the child’s independence, turning a complicated relationship into a motivating memory.

There are other ways to grow up. A child can discover that a beloved parent is fallible and learn to disagree with them. Taking responsibility while that parent is still watching brings its own discomfort. These experiences are less tidy than inheriting a dead father’s wisdom, and they are recognizable to children struggling to become themselves within a loving family.

I would have liked to see Simba mature alongside Mufasa, with opportunities to question him as well as learn from him.

## What Scar’s betrayal could change

The attempted murder could remain almost exactly as it is. The stampede, the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall could leave Mufasa gravely injured, stranded beyond the Pride Lands, and unable to return.

Scar could seize power, either believing his brother dead or intending to finish the job. Simba could still flee because of Scar’s lie, and the kingdom could still suffer under Scar’s rule.

Meanwhile, Mufasa would have to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. A ruler confident enough to face down hyenas would have to reckon with how thoroughly his brother had deceived him. His injuries might leave him dependent on help and unable to reclaim his position by force.

The original film largely spares Mufasa that kind of change. Death preserves him at the height of his moral stature. He can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. A surviving Mufasa could remain wise while struggling with mistakes and limitations his son had never seen.

## Giving Simba a reason to act

The strongest objection is that a surviving Mufasa would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

That objection depends on Mufasa being able to save the kingdom himself. Imagine Simba discovering that his father is alive but cannot put things right. He would have to return knowing that the parent who once protected him now needed his help.

Perhaps Mufasa’s injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps returning would expose him to immediate capture. Simba would have to find allies and persuade a frightened pride to act. He would need to take risks for others, without waiting for his father to restore order.

This would also give Simba a chance to develop a leadership style of his own. With Mufasa alive, they could disagree about what to do. Simba could discover what he had to offer that his father could not, and Mufasa would have to learn to trust him with responsibilities he could no longer carry himself.

## Loss without death

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with death. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. Those are good reasons to value the film as it stands, though they do not settle whether killing Mufasa was its best creative choice.

Children need stories about death. They also need stories about disability and recovery, about reconciliation within a family, and about relationships that change as people age. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for those experiences.

His survival would still leave lasting damage. He could lose his health or his authority, along with his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could lose years with his family. Scar’s rule could devastate their home. Even after Scar’s defeat, father and son would have to live with what had happened to them.

That ending could be happy without returning anyone to an untouched past.

## A different relationship

Arguing against Mufasa’s death means questioning one of the film’s greatest artistic successes. It gives *The Lion King* much of its emotional force and helps make the movie unforgettable. I understand why so many viewers see it as essential.

Yet letting Mufasa survive would have asked something difficult of both characters. He would have to give up being the invincible protector Simba remembered. Simba would have to accept that his father’s survival did not mean he could go back to being a protected child. Together, they would have to work out a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

The story could still have betrayal and danger, with the fate of the Pride Lands depending on Simba’s willingness to act. His father’s presence would complicate that responsibility. Mufasa should have lived because watching Simba become a leader while his father learned to depend on him could have been just as moving.

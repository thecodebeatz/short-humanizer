# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have survived *The Lion King*. Children's movies can include tragedy, and fictional animals have no special claim to immunity from it. His death scene succeeds almost unbearably well. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched the emotional force of the stampede, Scar's betrayal, and Simba nudging his father's motionless body.

That success has largely spared the decision to kill Mufasa from scrutiny. Was his death actually the best choice for the story? I would argue no. Keeping him alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up while preserving the suspense and giving Scar just as much to answer for.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. Removing the father creates the son's journey, giving Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. According to this argument, the movie depends on that loss. I think a better movie might have allowed them both to live.

## A father who stays

Mufasa's appeal comes partly from the range of his character. He is powerful and affectionate, occasionally exasperated, playful with his son, and serious about the obligations of leadership. His lesson about understanding the balance of the kingdom suggests an ethical framework that extends beyond royal privilege. He wants Simba to understand authority as service rather than permission to get his way.

The film removes him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation.

Children's stories often make this bargain: a parent becomes most useful when absent. Death clears the stage and grants the young protagonist independence. Complicated family relationships become motivating memories. That efficiency comes at the cost of exploring the relationship itself.

Growing up can involve discovering that a beloved parent is fallible, disagreeing with that parent, and accepting responsibility while that parent is still watching. Those experiences are less tidy than inheriting a dead father's wisdom. Children learning how to become themselves, whose central challenge is not bereavement, might recognize them. Simba could have matured alongside Mufasa.

## Scar's attempted murder could still happen

The stampede could remain, along with the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar, believing his brother dead or determined to finish the job, could seize power. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under Scar. The central conflict would remain intact, with more possibilities for both father and son.

A surviving Mufasa would have to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. The confident ruler who could face down hyenas would have to reckon with his vulnerability to manipulation. He might need help, and his injuries might prevent him from reclaiming his position through physical dominance. The film would then ask an older authority figure to change, something its original structure largely avoids.

Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He becomes an ideal who can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own decisions. A living Mufasa could retain his wisdom while having to question his judgment about his brother.

## Give Simba a reason to act

The strongest objection is that a surviving Mufasa would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

Leadership should demand more of Simba than his availability as the only heir. Imagine him learning that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. Returning would require him to act with the knowledge that Mufasa could no longer handle everything for him.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps his return would expose him to immediate capture. Either way, Simba would have to build alliances, persuade a frightened pride, and take risks on behalf of others. Waiting for his father to restore order would leave the kingdom in Scar's hands.

Simba would face the adult responsibility of helping the person who once protected him. He would also have a reason to develop a leadership style distinct from Mufasa's. Alongside the question of whether he could live up to his father, he would have to work out what he could contribute that his father could not.

## Other experiences deserve room

Mufasa's death has meant a great deal to audiences. For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. I value that comfort and still think the film's creative choice deserves criticism.

The defense that "children need stories about death" is true in general, but it does not establish that this particular story needed Mufasa to die. Children also need stories about disability, recovery, reconciliation, and the changing relationship between parents and children. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all four.

Survival could carry lasting consequences. Mufasa might lose his health, his authority, or his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family, and Scar's rule could still devastate their home. A happy outcome could require them to live with damage they could not undo.

## The harder relationship to write

Mufasa's death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound and helps make the movie memorable. Arguing against it means questioning one of the film's greatest artistic successes. Even so, I think letting him live would have been the more challenging choice.

Both father and son would have to give up familiar roles. Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba could no longer remain a protected child. They would have to discover a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

The story would retain its betrayal, danger, moral urgency, and decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. It could also show how much growing up remains possible when a parent survives: Simba taking responsibility for the kingdom while his father learns to accept his help.

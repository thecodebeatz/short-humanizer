# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa’s death in *The Lion King* works almost unbearably well. The stampede, Scar’s betrayal, Simba nudging his father’s motionless body: few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. I still think the film should have let him live.

The scene’s effectiveness can make that choice seem inevitable. Yet keeping Mufasa alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, with plenty of suspense and consequences for Scar.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. His death gives Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. But that argument treats one route to Simba’s maturity as the only route worth taking.

## A father with more to teach and learn

Mufasa is affectionate, occasionally exasperated, and playful with his son. He also takes the obligations of leadership seriously. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom gives Simba an understanding of kingship that extends beyond royal privilege. He wants his son to understand whom he will serve and what he will owe them.

The film removes him before those lessons can develop into an ongoing conversation. It follows a familiar convention in children’s stories: a parent’s death clears the stage, gives the young protagonist independence, and turns a complicated family relationship into a motivating memory. That is efficient storytelling, though it limits what the relationship can become.

Growing up with a parent brings its own difficulties. A child discovers that a beloved father can be wrong, finds reasons to disagree with him, and takes responsibility while he is still there to judge the result. Those experiences are recognizable to children learning how to become themselves, including children whose lives have not been shaped by bereavement.

Mufasa’s survival would have allowed the film to follow that process. Simba could have tested his father’s lessons, misunderstood them, or found circumstances in which they needed revising. Mufasa would have had to respond to the son Simba was becoming.

## Scar’s betrayal could still change everything

The attempted murder could remain almost exactly as it is. There could still be a stampede, outstretched claws, betrayal, and a fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar, either believing his brother dead or determined to finish the job, could seize power. Simba could still flee because of Scar’s lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under his uncle’s rule.

Survival would give Mufasa problems that physical strength could not solve. He would have to reckon with his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. A ruler confident enough to face down hyenas might struggle to accept how thoroughly his brother had deceived him. Injured and dependent on help, he might have no way to reclaim his position through force.

The film would then ask an older authority figure to change. Mufasa’s death preserves him at the height of his moral stature; he can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. Alive, he could remain wise while confronting mistakes and limitations that his son would also have to understand.

## Give Simba a reason to act

The strongest objection is that Mufasa’s survival would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, what does the prince need to do?

Much would depend on what returning cost Mufasa and what he could accomplish once he got there. Simba might learn that his father was alive but unable to save the kingdom. He would then have to go home knowing that Mufasa could no longer handle everything for him.

Perhaps Mufasa’s injuries would prevent him from fighting, or returning would expose him to immediate capture. Simba would need allies and the trust of a frightened pride. Taking risks on their behalf would give him responsibilities he could neither inherit automatically nor leave to his father.

There is a difficult, familiar kind of adulthood in recognizing that the person who once protected you now needs your help. It could also give Simba room to develop a leadership style of his own. Alongside the question of whether he could live up to Mufasa would come another: what could he do for the kingdom that his father could not?

## What survival would cost

Mufasa’s death has meant a great deal to audiences. For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. Those are substantial reasons to value the film as it exists, though they do not settle whether killing Mufasa was its best creative choice.

Children need stories about death. They also need stories about disability and recovery, about reconciliation, and about relationships with parents that change as both people grow older. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have given those experiences space in the film.

His survival could carry permanent losses. He might lose his health or his authority, as well as his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still spend years separated from his family, and Scar’s rule could still devastate their home. Reuniting would leave them with the work of living through what had happened. They could be happy to find one another without recovering the lives they had before.

## A different relationship

Arguing against Mufasa’s death means questioning one of *The Lion King*’s greatest artistic successes. It gives the movie its defining wound and helps make it unforgettable. I think the alternative was worth pursuing anyway.

Letting Mufasa live would have forced father and son out of roles they understood. Mufasa could no longer be the invincible protector, and Simba could no longer depend on being protected. As they confronted the damage Scar had done, they would have to learn how to rely on each other differently.

The struggle for the Pride Lands would still have betrayal, danger, and moral urgency. Simba would still have to grow up. With Mufasa alive, the film could have followed him through the difficult business of becoming a leader while his father watched, needed his help, and learned to make room for him.

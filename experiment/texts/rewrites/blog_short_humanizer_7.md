# Why does everything look less colorful now?

Walk through a parking lot or scroll through real-estate listings, and much of everyday life seems to have faded toward grayscale. Compare a few generations of corporate logos, and you may notice the same thing.

Cars are white, black, silver, and gray. Cafés combine pale wood with white walls and black fixtures. Renovated homes have greige floors beneath off-white cabinets. Storefronts and apps use simplified lettering, with shadows, textures, and sometimes color removed.

There is some truth to the impression. In certain categories, most clearly new cars, neutral colors really do dominate. Several forces help explain it: financial caution, global marketing, changing technology, and a particular idea of what "good taste" looks like. Institutions and consumers often seem to feel they need a reason to choose color.

## Was the past actually more colorful?

Sometimes, yes. The automotive palettes of the 1950s and the kitchens of the 1970s look strikingly different from today's. Turquoise cars, mustard countertops, avocado appliances, and patterned wallpaper were ordinary consumer choices. You didn't have to be considered eccentric to buy them.

Still, "the past" covers many different palettes. Plenty of historical streets and homes were drab, dusty, or industrial. We also preserve and circulate the most visually memorable examples: the pink diner gets more attention than the unremarkable beige restaurant beside it.

Today's color often appears in clothing, packaging, screens, lighting, and temporary installations. Many of the durable, expensive things that form our visual backdrop have become more neutral, even as color remains common elsewhere.

## Cars are expensive to personalize

A car can express its owner's taste. It is also an expensive purchase that many people expect to sell.

That gives buyers a strong incentive to choose a color that few people will object to. You might love orange. You are less certain that the next buyer will.

Dealers make a similar calculation. A gray SUV is unlikely to be someone's dream specification, but many shoppers will accept it. A purple one may delight a smaller audience and take longer to sell. Expensive inventory makes that wait a concern.

Fleet purchasing, leasing, and standardized production can reinforce this conservatism. Manufacturers offer what dealers expect to sell. Dealers stock what buyers seem to want, and buyers choose from what is available.

Unusual colors can sometimes benefit from scarcity and enthusiast demand, so neutrals do not always have better resale value. Buyers and sellers often *perceive* them as safer, though, and make their decisions accordingly.

White, black, and metallic gray also fit contemporary ideas about technology and luxury. A silver car can suggest precision; a black one, prestige. These colors can express a carefully cultivated identity.

## Homes are designed with the next owner in mind

Housing involves even more money, which gives owners another reason to worry about resale.

A home increasingly has to serve as both a place to live and a marketable asset. Renovation advice therefore favors choices that photograph cleanly and appeal to hypothetical future buyers: neutral walls, restrained finishes, uncomplicated cabinetry.

Distinctive taste becomes a potential liability. A cobalt kitchen is someone's favorite kitchen and someone else's renovation budget.

Landlords, developers, and hospitality businesses have additional reasons to standardize. A limited palette makes it easier to repeat a design across properties and replace components without rethinking the whole room. Bold color is not inherently more expensive, though variation and customization can create costs.

These incentives favor interiors that few people will dislike, even if fewer people will love them.

## Minimalism became a mark of good taste

Neutrality also acquired cultural prestige.

Influential strands of modern design associated simplicity with honesty, order, and progress. Later, technology companies, luxury retailers, architecture magazines, and lifestyle brands helped make clean surfaces and restrained palettes familiar signs of sophistication.

Within that design vocabulary, people can read bright color as childish, cheap, chaotic, or dated. White, charcoal, beige, and natural wood are more readily described as "timeless."

Calling beige timeless is a cultural judgment. The all-gray renovation will eventually identify its era just as clearly as harvest-gold appliances identify theirs. For now, neutral design is so common that it often barely registers as a style.

## Brands have to fit everywhere

A logo must work across an enormous range of sizes and contexts.

A detailed emblem that looked impressive on a shop sign may become unreadable as a tiny app icon. Gradients, shadows, fine outlines, and elaborate lettering can complicate reproduction. Flat shapes and simple typography are easier to use across screens, packaging, signage, and merchandise.

Global businesses also want recognizable consistency across markets. Simplification reduces the number of elements they have to manage.

Those pressures help explain why many brands have adopted similar designs. They do not make every redesign inevitable or successful: a brand can lose distinctiveness as it tries to make its logo more flexible.

Simplification and desaturation are also separate choices. Some highly streamlined identities use vivid color. Logo design and interior design share some pressures, but their changes have not been identical.

## The internet spread one room around the world

A successful café interior once influenced neighboring businesses. Now it can influence cafés on several continents.

Design platforms and social media make styles easy to discover, imitate, and purchase. Pale wood, greenery, concrete, white walls, and black metal became a familiar combination. Businesses could buy the materials readily and trust that customers would recognize the look as contemporary.

Photography strengthens the effect. Uncluttered backgrounds help products, food, and people stand out. A neutral room gives the camera less to compete with when its occupants or their purchases become the content.

The internet is full of color, while many of the physical spaces designed to appear on it use deliberately restrained palettes.

## Neutral choices feel less risky

A household, a restaurant owner, or a multinational brand can all face rejection after making a strong aesthetic choice. Costly purchases, broad audiences, and fast-moving trends make neutrality seem like protection against that risk.

Colorful cushions can come later. Owners can change the lighting or put a bright image on a screen while keeping the expensive underlying object safely noncommittal.

Thousands of people and businesses can make individually sensible decisions like these and collectively produce a remarkably monotonous environment, without anyone coordinating the outcome.

## Choosing color

Saturated interiors, expressive independent shops, colorful appliances, playful branding, and renewed interest in decorative styles offer visible alternatives. Their uneven success shows that tastes can change, though people do not change them at the same pace.

Neutral design has its own pleasures. A quiet room can be beautiful, and a white building can make extraordinary use of light. Visual calm has value. What troubles me is the narrowing of choices until one aesthetic starts to feel compulsory.

Making the world more colorful would require people to accept more specific tastes, including choices they do not personally like. Occasionally, it would mean giving someone's enjoyment more weight than the possibility of another person's objection, or choosing the home we want to inhabit with less regard for the asset we may someday sell.

# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have survived *The Lion King*. His death scene works almost unbearably well: the stampede, Scar’s betrayal, Simba nudging his father’s motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. Even so, I think keeping him alive could have made for a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, with plenty of suspense and consequences for Scar.

The usual defense of his death is familiar. Removing the father creates the son’s journey, giving Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. Without that loss, the argument goes, there is no movie. But a surviving Mufasa could have given Simba a different, equally demanding path to adulthood.

## A father with more to teach and learn

Mufasa is affectionate, sometimes exasperated, and playful with his son. He also takes the obligations of leadership seriously. When he teaches Simba about the kingdom’s balance, he is trying to explain why a king must serve others and cannot simply do whatever he wants.

The film kills him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation. His death follows a familiar pattern in children’s stories: removing a parent gives the young protagonist independence and turns a complicated relationship into a motivating memory. It is an efficient way to move a story along, though it limits what that relationship can become.

Growing up with a parent brings its own difficulties. A child may discover that a beloved father is fallible, disagree with him, or struggle to take responsibility while he is still watching. Those experiences can be messier than inheriting a dead father’s wisdom. They are also familiar to children trying to become themselves within a loving family.

Simba could have faced those difficulties alongside Mufasa. Their relationship had enough warmth, authority, and friction to sustain them.

## What surviving Scar’s betrayal could cost

The stampede could remain, along with Scar’s outstretched claws, his betrayal, and Mufasa’s fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar could seize power, either believing his brother dead or intending to finish the job. Simba could still flee because of Scar’s lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under his uncle’s rule.

Survival would give Mufasa problems he could not solve through strength. He would have to face his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. A ruler capable of facing down hyenas could still be vulnerable to manipulation, and his injuries might leave him dependent on others. Reclaiming his position through physical dominance might no longer be possible.

The film would then have to follow an older authority figure through change. Mufasa’s death preserves him at the height of his moral stature. As a memory, he can guide Simba without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. Alive, he could remain wise while confronting mistakes and limits that his son would see.

## Why Simba would still have to act

The strongest objection is practical: if Mufasa survives, why does Simba need to become king?

A living father need not be a father who can save the kingdom. Imagine Simba discovering that Mufasa is alive but unable to fight, or that returning would put him at immediate risk of capture. Simba would have to go home knowing he could no longer count on his father to handle everything.

He might need to build alliances and persuade a frightened pride to resist Scar. Taking those risks for others would test his readiness to lead, especially if the father who once protected him now needed his help.

It could also give Simba room to develop a leadership style of his own. Much of his struggle in the original film concerns whether he can live up to Mufasa. With his father alive and in need, Simba would have to discover what he could contribute that Mufasa could not. His place in the succession would offer little help with that.

## Loss without death

For some children, *The Lion King* is an early encounter with death. For grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death can offer genuine comfort. Questioning the decision to kill Mufasa does not diminish what those viewers have found in the film.

Still, that comfort leaves room to question the choice. Children need stories about death, and they also need stories about disability, recovery, reconciliation, and changing relationships with their parents. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all of these.

He could lose his health or authority, along with his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still spend years separated from his family. Scar’s rule could devastate their home. Even after his defeat, father and son would have losses to live with and damage to repair. Mufasa’s survival would hardly return them to the life they had before the stampede.

## A changed relationship

Mufasa’s death helps make *The Lion King* memorable. Arguing against it means questioning one of the film’s greatest artistic successes. I think the alternative was worth pursuing because it would have asked more of the relationship the film had already established.

An injured Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba could no longer remain a protected child. They would have to learn how to treat each other while confronting what Scar had done. Betrayal, danger, and a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands would still drive the story, but the victory would leave them with a relationship neither had expected.

I would have liked to see Simba take responsibility while his father was there to witness it, and Mufasa learn to accept help from the son he had raised to lead.

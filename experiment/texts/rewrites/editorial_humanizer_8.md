# Mufasa Should Have Lived

Mufasa should have survived *The Lion King*. His death works almost unbearably well: the stampede, Scar’s betrayal, Simba nudging his father’s motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. I still think keeping him alive could have made a better film.

The scene’s effectiveness makes that possibility easy to dismiss. Mufasa’s death gives Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. The standard defense is that removing the father makes the son’s journey possible. Without that loss, what would drive the movie?

A surviving Mufasa could have given Simba just as much reason to act, while allowing the film to explore a relationship it ends too soon.

## Growing up with a father

Mufasa is affectionate, playful with his son, and occasionally exasperated. He takes leadership seriously. When he teaches Simba about the balance of the kingdom, he is trying to help his son understand the obligations that come with power. Simba wants the freedom to do whatever he likes; Mufasa wants him to understand whom a king serves.

The film kills Mufasa before that disagreement can develop into a sustained conversation.

Children’s stories often remove parents to give young protagonists room to act. Death clears the stage and turns a complicated relationship into a motivating memory. It is an efficient choice, though it closes off other ways of telling a story about growing up.

A child can discover that a beloved parent is fallible. They can disagree, make their own decisions, and accept responsibility while that parent is still there to watch. Those experiences are messier than inheriting a dead father’s wisdom. They may also feel familiar to children who are learning how to become themselves within a loving family.

I would have liked to see Simba struggle with Mufasa’s expectations in person.

## What survival would cost Mufasa

Scar could still attempt to murder his brother. Keep the stampede, the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Let Mufasa survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar could seize power, either believing Mufasa dead or intending to finish the job. Simba could still flee because of Scar’s lie, and the kingdom could still suffer under Scar’s rule. Survival would leave the central conflict in place while giving Mufasa a part in its aftermath.

He would have to reckon with his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. A ruler who could face down hyenas might struggle to accept how thoroughly his brother had deceived him. Injured and dependent on help, he might also have to give up the expectation that he could reclaim his position through physical strength.

Mufasa would have to change. That is something the original film rarely asks of him. Death preserves him at the height of his moral authority; he can return to guide Simba without having to reconsider his own judgments. Keeping him alive would allow him to remain wise while making mistakes he had to live with.

## Give Simba a reason to lead

The strongest objection is that Mufasa’s survival would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

That objection assumes Mufasa could simply take charge again. Imagine Simba discovering that his father is alive but unable to save the kingdom. His injuries might prevent him from fighting, or returning might expose him to immediate capture. Simba would have to act knowing that his father could no longer handle everything for him.

He could need to build alliances and persuade a frightened pride to resist Scar. Taking those risks for others would test his readiness to lead more fully than his position in the succession could.

It would also change his relationship with Mufasa. The father who once protected him would now need his help. Simba would have to work out what he could offer that Mufasa could not, giving him a reason to develop his own way of leading. Living up to his father would become only part of the task.

## Other losses worth telling stories about

For some children, *The Lion King* is an early encounter with death. For grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives loss can offer genuine comfort. Questioning the decision to kill Mufasa does not erase what the film has given those audiences.

Children need stories about death. They also need stories about disability and recovery, about reconciliation, and about relationships that change as children and parents grow older. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for those experiences.

His survival could carry lasting consequences. He might lose his health or his authority, along with his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family. Scar’s rule could still devastate their home. Bringing them back together would leave plenty of damage for them to face.

## A different ending for father and son

Arguing against Mufasa’s death means questioning one of *The Lion King*’s greatest artistic successes. The loss helps make the film memorable, and an alternative would have to earn its place.

I think letting him live could have done that. Mufasa would have to accept that he could no longer protect Simba as he once had. Simba would have to take responsibility with his father still present, watching and perhaps disagreeing. Together, they would have to find a new relationship while confronting what Scar had done.

There would still be betrayal, danger, and a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. There would also be room to follow the affectionate, imperfect relationship the film begins between father and son. Mufasa should have lived long enough for us to see what they might become together.

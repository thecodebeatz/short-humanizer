# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have lived in *The Lion King*. His death scene succeeds almost unbearably well. The stampede, Scar's betrayal, Simba nudging his father's motionless body: few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force.

That success has largely kept one creative choice from scrutiny: was killing him the best choice? I would argue no. Keeping Mufasa alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up while preserving the suspense and the severity of Scar's betrayal. Children's movies can include tragedy, and fictional animals need no immunity from it. The question is what this particular death allows the story to do, and what it prevents.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. Removing the father creates the son's journey, giving Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. According to this argument, there is no movie without that loss. I think his survival could have made a better one.

## A father who stays

Mufasa combines power with affection. He plays with his son, occasionally loses patience with him, and takes the obligations of leadership seriously. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom gives Simba an ethical framework beyond royal privilege. He wants his son to understand that authority means serving others rather than simply getting his way.

The film removes him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation.

Children's stories often make this bargain: a parent becomes most useful when absent. Death clears the stage and grants the young protagonist independence. Complicated family relationships become motivating memories. That efficiency comes at the cost of exploring the relationship itself.

Growing up can mean discovering that a beloved parent is fallible, disagreeing with that parent, and accepting responsibility while that parent is still watching. Those experiences are less tidy than inheriting a dead father's wisdom. Children whose central challenge is learning how to become themselves, rather than bereavement, may recognize them.

Simba could have matured alongside Mufasa, with opportunities to question his father's judgment and put his lessons into practice.

## Scar's betrayal could leave Mufasa alive

The attempted murder could remain, including the stampede, the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar, believing his brother dead or determined to finish the job, could seize power. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under Scar's rule.

A surviving Mufasa would have to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. The confident ruler who could face down hyenas would discover his vulnerability to manipulation. He might need help, and his injuries might leave him unable to reclaim his position through physical dominance. An older authority figure would have to change, something the original structure largely avoids.

Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. As an ideal, he can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. A living Mufasa could retain his wisdom while reckoning with what he had misunderstood about his brother.

## Give Simba responsibility

The strongest objection is that Mufasa's survival would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

Simba could learn that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. Leadership would then demand more of him than being the only available heir. Returning would mean acting without the comforting assumption that Mufasa could handle everything.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps returning would expose him to immediate capture. Either possibility would require Simba to build alliances, persuade a frightened pride, and take risks on behalf of others while his father remained unable to restore order.

Having to help the person who once protected him would test Simba's adulthood. It would also give him room to develop a leadership style distinct from Mufasa's. Alongside the question of whether he could live up to his father, Simba would have to work out what he could contribute that his father could not.

## Other experiences children need stories about

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. Those responses matter when considering the film's choices, though they leave room to question them.

The defense that "children need stories about death" is true in general, but it does not settle whether Mufasa needed to die. Children also need stories about disability, recovery, reconciliation, and the changing relationship between parents and children. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all four.

His survival could carry lasting consequences. He could lose his health, his authority, or his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family, and Scar's rule could still devastate their home. Even a happy outcome would leave them living with those losses.

## Let father and son change

Mufasa's death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound and helps make the movie memorable. Arguing against it means questioning one of the film's greatest artistic successes. I still think letting him live would have been the more challenging choice.

Both father and son would have to give up familiar roles. Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba could no longer remain a protected child. They would have to discover a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

The story would retain its betrayal and danger, its moral urgency, and a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. With Mufasa alive, Simba could become a leader while learning how to help a father who needed him.

# Mufasa Should Have Lived

Mufasa should have survived *The Lion King*. His death scene works almost unbearably well: the stampede, Scar’s betrayal, Simba nudging his father’s motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. I still think keeping him alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, with just as much suspense and consequences for Scar.

The usual defense of his death is familiar. Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. His absence gives Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. Without that loss, the argument goes, there is no movie.

But an injured, surviving Mufasa could have given Simba a different reason to grow up, and given both characters more to do.

## A father still learning

Mufasa’s appeal comes partly from how he behaves with his son. He is affectionate and playful, occasionally exasperated, and serious about what leadership requires. When he teaches Simba about the balance of the kingdom, he asks him to look beyond royal privilege. Simba needs to understand that being king means serving others, even when he would prefer to get his way.

The film removes Mufasa before those lessons can become a sustained conversation. His death follows a familiar pattern in children’s stories: an absent parent gives the young protagonist independence, while a complicated family relationship becomes a motivating memory. It is an efficient way to get a story moving, though it limits what that relationship can become.

Growing up with a parent brings its own difficulties. A child discovers that someone they love is fallible and begins to disagree with them. Eventually, they have to accept responsibility while that parent is still watching. Those experiences can be messy, and they are recognizable to children learning how to become themselves.

Simba could have gone through that process alongside Mufasa. We could have watched him struggle with his father’s advice, decide where it fell short, and work out which parts he wanted to carry into adulthood.

## What surviving Scar’s betrayal could change

Scar could still attempt to murder Mufasa. The stampede could remain, along with the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Believing his brother dead, or determined to finish the job, Scar could seize power. Simba could still flee because of Scar’s lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under his uncle’s rule.

Mufasa would then have to reckon with a failure of his own: he had not recognized the danger within his family. A ruler capable of facing down hyenas had been vulnerable to his brother’s manipulation. Recovering from that betrayal might require him to depend on others, especially if his injuries left him unable to reclaim his position by force.

That would require the older authority figure to change, something the original film largely avoids. Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. A surviving Mufasa could remain wise while having mistakes to answer for and limits to accept.

## Give Simba a reason to act

The strongest objection is that Mufasa’s survival would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

That objection assumes Mufasa could simply resume his old role. Imagine Simba learning that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. He would have to return knowing that Mufasa could no longer handle everything for him.

Perhaps Mufasa’s injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps returning would put him at immediate risk of capture. Simba would need to build alliances and persuade a frightened pride to resist Scar. Taking those risks on behalf of others would force him to accept responsibilities he could once leave to his father.

Many people reach adulthood through some version of that reversal, when the person who protected them begins to need their help. It could also give Simba room to develop a leadership style of his own. Alongside the question of whether he can live up to Mufasa, the film could ask what he can contribute that his father cannot.

## Other losses worth exploring

Mufasa’s death has meant a great deal to audiences. For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. Some grieving viewers find genuine comfort in its insistence that love survives death. Those responses deserve acknowledgment, and the creative choice that made them possible can still be questioned.

Children need stories about death. They also need stories about disability and recovery, reconciliation, and relationships with parents that change over time. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for those experiences.

His survival could come with lasting losses. He might lose his health or his authority, and he would have to live with the knowledge that he had misjudged his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family. Scar’s rule could still devastate their home. Reuniting would leave them with damage to address and a life very different from the one they had before.

## A different ending for father and son

Mufasa’s death helps make *The Lion King* memorable. Arguing against it means questioning one of the film’s greatest artistic successes. Even so, I think letting him live would have been the more challenging choice.

Mufasa would have to give up being an invincible protector, while Simba would have to stop relying on that protection. As they confronted the damage Scar had done, they would also have to discover how to be father and son under circumstances neither had chosen.

The betrayal and danger would remain, as would the moral urgency of a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. Keeping Mufasa alive would give that struggle another dimension: Simba would have to take responsibility in front of the father who had taught him what it meant.

I would have liked to see Mufasa live long enough to watch his son become someone he could depend on.

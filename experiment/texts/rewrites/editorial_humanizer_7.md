# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have survived the stampede in *The Lion King*. His death is almost unbearably effective: Scar’s betrayal, the fall, Simba nudging his father’s motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. I can admire the scene and still wonder whether killing him was the best choice for the story.

Keeping Mufasa alive could have given the film more room to explore responsibility, power, and growing up. The suspense could remain, and Scar would still have to answer for what he had done.

The familiar defense of Mufasa’s death is that it makes Simba’s journey possible. Simba needs grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. His father’s removal gives him all of those things. But a surviving Mufasa could have placed demands on Simba that the original film never gets to explore.

## A father with more to teach, and something to learn

Mufasa’s appeal comes partly from how he behaves with his son. He is affectionate and playful, occasionally exasperated, and serious about the obligations Simba will inherit. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom gives kingship an ethical purpose beyond royal privilege. He wants Simba to understand that a ruler owes something to everyone who lives under his authority.

The film kills him before those lessons can develop into an ongoing conversation.

Children’s stories often make this bargain. An absent parent gives a young protagonist independence and turns a complicated relationship into a motivating memory. It is an efficient way to get a story moving, though it limits what that relationship can become.

Growing up with a parent presents its own difficulties. A child discovers that someone they admire can be wrong. Arguments become harder to settle by simply deferring to the adult. Eventually, the child has to accept responsibility while the parent is still there to watch, worry, or disagree. Many children will recognize those experiences long before they encounter bereavement.

Simba could have matured through that changing relationship with Mufasa. He could have questioned his father’s judgment while still loving him, then worked out which lessons to carry forward.

## Scar’s betrayal could still drive the story

The attempted murder could happen much as it does in the film. Keep the stampede, the outstretched claws, and the betrayal. Mufasa falls, but survives with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar could seize power believing his brother dead, or knowing he still had to finish the job. Simba could flee under the weight of Scar’s lie. The kingdom would suffer under the same ruler. Survival would leave the central conflict intact while giving Mufasa a part in its consequences.

He would have to reckon with his failure to recognize the danger within his family. A ruler who could face down hyenas would have been undone by his brother’s manipulation. Recovering might require him to depend on others, and his injuries might leave him unable to reclaim the throne by force.

An older authority figure would then have to change alongside the young hero.

Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He becomes an ideal who can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. Alive, he could retain his wisdom while admitting that some of his confidence had been misplaced. I would have liked to see what he did with that knowledge.

## Give Simba a reason to act

The strongest objection to this version is practical: if Mufasa can return, what does Simba need to do? A surviving king could leave the prince waiting on the sidelines.

That problem depends on how Mufasa survives. Imagine Simba discovering that his father is alive and unable to save the kingdom. He would have to return knowing that Mufasa could no longer handle everything for him.

Perhaps the injuries prevent Mufasa from fighting. Perhaps returning would expose him to immediate capture. Simba would need to find allies and persuade a frightened pride to act, taking risks for others without waiting for his father to restore order.

Having to help the person who once protected him would give Simba a difficult, recognizable experience of adulthood. It would also make room for him to develop his own way of leading. Mufasa’s limitations could force Simba to contribute something his father could not, giving him a reason to lead beyond his position in the succession.

## What survival would cost

Mufasa’s death has meant a great deal to audiences. For some children, *The Lion King* is an early encounter with loss. Its insistence that love survives death offers real comfort to some grieving viewers. Questioning the choice to kill him does not diminish those experiences.

Still, that value leaves room to consider what another version might offer. Children need stories about death. They also need stories about living with disability and recovering from injury, about reconciliation and relationships that change as children grow older. An injured Mufasa could have brought those experiences into the film.

His survival would carry lasting consequences. He might lose his health or his authority. He would certainly have reason to doubt how well he had understood his brother. Simba could still spend years separated from his family, and Scar’s rule could still devastate their home. Even after Scar’s defeat, father and son would have to live with damage they could not undo.

There could be happiness in their reunion without a return to the lives they had before.

## Let father and son face each other

Mufasa’s death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound. It helps make the film memorable, and I understand why an argument against it can sound like an argument against one of the movie’s greatest successes. The scene works. I am less convinced that the story needed it.

Letting Mufasa live would have required the film to change both father and son. Mufasa could no longer be the invincible protector Simba remembered. Simba would have to give up the security of being the child his father could always rescue. Together, they would need to work out a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

That version would still have betrayal and danger, with the fate of the Pride Lands demanding a decisive struggle. It would also challenge the familiar assumption that a loving father has to become a memory before his child can become a leader.

I wish Mufasa had lived long enough to see Simba take responsibility, and to discover how much growing up they both still had to do.

# How to start a vegetable garden in a small backyard

A small backyard can produce plenty of fresh vegetables without becoming a full-time job. Start with a space you can manage and crops you enjoy eating, then settle into a routine for watering and harvesting. You don't need expensive equipment or perfect soil to begin.

## Find the sun

Before buying plants, spend a day watching where sunlight falls in your backyard. Check in the morning, at midday, and in the afternoon. Trees and buildings cast different shadows as the seasons change.

Most fruiting vegetables, such as tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, and beans, grow best with at least six to eight hours of direct sun daily. Leafy vegetables, including lettuce, spinach, and kale, can manage with four to six hours, especially in warm climates.

Choose a spot close to a water source and easy to reach from your home. You'll find it easier to notice problems and keep up with care if you pass the garden every day. Avoid low areas where water collects after rain.

## Start small

For your first season, try one bed measuring about 4 by 8 feet, two smaller beds, or several large containers. That gives you room to learn without making watering and weeding overwhelming.

Keep beds no wider than 4 feet if you can reach them from both sides, or about 2 feet if you have access from only one side. You should be able to reach the center without stepping on the soil.

Leave enough room along paths to walk, kneel, and carry a watering can comfortably. In a cramped yard, a few well-positioned containers may work better than squeezing in another bed.

## Choose your setup

In-ground beds are usually the least expensive choice if your soil drains well and is safe for growing food. Raised beds help where soil is compacted, drainage is poor, or you want a defined growing space, though they cost more to build and fill.

Containers work well on patios and in awkward corners. They're flexible, but usually need more frequent watering.

Every container needs drainage holes. Use roughly 5-gallon containers for peppers. Tomatoes need larger pots, often 10 gallons or more, depending on the variety. Leafy greens and radishes can grow in shallower containers.

If you're using salvaged materials, avoid anything that may contain lead paint or chemical residues.

## Prepare the soil

Healthy soil supports strong roots and holds moisture without becoming waterlogged.

For an in-ground garden, remove grass and weeds, then loosen the soil when it is moist but not wet. Mix a layer of finished compost into the upper soil. Working soggy soil can damage its structure.

A soil test from a local laboratory or extension service can identify nutrient needs and pH problems. If your property has a history of industrial use, older painted structures, or possible contamination, ask about testing for contaminants before growing food in the ground.

Fill raised beds with a suitable topsoil-and-compost blend. Use potting mix in containers; backyard soil can become dense and drain poorly.

## Pick reliable crops

Choose vegetables you already like eating, then check that they suit your available sunlight, space, and growing season.

Some good crops for beginners are:

- Leaf lettuce grows quickly, and you can harvest a few leaves at a time.
- Radishes grow fast, particularly in cool weather.
- Bush beans are productive without needing a tall support.
- Swiss chard provides repeated harvests over a long period.
- Cherry tomatoes are often productive, though they need support and adequate space.
- Cucumbers suited to a trellis let you grow upward.

Look for varieties labeled "compact," "patio," or "bush." Skip sprawling crops such as pumpkins unless you have room for vines, and limit your first season to a few crop types.

## Plant at the right time

Planting dates depend on your local climate. Find a local planting calendar through an extension service, reputable nursery, or regional gardening organization.

Cool-season crops, including peas, lettuce, spinach, and radishes, generally grow best in cooler weather. Tomatoes, beans, peppers, and cucumbers need warmer soil and may be damaged by frost.

Seeds are economical and work well for beans, peas, radishes, and many greens. Buying young plants is often easier for tomatoes and peppers.

Follow seed packets and plant labels for planting depth and spacing. Crowding plants can reduce airflow, increase disease, and limit harvests.

## Use vertical space

A trellis can make a narrow strip of soil productive. Pole beans, climbing peas, and suitable cucumber varieties can climb, leaving more ground space free.

Install supports when you plant to avoid damaging roots later. Position tall crops where they won't shade shorter plants that need sun. In much of the Northern Hemisphere, the north side of a bed is a useful starting point.

Secure trellises firmly against wind. Tie plants loosely with soft ties, and check them as stems thicken. Plants grown vertically still need adequate root room and water.

## Water and mulch

Check moisture regularly to decide when to water. Push a finger into the soil: if it feels dry an inch or two below the surface, watering may be needed. Seedbeds and young seedlings need closer attention.

Water slowly at soil level so moisture reaches the root zone. Morning watering is convenient. Drip irrigation or a soaker hose can reduce the work, while containers may need daily moisture checks during hot weather.

Once plants are established, add a thin layer of clean straw, shredded leaves, or another suitable mulch, keeping it away from stems. Mulch reduces evaporation and weeds while moderating soil temperature.

## Feed and protect

Compost improves soil, but vegetables may also need fertilizer. Use soil-test recommendations where available, or follow a vegetable fertilizer's label. Excess fertilizer can harm plants or encourage leaves at the expense of fruit.

Walk through the garden several times a week. Look under leaves for insects and watch for wilting. Remove weeds while they're small.

Identify a pest before treating it. Hand-picking, insect netting, and appropriate barriers often solve small problems. If a pesticide is necessary, choose one labeled for the crop and pest. Follow all directions, including harvest waiting periods.

## Harvest and learn

Harvest regularly to enjoy vegetables at their best and encourage continued production. Pick beans and cucumbers while tender, snip outer lettuce leaves, and check fast-growing crops frequently.

Keep brief notes on planting dates, varieties, problems, and harvests. After an early crop finishes, replant the space with something suited to the remaining season.

At the end of the year, consider expanding if the watering, weeding, and other care felt manageable and you enjoyed tending the garden.

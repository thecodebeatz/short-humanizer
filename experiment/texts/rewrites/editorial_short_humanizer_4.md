# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have lived in *The Lion King*. His death succeeds almost unbearably well: the stampede, Scar's betrayal, Simba nudging his father's motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. Children's movies can include tragedy, and fictional animals have no claim to immunity from it.

The scene's effectiveness has largely spared it scrutiny as a storytelling choice. Keeping Mufasa alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, with suspense intact and consequences just as severe for Scar.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. Removing the father creates the son's journey, giving him grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. According to that argument, there is no movie without the loss. I think there could have been a better one with Mufasa alive.

## A father with more to teach and learn

Mufasa combines power with affection. He plays with his son, occasionally loses patience with him, and takes the obligations of leadership seriously. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom suggests an ethical framework that extends beyond royal privilege. He wants Simba to understand that authority involves service, even when Simba would rather get his way.

The film removes him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation. Children's stories often make this bargain: a parent's absence gives the young protagonist independence and turns a complicated family relationship into a motivating memory. Death clears the stage efficiently, but it also limits what the story can explore.

Growing up can involve discovering a beloved parent's fallibility, disagreeing with that parent, and accepting responsibility while the parent is still watching. Those experiences are messier than inheriting a dead father's wisdom. They are recognizable to children whose central challenge is learning how to become themselves rather than coping with bereavement. Simba could have matured alongside Mufasa, with opportunities to question him and take responsibility in his presence.

## Scar's betrayal could still devastate the family

Scar could still attempt to murder Mufasa. The stampede, the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall could all remain. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Believing his brother dead, or determined to finish the job, Scar could seize power. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under Scar's rule.

Mufasa would then have to confront his failure to recognize danger within his own family. A ruler confident enough to face down hyenas would have to reckon with his vulnerability to manipulation. He might need help, especially if his injuries left him unable to reclaim his position through physical dominance. The film would have an older authority figure who had to change, something its original structure largely avoids.

Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his judgment. Survival would let him remain wise while requiring him to acknowledge what he had misunderstood about his brother.

## Simba could lead while his father lived

A surviving Mufasa raises a substantial objection: if the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up? Simba would need responsibilities beyond being the only available heir.

Imagine him learning that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. Returning would require him to act without the comforting assumption that Mufasa could handle everything. He would have to decide what to do himself, even with his father still there.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps returning would expose him to immediate capture. Either possibility would require Simba to build alliances, persuade a frightened pride, and take risks on behalf of others. Waiting for his father to restore order would leave the kingdom in Scar's hands.

Simba would also have to recognize that the parent who once protected him now needed his help. That would test his adulthood and give him room to develop a leadership style distinct from Mufasa's. Alongside the pressure to live up to his father, he would face the practical question of what he could contribute that his father could no longer provide.

## Grief and other childhood experiences

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. Mufasa's death has meant a great deal to those audiences, and that value deserves acknowledgment when questioning the choice to kill him.

The defense that "children need stories about death" is true in general, but it does not settle whether this particular story needed Mufasa to die. Children also need stories about disability, recovery, reconciliation, and the changing relationship between parents and children. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all four.

Survival could carry lasting consequences. Mufasa could lose his health, his authority, or his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family, and Scar's rule could still devastate their home. A happy outcome could leave father and son facing damage they could never fully repair.

## Letting Mufasa stay

Mufasa's death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound and helps make the movie memorable. Arguing that he should have survived means questioning one of the film's greatest artistic successes. I still think survival would have been the more challenging choice.

Both father and son would have to give up their familiar roles. With Mufasa unable to remain an invincible protector, Simba would have to take on responsibilities he had once left to his father. They would need to discover a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

That story could retain betrayal, danger, moral urgency, and a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. It could also challenge the assumption that a loving father must become a memory before his child can become a leader. Mufasa should have lived, with Simba learning to lead while his father learned to depend on him.

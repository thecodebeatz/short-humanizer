# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have lived in *The Lion King*. His death succeeds almost unbearably well: the stampede, Scar's betrayal, Simba nudging his father's motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force.

That success has largely shielded the decision to kill him from scrutiny. Was it the best choice for the story? I would argue no. Keeping Mufasa alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, with suspense intact and consequences just as severe for Scar. The case for his survival has nothing to do with requiring cheerful children's movies or shielding fictional animals from tragedy.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. His removal gives Simba a journey through grief and guilt toward reclaiming the throne. According to that argument, there is no movie without the loss. But Simba could have matured while his father was still alive.

## A father with room to change

Mufasa's appeal includes his power, his affection, and his occasional exasperation. He plays with his son and takes the obligations of leadership seriously. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom suggests an ethical framework larger than royal privilege. He wants Simba to understand that authority means serving others rather than simply getting his way.

The film kills him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation.

Children's storytelling often makes this bargain: a parent becomes most useful when absent. Death clears the stage and grants the young protagonist independence. Complicated family relationships become motivating memories. That efficiency comes at the expense of experiences a story could explore with the parent still present.

Growing up can involve discovering a beloved parent's fallibility, disagreeing with them, and accepting responsibility while they are still watching. These experiences are less tidy than inheriting a dead father's wisdom. Children learning how to become themselves may recognize them more readily than bereavement.

## The consequences of Scar's betrayal

Scar could still attempt to murder Mufasa. The stampede could remain, along with the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Believing his brother dead, or determined to finish the job, Scar could seize power. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under Scar's rule.

Survival would force Mufasa to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. The confident ruler who could face down hyenas would have to reckon with his vulnerability to manipulation. He might need help, and his injuries might leave him unable to reclaim his position through physical dominance. An older authority figure would have to change, something the original structure largely avoids.

Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. As an ideal, he can offer guidance without ever reconsidering his own judgments. A living Mufasa could retain his wisdom while struggling with mistakes he had to address.

## Simba's responsibility

The strongest objection to keeping Mufasa alive is that it would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

Leadership should demand more of Simba than being the only available heir. Imagine him learning that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. Returning would require action without the comforting assumption that Mufasa could handle everything.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps returning would expose him to immediate capture. Simba would have to build alliances and persuade a frightened pride to act. He would take risks on behalf of others, knowing his father could no longer restore order for him.

Helping the person who once protected him would test Simba's maturity. It would also give him room to develop a leadership style distinct from Mufasa's. Alongside the question of whether he could live up to his father, the film could ask what Simba could contribute that Mufasa could not.

## Grief and other experiences

Mufasa's death has meant a great deal to audiences. For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. Those are valuable experiences, though they leave the film's creative choices open to criticism.

The defense that "children need stories about death" is true in general. It does not establish why this particular story needed Mufasa to die. Children also need stories about disability, recovery, reconciliation, and changing relationships with their parents. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all four.

Survival would carry its own consequences. Mufasa could lose his health, his authority, or his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family, and Scar's rule could still devastate their home. Even a happy outcome would leave them living with those losses.

## A father and son after the betrayal

Mufasa's death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound and helps make the movie memorable. Arguing against it means questioning one of the film's greatest artistic successes. I still think letting him live would have been the more challenging choice.

Both father and son would have to give up familiar roles. Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba could no longer remain a protected child. The film could show how much growing up remains possible when a parent survives, as they worked out a new relationship amid the damage Scar had done.

Betrayal and danger would remain, along with the moral urgency of their return and a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. Simba would have to lead while his father was still there to see it.

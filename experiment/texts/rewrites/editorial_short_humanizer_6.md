# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have survived *The Lion King*. His death succeeds almost unbearably well: the stampede, Scar's betrayal, Simba nudging his father's motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. Children's movies can accommodate tragedy, and fictional animals have no special claim to immunity from it.

The scene's effectiveness has largely shielded the decision to kill Mufasa from scrutiny. Was it the best choice for the story? I would argue no. Keeping him alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, with just as much suspense and equally severe consequences for Scar.

The familiar defense is that Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. Removing the father gives the son a journey: grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. According to that argument, there is no movie without the loss. Yet Mufasa's survival offers enough dramatic possibilities to suggest there might have been a better one.

## A father with more to teach and learn

Mufasa's appeal extends beyond his power. He is affectionate, occasionally exasperated, playful with his son, and serious about the obligations of leadership. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom gives Simba an ethical framework larger than royal privilege. He wants his son to understand that authority carries a duty of service, rather than simply the freedom to get his way.

The film kills him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation.

Children's stories often make this bargain: a parent becomes most useful when absent. Death clears the stage and gives the young protagonist independence. It also turns complicated family relationships into motivating memories. That efficiency comes at a cost.

Growing up with a parent presents its own difficulties. A child may discover that a beloved parent is fallible, disagree with them, and accept responsibility while they are still watching. These experiences are less tidy than inheriting a dead father's wisdom. Children whose central challenge is learning how to become themselves, rather than bereavement, can recognize them.

Simba could have faced those difficulties alongside Mufasa.

## Scar's betrayal could leave Mufasa alive

The attempted murder could remain almost as it is. There could still be a stampede, outstretched claws, a betrayal, and a fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar could seize power, either believing his brother dead or intending to finish the job. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, and the kingdom could still suffer under Scar's rule.

Mufasa would then have to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. A ruler confident enough to face down hyenas would have to reckon with his vulnerability to manipulation. He might need help. His injuries might leave him unable to reclaim his position through physical dominance.

The film largely avoids requiring that kind of change from an older authority figure. Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He becomes an ideal who can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. A living Mufasa could retain his wisdom while struggling with what he had failed to see in his brother.

## Simba could earn his authority

The strongest objection to Mufasa's survival concerns Simba's role. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up? That objection assumes too little of leadership, treating the absence of another available heir as sufficient reason for Simba to take responsibility.

Imagine Simba learning that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. He would have to return knowing that Mufasa could no longer handle everything for him. Accepting his place in the succession would be only part of what the situation demanded.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps returning would expose him to immediate capture. In either case, Simba would need to build alliances, persuade a frightened pride, and take risks on behalf of others. Waiting for his father to restore order would leave Scar in power.

Simba would face a recognizable test of adulthood when the parent who once protected him needed his help. He would also have room to develop a leadership style of his own. Alongside the question of whether he could live up to Mufasa would be a more practical one: what could he contribute that his father could no longer provide?

## Other losses deserve stories too

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with death. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. Those experiences matter, and questioning the decision to kill Mufasa leaves their value intact.

The claim that "children need stories about death" is true in general. It does not settle whether this particular story needed Mufasa to die. Children also need stories about disability, recovery, reconciliation, and the changing relationship between parents and children. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all four.

Survival could carry lasting consequences. Mufasa might lose his health, his authority, or his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family. Scar's rule could still devastate their home. Even a happy outcome would leave them with damage they could not undo.

## The harder choice would be to let him stay

Mufasa's death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound and helps make the movie memorable. Arguing that he should have lived means questioning one of the film's greatest artistic successes. The scene is unforgettable; whether the story required it remains open to argument.

Letting Mufasa survive would have been the braver choice because it would force both father and son out of their familiar roles. Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba could no longer remain a protected child. They would have to work out a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

The film could show Simba becoming a leader with his loving father still alive, and explore how much growing up remains possible in that relationship. Betrayal, danger, and moral urgency would still drive the story toward a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands.

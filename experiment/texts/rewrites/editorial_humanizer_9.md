# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa’s death in *The Lion King* works almost unbearably well. The stampede, Scar’s betrayal, Simba nudging his father’s motionless body: few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. I still think the film should have kept him alive.

The scene’s effectiveness makes that choice difficult to question. Yet keeping Mufasa alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, with plenty of suspense and equally severe consequences for Scar.

The standard defense of his death is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. His absence gives Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. It sets the son’s journey in motion. But that journey could also begin with a father who survives and can no longer protect him.

## Mufasa had more to teach, and more to learn

Mufasa is affectionate, occasionally exasperated, and playful with his son. He also takes the obligations of leadership seriously. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom gives Simba an understanding of kingship that extends beyond royal privilege. Simba wants to get his way; Mufasa wants him to understand whom he serves.

The film ends that conversation before either of them has much chance to struggle with it.

Children’s stories often remove a parent to give the young protagonist independence. Death clears the stage and turns a complicated relationship into a motivating memory. That is efficient storytelling, though it leaves out much of what growing up with a parent involves.

A child can discover that a beloved parent is fallible. They can disagree, disappoint each other, and learn to accept responsibility while the parent is still watching. Those experiences are messier than inheriting a dead father’s wisdom. For children learning how to become themselves, they are also familiar.

I would have liked to see Simba mature alongside Mufasa, with both of them having to adjust to the person he became.

## Scar’s betrayal could still change everything

The stampede could remain, including the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar could seize power, either believing his brother dead or intending to finish the job. Simba could still flee under the weight of Scar’s lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under his rule.

Survival would give Mufasa a difficult reckoning of his own. He failed to recognize the danger within his family. A ruler confident enough to face down hyenas would have to confront how badly he had misunderstood his brother. His strength had offered little protection against that betrayal, and his injuries might leave him dependent on others, unable to reclaim his position by force.

The original film largely spares its older authority figure the need to change. Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He can return to offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. Alive, he could remain wise while having to acknowledge what he got wrong.

## Simba would still have to act

The strongest objection is that a surviving Mufasa would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

That objection depends on Mufasa being able to return and put everything right. Imagine Simba discovering that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. His injuries might prevent him from fighting, or returning might expose him to immediate capture. Simba would have to act knowing that his father could no longer handle the danger for him.

He might need to build alliances and persuade a frightened pride to resist Scar. Taking those risks on behalf of others would give him responsibilities that being the available heir alone could never teach him. He would also have to face an unsettling reversal: the father who once protected him now needs his help.

There would be room, too, for Simba to develop a way of leading that differed from Mufasa’s. Alongside the pressure to live up to his father, he would have to work out what he could contribute that Mufasa could not.

## What survival would cost

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. Questioning the decision to kill Mufasa means taking that value seriously.

Still, “children need stories about death” does not settle whether this particular story needed it. Children also need stories about disability and recovery, about reconciliation, and about relationships with parents changing over time. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for those experiences.

His survival could carry lasting costs. He might lose his health or his authority, along with his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family, and Scar’s rule could still devastate their home. Even after Scar’s defeat, they would have to live with damage that returning to the Pride Lands could not undo.

## A father and son after the fall

Mufasa’s death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound. It helps make the movie memorable, and arguing against it means questioning one of the film’s greatest artistic successes. I think the alternative would have asked more of both characters.

An injured Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector. Simba could no longer rely on being the protected child. They would have to work out a new relationship while confronting what Scar had done to their family and their home.

That story would still have betrayal, danger, moral urgency, and a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. It would also give father and son a future together that neither had expected or felt ready for. Mufasa should have lived so we could watch them attempt it.

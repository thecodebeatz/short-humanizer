# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have lived in *The Lion King*. His death scene succeeds almost unbearably well. The stampede, Scar's betrayal, Simba nudging his father's motionless body: few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force.

That success has largely shielded the decision to kill him from scrutiny. Keeping Mufasa alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, with suspense intact and consequences just as severe for Scar. Children's movies can include tragedy, and fictional animals have no claim to immunity from it. The question is whether this particular death was the best choice for the story.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. Removing the father creates the son's journey, giving Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. According to this argument, there is no movie without that loss. I think there could have been a better one with Mufasa still alive.

## A father who stays in the story

Mufasa is powerful, affectionate, occasionally exasperated, and playful with his son. He takes the obligations of leadership seriously. His lesson that a king must understand the balance of the kingdom suggests an ethical framework extending beyond royal privilege. He wants Simba to understand authority as service rather than permission to get his way.

The film removes him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation.

Children's stories often make this bargain: a parent becomes most useful when absent. Death clears the stage and gives the young protagonist independence. Complicated family relationships become motivating memories. The efficiency comes at the cost of exploring those relationships as they change.

Growing up can mean discovering that a beloved parent is fallible, disagreeing with that parent, or accepting responsibility while that parent is still watching. Those experiences are less tidy than inheriting a dead father's wisdom. Children learning how to become themselves may recognize them more readily than bereavement.

Simba could have matured alongside Mufasa, with opportunities to question his father's judgment and respond to it.

## Scar's betrayal could leave Mufasa alive

Scar could still attempt to murder his brother. The stampede could remain, along with the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Believing his brother dead or determined to finish the job, Scar could seize power. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under Scar's rule.

A surviving Mufasa would have to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. The confident ruler who could face down hyenas would have to reckon with his vulnerability to manipulation. He might need help, and his injuries might leave him unable to reclaim his position through physical dominance. The film would then require something its original structure largely avoids: an older authority figure who has to change.

Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He becomes an ideal who can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider anything. A living Mufasa could retain his wisdom while facing decisions that his old certainties no longer helped him make.

## Give Simba responsibility while his father lives

A surviving Mufasa raises a real question about Simba's role: if the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

Imagine Simba learning that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. Simba would have to return knowing that Mufasa could no longer handle everything. His place in the succession would give him a claim to the throne; it would leave him responsible for deciding how to reclaim it.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps his return would expose him to immediate capture. Either possibility would require Simba to build alliances, persuade a frightened pride, and take risks on behalf of others. Waiting for his father to restore order would leave the kingdom in Scar's hands.

Recognizing that the person who once protected him now needs his help would be a strong test of Simba's adulthood. It would also give him room to develop a leadership style distinct from Mufasa's. Alongside the question of whether he could live up to his father, the film could explore what Simba could contribute that Mufasa could not.

## Grief and other childhood experiences

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. Mufasa's death has value for those audiences, even as the creative choice remains open to criticism.

The defense that "children need stories about death" is true in general, but it does not establish why Mufasa had to die in this film. Children also need stories about disability, recovery, reconciliation, and the changing relationship between parents and children. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all four.

Survival could carry lasting consequences. Mufasa could lose his health, his authority, or his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family, and Scar's rule could still devastate their home. Even a happy outcome would leave them with losses they could not undo.

## Let father and son change

Mufasa's death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound and helps make the movie memorable. Arguing that he should have lived means questioning one of the film's greatest artistic successes. Its emotional force, however, does not settle whether the story needed it.

The more challenging, braver choice would have been to let Mufasa live and require both father and son to give up their familiar roles. Mufasa would have to live with his inability to protect Simba as he once had. Simba would have to accept responsibilities his father could no longer carry. Their relationship would change as they confronted the damage Scar had done.

That story could retain betrayal, danger, moral urgency, and a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. It could show how much growing up remains possible with a loving father still alive: Simba taking responsibility for the kingdom while Mufasa learns to depend on his son.

# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have lived in *The Lion King*. His death succeeds almost unbearably well: the stampede, Scar's betrayal, Simba nudging his father's motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. My objection concerns the story the film could have told with him alive, rather than any belief that children's movies must be cheerful or fictional animals deserve immunity from tragedy.

The scene's effectiveness has largely spared it a question: Was killing Mufasa the best choice? I would argue no. Keeping him alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, while preserving the suspense and giving Scar consequences just as severe.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. Removing the father creates the son's journey, giving Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. According to this argument, the movie depends on that loss.

## Mufasa as a father

Mufasa's appeal comes from his affection as much as his power. He plays with his son, occasionally loses patience with him, and takes the obligations of leadership seriously. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom suggests an ethical framework that extends beyond royal privilege. He wants Simba to understand that authority requires service and places limits on getting his own way.

The film removes him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation.

Children's stories often use a parent's absence this way. Death clears the stage and grants the young protagonist independence. Complicated family relationships become motivating memories. That approach is efficient, but it limits what the story can explore.

Growing up can involve discovering that a beloved parent is fallible, disagreeing with that parent, and accepting responsibility while that parent is still watching. Those experiences are less tidy than inheriting a dead father's wisdom. Children learning to become themselves while their parents are alive may recognize them more readily than a story about bereavement. Simba could have matured through an ongoing relationship with Mufasa.

## The consequences of Scar's betrayal

Scar could still attempt to murder Mufasa. The stampede could remain, along with the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar, believing his brother dead or determined to finish the job, could seize power. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under Scar's rule.

A surviving Mufasa would have to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. The confident ruler who could face down hyenas would have to reckon with his vulnerability to manipulation. He might need help, and his injuries might leave him unable to reclaim his position through physical dominance. The film would then require something its original structure largely avoids: an older authority figure who has to change.

Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He becomes an ideal who can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. A living Mufasa could retain his wisdom while acknowledging what he had misunderstood about his brother.

## Simba's responsibility

The strongest objection is that Mufasa's survival would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up? A story about leadership could ask more of Simba than being the only available heir.

Imagine Simba learning that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. Returning would require him to act without the comforting assumption that Mufasa could handle everything. Taking his place in the succession would be only part of that responsibility.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps returning would expose him to immediate capture. Either way, Simba would have to build alliances, persuade a frightened pride, and take risks on behalf of others. Waiting for his father to restore order would leave them all in danger.

Having to help the person who once protected him would test Simba's adulthood. It would also give him room to develop a leadership style distinct from Mufasa's. Alongside the question of whether he could live up to his father, the film could explore what he had to contribute that his father could not.

## Grief and survival

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. That value deserves acknowledgment, and the creative choice that produced it remains open to criticism.

The defense that "children need stories about death" is true in general, but it does not settle whether this particular story needed Mufasa to die. Children also need stories about disability, recovery, reconciliation, and the changing relationship between parents and children. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all four.

Survival could carry lasting consequences. Mufasa could lose his health, his authority, or his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family, and Scar's rule could still devastate their home. A happy outcome could require them to live with damage they could never fully repair.

## A father who stays

Mufasa's death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound and helps make the movie memorable. Arguing against it means questioning one of the film's greatest artistic successes. Even so, I think letting him live would have been the braver, more challenging choice.

Both father and son would have to give up their familiar roles. Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba could no longer remain a protected child. They would have to develop a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

The story could retain its betrayal, danger, moral urgency, and decisive struggle for the Pride Lands while allowing a loving father to remain part of his child's life. Mufasa should have lived so the film could explore Simba becoming a leader while his father learned to depend on him.

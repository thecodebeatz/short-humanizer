# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have survived the stampede in *The Lion King*. His death is almost unbearably effective: Scar’s betrayal, the fall, Simba nudging his father’s motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. I still think keeping him alive could have made for a richer story about growing up and taking responsibility, with plenty of danger left for Simba to face.

The usual defense of his death is that it makes Simba’s journey possible. He needs grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. His father’s removal gives him all of those things. But that defense assumes a living Mufasa would have to remain the father Simba knows: powerful, certain, and able to protect him. The stampede could have changed that.

## A father with something left to learn

Mufasa is affectionate and playful with Simba, sometimes exasperated by him, and serious about the obligations of leadership. When he teaches his son about the balance of the kingdom, he asks Simba to think beyond royal privilege. Being king means serving others, even when Simba would prefer to get his way.

The film ends their relationship before Simba has much chance to question those lessons or struggle with them in his father’s presence. Mufasa becomes a memory whose wisdom Simba must learn to accept.

Absent parents are useful in children’s stories. Their absence gives young characters independence and turns complicated relationships into memories that motivate them. It also limits the kinds of growing up those stories can explore.

A child can discover that a beloved parent is fallible while still loving and depending on them. Disagreements can force both of them to change. Taking responsibility while a parent is watching has its own difficulties, especially when that parent has always seemed to know what to do. Children learning how to become themselves might recognize those struggles as readily as they recognize Simba’s longing for his father.

I would have liked to see Simba mature alongside Mufasa, with room for their relationship to become difficult.

## What the stampede could change

Scar could still attempt to murder his brother. The stampede, the outstretched claws, and the fall could all remain. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Believing his brother dead, or determined to finish the job, Scar could seize power. Simba could flee believing the lie that he caused his father’s death. Scar’s rule could still devastate the kingdom.

Meanwhile, Mufasa would have to reckon with his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. A ruler who could face down hyenas had trusted a brother who wanted him dead. Recovering from that betrayal would ask something of him that physical strength could not provide.

His injuries could also leave him dependent on others, unable to reclaim his position by fighting. He would have to learn how to accept help and exercise judgment under circumstances he had never expected.

Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral authority. When he returns to offer guidance, nothing requires him to reconsider his own choices. Survival would leave him with unfinished business. He could remain wise while having mistakes to acknowledge and limits to understand.

## Give Simba a reason to act

The strongest objection is that a surviving Mufasa would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does his son need to grow up?

That depends on what Mufasa can do when he returns. Imagine Simba discovering that his father is alive but unable to save the kingdom. The person he once trusted to handle everything now needs his help.

Perhaps Mufasa’s injuries prevent him from fighting, or returning would expose him to immediate capture. Simba would have to find allies and persuade a frightened pride to resist Scar. Waiting for his father to restore order would leave everyone in danger.

That would give Simba a reason to take responsibility beyond his place in the succession. Other lives would depend on choices he had to make himself, including choices his father could no longer make for him.

It could also give him room to develop a different way of leading. Living up to Mufasa would remain important, but Simba would have to discover what he could contribute that his father could not.

## What survival would cost

Mufasa’s death has helped some children encounter the idea of loss. For grieving viewers, the film’s insistence that love survives death can offer genuine comfort. Questioning the decision to kill him means acknowledging how much that scene has given audiences.

Children need stories about death. They also need stories about disability and recovery, about reconciliation, and about relationships with parents that change as they grow older. An injured Mufasa could have brought those experiences into the film.

His survival would still carry consequences. He could lose his health or his authority, along with his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still spend years separated from his family. They would return to a home damaged by Scar’s rule, with no way to recover the time they had lost.

Even after Scar’s defeat, father and son would have to work out how to live together again. Mufasa could no longer assume he could protect Simba from everything. Simba would have to accept his father’s limitations and decide when to trust his own judgment. Neither could return to the relationship they had before the stampede.

Mufasa’s death helps make *The Lion King* unforgettable. Keeping him alive would have risked giving up one of the film’s greatest scenes in its familiar form. I think that risk would have been worth taking. The betrayal and the struggle for the Pride Lands could remain, while father and son faced a future neither had been prepared for.

I wish Mufasa had lived long enough for Simba to know him as someone who needed help, made mistakes, and could still learn. Watching them find their way through that relationship could have been as moving as watching Simba learn to live without him.

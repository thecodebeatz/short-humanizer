# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have survived *The Lion King*. His death is almost unbearably effective: the stampede, Scar’s betrayal, Simba nudging his father’s motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. I would still question whether killing him was the best choice for the story.

Keeping Mufasa alive could have given the film more room to explore responsibility, power, and growing up, while preserving its suspense and the full consequences of Scar’s betrayal.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. His death gives Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. It sets the son’s journey in motion. But a surviving Mufasa could have given Simba a different journey, and I think it might have made a better movie.

## A father with more to teach and learn

Mufasa is affectionate, occasionally exasperated, and playful with his son. He also takes leadership seriously. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom gives Simba an understanding of kingship beyond royal privilege. Simba needs to learn that his authority carries obligations to others.

The film removes Mufasa before those lessons can become a sustained conversation between them.

Children’s stories often make this bargain. A parent’s death clears the stage and gives the young protagonist independence. A complicated family relationship becomes a motivating memory. That is an efficient way to start a story, though it limits what can happen within the relationship.

Growing up can also involve discovering that a beloved parent is fallible. A child can disagree with that parent and learn to take responsibility while the parent is still there to watch. Those experiences are messier than inheriting a dead father’s wisdom, and familiar to children learning how to become themselves.

I would have liked to see Simba mature alongside Mufasa, with both of them having to adjust.

## What survival would cost Mufasa

Scar could still attempt to murder his brother. The stampede could remain, along with the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar could seize power, either believing his brother dead or intending to finish the job. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under Scar’s rule.

For Mufasa, survival would bring a problem he could no longer solve by being strong. He would have to face his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. A ruler who could face down hyenas would need help after being deceived by his brother. His injuries might leave him unable to reclaim his position through physical dominance.

The film would then have to let an older authority figure change. Mufasa would have reasons to reconsider his judgment and his understanding of power.

Death preserves him at the height of his moral stature. He becomes an ideal who can offer guidance without ever having to revise his own beliefs. A living Mufasa could remain wise while making mistakes and struggling with what came next. That would give him something to do beyond supplying the answer Simba needs.

## Why Simba would still have to act

The strongest objection is that Mufasa’s survival would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

That objection assumes Mufasa would return capable of putting everything right. Imagine Simba discovering that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. He would have to go home knowing that Mufasa could no longer handle everything for him.

Perhaps Mufasa’s injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps returning would expose him to immediate capture. Simba would need to find allies and persuade a frightened pride to act, taking risks on behalf of others. Waiting for his father to restore order would accomplish nothing.

There is plenty of room for a coming-of-age story in the moment a child recognizes that the person who once protected him now needs his help.

It would also give Simba a reason to develop his own way of leading. Living up to Mufasa would remain important, but Simba would have to work out what he could contribute that his father could not. His claim to adulthood would depend on the choices he made while his father was still alive to see them.

## Other losses the film could explore

Mufasa’s death has meant a great deal to audiences. For some children, *The Lion King* is an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death offers genuine comfort. Questioning the decision to kill Mufasa means taking that achievement seriously.

It also leaves room to ask what another version of the story could have offered.

“Children need stories about death” is a reasonable defense of the film, though it does not settle whether this particular character needed to die. Children also need stories about disability and recovery, about reconciliation, and about relationships with parents changing as they grow older. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for those experiences.

Survival would still cost him. He could lose his health or his authority, along with his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could lose years with his family. Scar’s rule could devastate their home. By the time they found each other again, neither father nor son would be able to resume the life they had before the stampede.

Their reunion could be happy and still leave them with difficult work ahead.

## Letting father and son change

Mufasa’s death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound. It helps make the movie memorable, and I understand why questioning it can seem like arguing against one of the film’s greatest artistic successes. Its success, though, makes the alternatives worth considering.

Letting Mufasa live would force both father and son out of the roles they knew. Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba would have to accept that he could no longer depend on that protection. They would need to build a different relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

The betrayal and danger would remain, as would the moral urgency of reclaiming the Pride Lands. Simba would still have to grow up and take responsibility for his home. With Mufasa alive, the film could also ask how a father learns to accept his son’s help, and how a son becomes a leader in the presence of a father he loves. I think that would have been worth seeing.

# How to start a vegetable garden in a small backyard

A small backyard can produce plenty of fresh vegetables without becoming a full-time job. Start with a space you can manage and crops you enjoy eating, then settle into a routine for watering and harvesting. You don't need expensive equipment or perfect soil to begin.

## Find the sun

Before buying plants, spend a day watching where sunlight falls in your backyard. Check in the morning, at midday, and in the afternoon. Trees and buildings cast different shadows as the seasons change.

Most fruiting vegetables, such as tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, and beans, grow best with at least six to eight hours of direct sun daily. Leafy vegetables, including lettuce, spinach, and kale, can manage with four to six hours, especially in warm climates.

Choose a spot near a water source and within easy reach of your home. You'll find it easier to keep an eye on a garden you pass every day. Avoid low areas where water collects after rain.

## Start small

For your first season, try one bed measuring about 4 by 8 feet, two smaller beds, or several large containers. That gives you room to learn without taking on too much watering and weeding.

Keep beds no wider than 4 feet if you can reach them from both sides, or about 2 feet if you have access from only one side. You should be able to reach the center without stepping on the soil.

Leave enough room between beds to walk and kneel comfortably, including when you're carrying a watering can. In a cramped yard, a few well-positioned containers may work better than squeezing in another bed.

## Choose your setup

In-ground beds are usually the least expensive option if your soil drains well and is safe for growing food.

Raised beds are useful where soil is compacted or drainage is poor. They also give you a defined growing space, though they cost more to build and fill.

Containers work well on patios and in awkward corners. They're flexible, but they usually need more frequent watering.

Every container needs drainage holes. Use roughly 5-gallon containers for peppers. Tomatoes need larger pots, often 10 gallons or more, depending on the variety. Leafy greens and radishes can grow in shallower containers.

If you're using salvaged materials, avoid anything that may contain lead paint or chemical residues.

## Prepare the soil

Healthy soil supports strong roots and holds moisture without becoming waterlogged.

For an in-ground garden, remove grass and weeds, then loosen the soil when it's moist but not wet. Mix a layer of finished compost into the upper soil. Working soggy soil can damage its structure, so wait until it dries enough to work.

A soil test from a local laboratory or extension service can identify nutrient needs and pH problems. If your property has a history of industrial use, older painted structures, or possible contamination, ask about testing for contaminants before growing food in the ground.

Fill raised beds with a suitable topsoil-and-compost blend. Use potting mix in containers; backyard soil can become dense and drain poorly in pots.

## Pick reliable crops

Choose vegetables you already like eating, then check that they suit your sunlight, space, and growing season.

Some good crops for beginners are:

- Leaf lettuce grows quickly, and you can harvest a few leaves at a time.
- Radishes grow fast, particularly in cool weather.
- Bush beans are productive without needing a tall support.
- Swiss chard provides repeated harvests over a long period.
- Cherry tomatoes are often productive, though they need support and adequate space.
- Suitable cucumber varieties can grow on a trellis to make use of vertical space.

Look for varieties labeled "compact," "patio," or "bush." Skip sprawling crops such as pumpkins unless you have room for the vines, and stick to a few crop types during your first season.

## Plant at the right time

Your climate determines planting dates. The month alone isn't enough to go by. Find a local planting calendar through an extension service, reputable nursery, or regional gardening organization.

Cool-season crops, including peas, lettuce, spinach, and radishes, generally grow best in cooler weather. Tomatoes, beans, peppers, and cucumbers need warmer soil and may be damaged by frost.

Seeds are economical and work well for beans, peas, radishes, and many greens. For tomatoes and peppers, buying young plants is often easier.

Follow seed packets and plant labels for planting depth and spacing. Crowding plants can reduce airflow and harvests, and increase disease.

## Use vertical space

A trellis lets you grow crops in a narrow strip of soil. Pole beans, climbing peas, and suitable cucumber varieties can grow upward without spreading across the yard.

Install supports when you plant to avoid damaging roots later. Place tall crops where they won't shade shorter plants that need plenty of sun. In much of the Northern Hemisphere, the north side of a bed is a useful starting point.

Secure trellises firmly against wind. Use soft ties to attach plants loosely, and check the ties as stems thicken. Plants grown vertically still need adequate root room and water.

## Water and mulch

Check soil moisture regularly and water as needed. Push a finger into the soil: if it feels dry an inch or two below the surface, it may be time to water. Seedbeds and young seedlings need closer attention.

Water slowly at soil level so moisture reaches the root zone. Morning watering is convenient. Drip irrigation or a soaker hose can reduce the work, while containers may need daily moisture checks during hot weather.

Once plants are established, add a thin layer of clean straw, shredded leaves, or another suitable mulch, keeping it away from stems. Mulch reduces evaporation and weeds and helps moderate soil temperature.

## Feed and protect

Compost improves soil, but vegetables may also need fertilizer. Use soil-test recommendations where available, or follow the directions on a vegetable fertilizer's label. Too much fertilizer can harm plants or encourage leaf growth at the expense of fruit.

Walk through the garden several times a week. Look under leaves for insects and watch for wilting. Remove weeds while they're small.

Identify a pest before treating it. Hand-picking, insect netting, or appropriate barriers often solve small problems. If a pesticide is necessary, choose one labeled for the crop and pest. Follow all directions, including harvest waiting periods.

## Harvest and learn

Harvest regularly to enjoy vegetables at their best and encourage continued production. Pick beans and cucumbers while they're tender, snip outer lettuce leaves, and check fast-growing crops frequently.

Keep brief notes on planting dates, varieties, problems, and harvests. When an early crop finishes, replant the space with something suited to the remaining season.

At the end of the year, consider expanding if the garden felt manageable. Keep it small if that's the size you enjoy tending.

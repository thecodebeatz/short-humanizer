# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have lived in *The Lion King*. His death succeeds almost unbearably well: the stampede, Scar's betrayal, Simba nudging his father's motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force.

That success has largely spared the decision to kill him from scrutiny. Was it the best choice for the story? I would argue no. Keeping Mufasa alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up while preserving the suspense and the severity of Scar's betrayal.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. His removal gives Simba a journey, with grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. According to this argument, there is no movie without that loss. I think there could have been a better one with Mufasa still alive.

## A father with more to teach and learn

Mufasa's appeal comes from his affection as well as his power. He plays with his son, sometimes grows exasperated with him, and takes the obligations of leadership seriously. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom gives Simba an ethical framework beyond royal privilege. He wants his son to understand that authority means serving others, even when Simba would prefer to get his way.

The film kills him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation.

Children's stories often make this bargain: a parent becomes most useful when absent. Death efficiently clears the stage and gives the young protagonist independence. Complicated family relationships become memories that motivate the child, with little room for those relationships to develop.

Growing up with a parent brings its own difficulties. A child may discover that a beloved parent is fallible or disagree with them while learning to accept responsibility under their watch. Those experiences are messier than inheriting a dead father's wisdom. Children learning how to become themselves, whose central challenge is not bereavement, can recognize them.

Simba could have matured alongside Mufasa, with opportunities to question his father and respond to his failures.

## Scar's betrayal could still happen

Scar could still attempt to murder Mufasa. The stampede could remain, along with the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Believing his brother dead, or determined to finish the job, Scar could seize power. Simba could flee under the weight of a lie, and the kingdom could suffer under Scar's rule.

Mufasa would then have to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. A ruler confident enough to face down hyenas would have to reckon with his vulnerability to manipulation. He might need help, and his injuries might prevent him from reclaiming his position through physical dominance. The film would have an older authority figure who had to change, something its original structure largely avoids.

Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. As an ideal, he can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. A surviving Mufasa could retain his wisdom while having to work through his mistakes.

## Simba's reasons to act

The strongest objection to keeping Mufasa alive is that it could make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

Simba could learn that his father is alive and unable to save the kingdom. His return would require him to accept responsibility without the comforting assumption that Mufasa could handle everything. Being the available heir would be only part of his claim to leadership.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps returning would expose him to immediate capture. Simba would have to build alliances and persuade a frightened pride to act. Taking risks on behalf of others would test his adulthood, especially once he understood that the father who had protected him now needed his help.

These demands would also give Simba a chance to develop a leadership style distinct from Mufasa's. Alongside the question of whether he could live up to his father, he would have to work out what he could contribute that his father could not.

## Grief and other childhood experiences

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. Its insistence that love survives death gives some grieving viewers genuine comfort. Mufasa's death has value for those audiences, even if the creative decision remains open to criticism.

The defense that "children need stories about death" is true in general, but it does not settle whether Mufasa needed to die in this film. Children also need stories about disability and recovery, reconciliation, and changing relationships with their parents. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all of these.

His survival could come with lasting losses: his health, his authority, or his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family, and Scar's rule could still devastate their home. Even a happy outcome would leave them with damage they could not undo.

## Letting Mufasa stay

Mufasa's death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound and helps make it memorable. Questioning that choice means questioning one of the film's greatest artistic successes. I still think the more challenging choice would have been to let him live.

Both father and son would have to give up familiar roles. Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba could no longer remain a protected child. They would have to develop a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

The story could retain its betrayal, danger, moral urgency, and decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. It could do so without treating a loving father's death as a prerequisite for his child's leadership. Mufasa should have lived, with Simba learning to lead while his father adjusted to needing his help.

# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have lived in *The Lion King*. His death succeeds almost unbearably well: the stampede, Scar's betrayal, Simba nudging his father's motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. Children's movies can accommodate tragedy, and fictional animals have no claim to immunity from it.

The scene's effectiveness has largely sheltered the decision to kill Mufasa from scrutiny. I think keeping him alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, while preserving the suspense and giving Scar consequences just as severe.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. Removing the father creates the son's journey, giving Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. Without that loss, the argument goes, there is no movie. I think there might have been a better one.

## A father with more to teach and learn

Mufasa is powerful, affectionate, occasionally exasperated, and playful with his son. He takes the obligations of leadership seriously. His lesson that a king must understand the balance of the kingdom suggests an ethical framework larger than royal privilege. He wants Simba to understand authority as service rather than permission to get his way. The film removes him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation.

Children's storytelling often treats a parent as most useful when absent. Death clears the stage and grants the young protagonist independence. Complicated family relationships become motivating memories. That efficiency comes at the cost of exploring the relationship itself.

Growing up can involve discovering that a beloved parent is fallible, disagreeing with that parent, and accepting responsibility while that parent is still watching. Those experiences are less tidy than inheriting a dead father's wisdom. Children learning how to become themselves may recognize them more readily than bereavement. Simba could have matured alongside Mufasa, with opportunities to question him and take responsibility in his presence.

## Scar's betrayal could leave Mufasa alive

The attempted murder could remain, including the stampede, the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar could seize power, either believing his brother dead or intending to finish the job. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, and the kingdom could still suffer under Scar's rule.

Mufasa would have to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. The confident ruler who could face down hyenas would have to reckon with his vulnerability to manipulation. He might need help, and his injuries might leave him unable to reclaim his position through physical dominance. The film would then have an older authority figure forced to change, something its original structure largely avoids.

Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He becomes an ideal who can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider anything. A living Mufasa could retain his wisdom while having to revise his judgment of his brother and his understanding of his own capabilities.

## Simba could lead while his father lives

The strongest objection is that a surviving Mufasa would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up? That objection makes Simba's leadership depend too heavily on his being the only available heir.

Imagine Simba learning that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. Returning would require him to act without the comforting assumption that Mufasa could handle everything. Accepting his place in the succession would be only part of that decision.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps his return would expose him to immediate capture. Either way, Simba would have to build alliances, persuade a frightened pride, and take risks on behalf of others. Waiting for his father to restore order would leave the kingdom in Scar's hands.

Simba would have to recognize that the person who once protected him now needed his help. That would test his adulthood and give him room to develop a leadership style distinct from Mufasa's. Alongside the question of whether he could live up to his father, he would have to work out what he could contribute that his father could not.

## Other losses deserve stories too

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. Mufasa's death has that value even if we question the creative decision behind it.

The defense that "children need stories about death" is true in general, but it does not settle whether this film needed Mufasa to die. Children also need stories about disability, recovery, reconciliation, and the changing relationship between parents and children. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all four.

Survival could carry lasting consequences. Mufasa could lose his health, his authority, or his certainty that he understood his brother. A happy outcome could still demand difficult adjustments from him. Simba could lose years with his family, and Scar's rule could devastate their home.

## Letting Mufasa stay

Mufasa's death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound and helps make the movie memorable. Arguing against it means questioning one of the film's greatest artistic successes. I still think the more challenging choice would have been to let him live.

A surviving parent would give the film room to show how much growing up Simba could do within that relationship. Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba could no longer remain a protected child. Both would have to give up their familiar roles and discover a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

The story would retain betrayal, danger, moral urgency, and a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. Simba would have to lead while his injured father depended on him for help.

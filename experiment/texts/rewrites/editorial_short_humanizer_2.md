# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have lived in *The Lion King*. His death succeeds almost unbearably well: the stampede, Scar's betrayal, Simba nudging his father's motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. Children's movies can contain tragedy, and fictional animals have no special claim to immunity from it. The scene itself works.

Its effectiveness has largely shielded the decision to kill Mufasa from scrutiny. Keeping him alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, with as much suspense and consequences just as severe for Scar.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. Removing the father creates the son's journey, giving Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. According to this argument, there is no movie without that loss. I think there might have been a better one if Mufasa had survived.

## Mufasa as a living parent

Mufasa combines power with affection. He plays with his son, occasionally loses patience with him, and takes the obligations of leadership seriously. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom suggests an ethical framework larger than royal privilege. He wants Simba to understand authority as service rather than permission to get his way. The film kills him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation.

Children's stories often make this bargain: a parent becomes most useful when absent. Death clears the stage and gives the young protagonist independence. Complicated family relationships become motivating memories. That is an efficient way to tell a story, but it limits what the relationship can become.

Growing up with a beloved parent can mean discovering their fallibility and disagreeing with them. A child may have to accept responsibility while that parent is still watching. Those experiences are less tidy than inheriting a dead father's wisdom. Children learning how to become themselves, without bereavement as their central challenge, would recognize them.

Simba could have gone through those changes alongside an affectionate father whose lessons he sometimes questioned.

## Scar's betrayal and Mufasa's survival

Scar could still attempt to murder Mufasa. The stampede could remain, along with the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Believing his brother dead, or determined to finish the job, Scar could seize power. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under Scar's rule. The central conflict would remain intact.

Mufasa would have to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. A confident ruler who could face down hyenas would have to reckon with his vulnerability to manipulation. He might need help, and his injuries might prevent him from reclaiming his position through physical dominance.

The original film largely avoids asking an older authority figure to change. Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He becomes an ideal who can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. A living Mufasa could retain his wisdom while struggling with what he had misunderstood about his brother.

## Simba's responsibility

The strongest objection to keeping Mufasa alive is that it would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up? Yet being the only available heir is a narrow basis for leadership.

Imagine Simba learning that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. Accepting his place in the succession would leave him with the practical task of returning, without the comforting assumption that Mufasa could handle everything.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps returning would expose him to immediate capture. Simba would have to build alliances and persuade a frightened pride to act. He would need to take risks on behalf of others while knowing that the father who once protected him now needed his help.

Those demands would test his adulthood and give him room to develop a leadership style distinct from Mufasa's. Alongside the pressure to live up to his father, Simba would face the question of what he could contribute that his father could not.

## Grief and other childhood experiences

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. Some grieving viewers find genuine comfort in its insistence that love survives death. Mufasa's death has value for those audiences, though the film's capacity to console still leaves its creative choices open to criticism.

The defense that "children need stories about death" is true in general, but it does not settle whether Mufasa needed to die. Children also need stories about disability and recovery, reconciliation, and the changing relationship between parents and children. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all four.

Survival could carry lasting consequences. Mufasa might lose his health or authority, along with his certainty that he understood his brother. Even with a happy outcome, the family would have to live with Simba's years away and the devastation Scar's rule had brought to their home.

## A different relationship

Mufasa's death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound and helps make the movie memorable. Questioning that choice means questioning one of the film's greatest artistic successes. I still think the more challenging choice would have been to let him live.

Both father and son would have to give up familiar roles. Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba could no longer be a protected child. Keeping them together would let the film explore how much growing up remains possible when a parent survives.

They would have to build a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done. That story would retain the betrayal and danger, the moral urgency, and the decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. Simba would return to a devastated home with a living father who needed his help.

# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa’s death in *The Lion King* works almost unbearably well. The stampede, Scar’s betrayal, Simba nudging his father’s motionless body: few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. I still think the film should have let him live.

The scene’s effectiveness makes it easy to overlook whether killing Mufasa was the best choice for the story. Keeping him alive could have given the film more room to explore responsibility, power, and growing up, while preserving the suspense and the consequences of Scar’s betrayal.

The usual defense is that Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. His death gives Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. Remove it, the argument goes, and there is no movie. But Simba could have faced those responsibilities with his father still alive, and their relationship could have made his journey more complicated.

## Mufasa as a father

Mufasa is affectionate, occasionally exasperated, and playful with his son. He also takes the obligations of leadership seriously. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom asks Simba to look beyond royal privilege and understand whom his authority serves.

The film removes him before those lessons can develop into a sustained conversation.

Children’s stories often make this bargain: a parent dies, the young protagonist gains independence, and a complicated family relationship becomes a motivating memory. It is an efficient way to set a story in motion, though it limits what that story can explore.

Growing up can mean discovering that a beloved parent is fallible. It can mean disagreeing with them or accepting responsibility while they are still watching. Those experiences are less tidy than inheriting a dead father’s wisdom, and familiar to children learning how to become themselves within a family.

Simba could have matured alongside Mufasa. There would have been plenty for them to struggle through together.

## Scar’s betrayal could still happen

The stampede could remain, along with the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar could seize power, either believing his brother dead or intending to finish the job. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under Scar’s rule.

Survival would also give Mufasa a problem he could not solve through strength. He would have to face his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. A ruler who could face down hyenas might need help recovering from his brother’s manipulation, and his injuries might leave him unable to reclaim his position by force.

An older authority figure would have to change. That is something the original film largely avoids. Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature: he can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. Alive, he could remain wise while having mistakes to reckon with.

## Simba would still have to act

The strongest objection is that Mufasa’s survival would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

That depends on what Mufasa could do when he returned. Simba might learn that his father is alive but unable to save the kingdom. He would then have to go home knowing that Mufasa could no longer handle everything for him.

Perhaps Mufasa’s injuries would prevent him from fighting, or returning would expose him to immediate capture. Simba would need to build alliances and persuade a frightened pride to take risks with him. Waiting for his father to restore order would accomplish nothing.

Recognizing that someone who once protected you now needs your help is a demanding part of adulthood. It would give Simba a reason to act beyond his place in the succession.

It would also let him develop a leadership style of his own. With Mufasa alive, Simba would have to work out what he could contribute that his father could not. Their differences could become part of how they reclaimed the kingdom.

## What survival would cost

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with death. For grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives loss can provide genuine comfort. Questioning the decision to kill Mufasa does not erase what the film has meant to them.

Still, that comfort is one reason to value the story, not a reason to rule out other versions of it. Children need stories about death. They also need stories about disability and recovery, and about parents and children learning to live with changes neither chose. An injured Mufasa could have made room for those experiences, as well as the possibility of reconciliation.

Survival would carry lasting consequences. Mufasa could lose his health or his authority, along with his confidence that he understood his brother. Simba could still spend years separated from his family. Scar’s rule could still devastate their home.

Even after Scar’s defeat, neither father nor son would be returning to the life they had before.

## Learning to live together again

Mufasa’s death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound. Arguing against it means questioning one of the film’s greatest artistic successes. I understand why the scene feels indispensable.

Yet letting him live would have asked something difficult of both characters. Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba could no longer rely on being protected. They would have to find a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

The betrayal and danger would remain, as would the need for a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. Alongside that struggle, the film could follow a father learning to accept help from his son, and a son learning to take responsibility with his father watching.

Mufasa should have lived. I would have liked to see who Simba became with his father still there, and who Mufasa became when his son was old enough to help him.

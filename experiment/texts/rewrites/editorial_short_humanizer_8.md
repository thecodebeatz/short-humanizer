# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have survived in *The Lion King*. His death succeeds almost unbearably well: the stampede, Scar's betrayal, Simba nudging his father's motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. Children's movies can include tragedy, and fictional animals have no special claim to immunity from it. My objection is to the storytelling choice.

The scene's effectiveness has largely kept another question out of view: Was killing Mufasa the best choice? I would argue no. Keeping him alive could have produced a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up, with just as much suspense and consequences just as severe for Scar.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. Removing the father creates the son's journey, giving Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. Without that loss, the argument goes, there is no movie. I think there might have been a better one.

## A father with more to teach and learn

Mufasa combines power with affection. He is playful with his son, occasionally exasperated, and serious about the obligations of leadership. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom suggests an ethical framework larger than royal privilege. He wants Simba to understand that authority carries an obligation to serve others.

The film removes him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation.

Children's stories often make this bargain: a parent becomes most useful when absent. Death clears the stage, grants the young protagonist independence, and turns complicated family relationships into motivating memories. That efficiency limits what the story can explore.

Growing up can mean discovering that a beloved parent is fallible. A child may disagree with that parent and learn to accept responsibility while the parent is still watching. Those experiences are less tidy than inheriting a dead father's wisdom. They are also recognizable to children learning how to become themselves while their parents remain part of their lives.

## Let Scar's betrayal leave Mufasa alive

Scar could still attempt to murder Mufasa. The stampede could remain, along with the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Believing his brother dead, or determined to finish the job, Scar could seize power. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under Scar's rule.

Mufasa would then have to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. The confident ruler who could face down hyenas would have to reckon with his vulnerability to manipulation. He might need help, and his injuries might leave him unable to reclaim his position through physical dominance.

The film would have an older authority figure forced to change, something its original structure largely avoids. Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He becomes an ideal who can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider anything. Keeping him alive would let the film explore his wisdom alongside his capacity to make mistakes and learn from them.

## Give Simba responsibility while his father lives

The strongest objection to this version is that Mufasa's survival would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

Simba could learn that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. Returning would require him to act without the comforting assumption that Mufasa could handle everything. His responsibility would extend beyond accepting his place in the succession.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps returning would expose him to immediate capture. Simba would have to build alliances, persuade a frightened pride, and take risks on behalf of others while his father remained unable to restore order.

Helping the person who once protected him would test Simba's maturity. It would also give him room to develop a leadership style distinct from Mufasa's. Alongside the question of whether he could live up to his father, Simba would have to work out what he could contribute that his father could not.

## Other experiences children need stories about

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. Some grieving viewers find genuine comfort in its insistence that love survives death. Mufasa's death has value for those audiences, and the film's creative choices remain open to criticism.

The defense that "children need stories about death" is true in general, but it does not establish why this particular story needed Mufasa to die. Children also need stories about disability, recovery, reconciliation, and the changing relationship between parents and children. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all four.

He could lose his health, his authority, or his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family, and Scar's rule could still devastate their home. Even with a happy outcome, the family would have to live with consequences that prevented a return to their untouched past.

## A harder relationship to write

Mufasa's death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound and helps make the movie memorable. Arguing against it means questioning one of the film's greatest artistic successes. I still think letting him live would have been the more challenging choice.

Both father and son would have to give up their familiar roles. Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba could no longer remain a protected child. The film could show how much growing up remains possible when a parent survives, as the two discover a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

There would still be betrayal, danger, moral urgency, and a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands. Simba would have to become a leader with his loving father alive to see it.

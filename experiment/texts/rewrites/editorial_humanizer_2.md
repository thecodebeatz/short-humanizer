# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have survived *The Lion King*. His death is almost unbearably effective: the stampede, Scar's betrayal, Simba nudging his father's motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. Questioning that choice means questioning one of the film's greatest successes.

Even so, I think keeping him alive could have made for a richer story about responsibility, power, and growing up. The suspense could remain, and Scar would still have to answer for what he had done.

The usual defense of Mufasa's death is that it makes Simba's journey possible. Losing his father gives Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. If Mufasa survives, this argument goes, Simba has no reason to become king and there is no movie. That assumes his father could return to his old life, with his strength and authority intact. A survival story could make that impossible.

## Growing up with a living father

Mufasa has enough personality to sustain a relationship with Simba as he grows. He is affectionate and playful, sometimes exasperated with his son, and serious about the obligations of leadership. When he teaches Simba about the kingdom's balance, he gives him a way to understand authority beyond royal privilege. Simba needs to learn to serve others and accept limits on his own wishes.

The film kills Mufasa before those lessons can develop into an ongoing conversation. It is a familiar choice in children's stories. An absent parent gives the young protagonist independence and turns a complicated relationship into a memory that motivates them. That gets the story moving quickly, but it also cuts off possibilities.

A child can grow by discovering a beloved parent's faults, disagreeing with them, and taking responsibility while they are still there to watch. That process is messier than inheriting a dead father's wisdom. It is also familiar to children who are learning to become themselves while living with parents they love.

Simba and Mufasa could have gone through that together.

## What survival would cost Mufasa

The attempted murder could happen much as it does in the film. Keep the stampede, the outstretched claws, Scar's betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa survives, gravely injured and stranded beyond the Pride Lands, unable to return.

Scar could seize power believing his brother dead, or knowing he still had to finish the job. Simba could flee under the weight of Scar's lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer. Those events would preserve the central conflict while giving Mufasa problems of his own.

He would have to reckon with his failure to recognize the danger in his family. A ruler confident enough to face down hyenas had been vulnerable to his brother's manipulation. Now he might need help with things he once handled easily. Physical strength might no longer be enough to reclaim his position.

I would have liked to see what Mufasa did with that knowledge. The film largely spares its older authority figure the need to change. Death preserves him at the height of his moral stature, ready to offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. Survival would leave room for his wisdom and his mistakes to exist together.

## Giving Simba a reason to act

The strongest objection to this version is that Mufasa's survival would make Simba unnecessary. Why should the prince take responsibility if the rightful king is still alive?

An injured Mufasa could give him an urgent reason. Imagine Simba discovering that his father has survived but cannot save the kingdom. He would have to return knowing that Mufasa could no longer handle everything for him.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting, or a return would put him at immediate risk of capture. Simba would need allies. He would have to persuade a frightened pride to act and accept risks on behalf of others, with no chance of waiting for his father to restore order.

Helping the person who once protected him would give Simba a demanding, recognizable step into adulthood. It would also let him develop his own way of leading. Alongside the question of whether he can live up to Mufasa, the film could ask what Simba can do that his father cannot.

## Other kinds of loss

Mufasa's death has meant a great deal to audiences. For some children, the film offers an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides real comfort. I understand why people would be reluctant to give that up.

That value still leaves room to question the choice. Children need stories about death, but that does not settle whether this particular story required it. They also need stories about disability and recovery, and about families trying to reconcile as their relationships change. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for those experiences.

Survival could carry lasting costs. Mufasa might lose his health or his authority, along with his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still spend years separated from his family. Scar's rule could still devastate their home. By the time they reunited, neither father nor son would be able to recover the life they had before the stampede.

Their happiness would have to accommodate those losses.

## A changed relationship

The harder story, I think, would have let Mufasa live while making it impossible for either of them to resume his old role. Mufasa could no longer be the invincible protector Simba remembered. Simba would have to give up being the child who depended on that protection. They would need to work out a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

There would still be betrayal and danger, with the fate of the Pride Lands demanding a decisive struggle. Scar's actions would have consequences that lasted beyond his defeat. And Simba would have to earn his place as a leader while his father was alive to see the choices he made, perhaps even to disagree with them.

Mufasa should have lived. I wanted to see how they would learn to rely on each other once being father and son no longer meant what it used to.

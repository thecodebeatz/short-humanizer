# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should not have died in *The Lion King*. His death is an extraordinarily effective scene: the stampede, Scar’s betrayal, Simba nudging his father’s motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force.

That success makes the choice difficult to question. But was killing him the best way to tell this story? Keeping Mufasa alive could have given the film more room to explore responsibility, power, and growing up while preserving its suspense and the full cruelty of Scar’s betrayal.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. His death gives Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. Without that loss, the argument goes, there is no movie. I think there could have been a better one.

## Mufasa had more to teach, and more to learn

Mufasa is affectionate, occasionally exasperated, and playful with his son. He also takes leadership seriously. His lesson about the balance of the kingdom gives Simba a sense of obligations beyond royal privilege. He wants his son to understand that a king has responsibilities to others.

The film removes him before those lessons can become a sustained conversation. It is a familiar bargain in children’s storytelling: death clears the stage and grants the young protagonist independence. Complicated family relationships become motivating memories. That makes for an efficient plot, though it limits what the story can explore.

Growing up can involve discovering that a beloved parent is fallible. A child may have to disagree with that parent or accept responsibility while the parent is still watching. These experiences are messier than inheriting a dead father’s wisdom, and recognizable to children learning how to become themselves.

Simba could have matured alongside Mufasa. Their relationship would have had to change as Simba grew old enough to question his father and make decisions of his own.

## Scar’s betrayal could still drive the story

Keeping Mufasa alive would leave Scar’s attempted murder intact. The stampede could remain, along with the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Scar could seize power, either believing his brother dead or determined to finish the job. Simba could still flee under the weight of a lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under his uncle’s rule.

Meanwhile, Mufasa would have to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. A ruler confident enough to face down hyenas would have to reckon with how thoroughly his brother had deceived him. His injuries might leave him dependent on others, unable to reclaim his position through physical strength.

This would require an older authority figure to change, something the original film largely avoids. Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. A surviving Mufasa could retain his wisdom while struggling with mistakes and limitations his son had never seen before.

## Simba would still have to act

The strongest objection is that Mufasa’s survival would make Simba unnecessary. If the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up?

Imagine Simba learning that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. He would have to return knowing that Mufasa could no longer handle everything for him. Accepting his place in the succession would be only part of what was required.

Perhaps Mufasa’s injuries would prevent him from fighting, or his return would expose him to immediate capture. Simba would need to win the support of a frightened pride and build alliances. Taking risks on behalf of others would become his responsibility; waiting for his father to restore order would get him nowhere.

There is a difficult, recognizable passage into adulthood here: realizing that the person who once protected you now needs your help. It would also give Simba a chance to develop a style of leadership distinct from Mufasa’s. Alongside the question of whether he could live up to his father, the film could ask what he could contribute that his father could not.

## A different kind of loss

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with death. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. Those are substantial reasons to value the film as it exists, even while questioning its choices.

The defense that “children need stories about death” does not settle whether this particular story required it. Children also need stories about disability and recovery, about reconciliation and the changing relationship between parents and children. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for those experiences.

His survival could carry lasting consequences. He might lose his health or his authority, and he would have to live with having misjudged his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family. Scar’s rule could still devastate their home. Neither father nor son would be able to recover the life they had before the stampede.

Such an ending could be happy while leaving both of them with losses they would have to learn to live with.

## A father and son after the betrayal

Mufasa’s death helps make *The Lion King* memorable. Arguing against it means questioning one of the film’s greatest artistic successes. I still think his survival could have asked more of both characters.

Mufasa would have to give up being an invincible protector. Simba would have to stop expecting that protection. Together, they would need to work out a new relationship while confronting the damage Scar had done.

There would still be danger and betrayal, with the fate of the Pride Lands depending on a decisive struggle. Simba would still need to take responsibility for others. His father’s presence could make that responsibility more complicated: he would have to lead with Mufasa watching, sometimes needing his help, and perhaps disagreeing with his choices.

I would have liked to see them face that. Mufasa should have lived so the film could follow Simba into adulthood with his father still there, changing too.

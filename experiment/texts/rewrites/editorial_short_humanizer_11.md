# Mufasa should have lived

Mufasa should have survived *The Lion King*. His death succeeds almost unbearably well: the stampede, Scar's betrayal, Simba nudging his father's motionless body. Few sequences in mainstream animation have matched its emotional force. Children's movies can accommodate tragedy, and fictional animals need no exemption from it.

The scene's effectiveness has largely kept audiences from asking whether killing him was the best choice for the story. I would argue that keeping Mufasa alive could have produced a richer account of responsibility, power, and growing up, with the suspense intact and consequences just as severe for Scar.

The standard defense is familiar: Mufasa must die so Simba can become king. Removing the father creates the son's journey, giving Simba grief to overcome, guilt to confront, and a throne to reclaim. According to this argument, there is no movie without that loss.

## A father who stays in the story

Mufasa combines power with affection. He plays with his son, occasionally loses patience with him, and takes the obligations of leadership seriously. His lesson about understanding the kingdom's balance suggests an ethic that extends beyond royal privilege. He wants Simba to understand authority as service rather than permission to get his way.

The film removes him before those lessons can develop into a sustained conversation. Children's stories often make that bargain: a parent's death clears the stage and gives the young protagonist independence. Complicated family relationships become memories that motivate the child. The approach is efficient, but it limits what the story can explore.

Growing up can mean discovering a beloved parent's fallibility or learning to disagree with them. It can mean accepting responsibility while that parent is still watching. Those experiences are messier than inheriting a dead father's wisdom, and recognizable to children learning how to become themselves without the defining experience of bereavement. Simba could have matured through daily life with Mufasa.

## Scar's betrayal could leave Mufasa alive

Scar could still attempt to murder his brother. The stampede, the outstretched claws, the betrayal, and the fall could all remain. Mufasa could survive with grave injuries, stranded beyond the Pride Lands and unable to return.

Believing his brother dead, or determined to finish the job, Scar could seize power. Simba could still flee because of a lie, leaving the kingdom to suffer under Scar's rule.

Mufasa would then have to confront his failure to recognize the danger within his own family. A ruler confident enough to face down hyenas would have to reckon with his vulnerability to manipulation. He might need help, especially if his injuries left him unable to reclaim his position through physical dominance.

The film's original structure largely spares its older authority figure that need to change. Death preserves Mufasa at the height of his moral stature. He can offer guidance without ever having to reconsider his own judgment. A surviving Mufasa would retain his wisdom while having to examine the decisions that helped leave his family exposed.

## Simba's responsibility

A surviving Mufasa raises a reasonable question: if the rightful king can return, why does the prince need to grow up? Simba's responsibility could come from knowing that his father is alive but cannot save the kingdom. Returning would require him to act without assuming Mufasa could handle everything.

Perhaps Mufasa's injuries would prevent him from fighting. Perhaps returning would expose him to immediate capture. In either case, Simba would need to build alliances and persuade a frightened pride to act. He would have to take risks for others while the father who once protected him now needed his help.

These circumstances would also give Simba room to develop a leadership style distinct from Mufasa's. Alongside the pressure to live up to his father, he would face the practical question of what he could contribute that Mufasa could no longer provide.

## Stories about grief and recovery

For some children, *The Lion King* offers an early encounter with loss. For some grieving viewers, its insistence that love survives death provides genuine comfort. Mufasa's death has that value, and the creative decision remains open to criticism.

The defense that "children need stories about death" is true in general, but it does not settle the choice in this particular film. Children also need stories about disability and recovery, about reconciliation and the changing relationship between parents and children. An injured Mufasa learning to live differently could have made room for all four.

Survival could carry lasting consequences. Mufasa might lose his health or his authority, along with his certainty that he understood his brother. Simba could still lose years with his family. Scar's rule could still devastate their home, leaving them with a difficult recovery even after a happy outcome.

## A changed relationship

Mufasa's death gives *The Lion King* its defining wound and helps make the movie memorable. Arguing that he should have lived means questioning one of the film's greatest artistic successes. I still think his survival would have been the more challenging choice.

Both father and son would have to give up familiar roles. Mufasa could no longer be an invincible protector, and Simba could no longer remain a protected child. Their relationship would have to change as they confronted the damage Scar had done. A loving father would remain present as his child became a leader, with growing up still necessary and possible for both of them.

They would face betrayal and danger, with the same moral urgency and a decisive struggle for the Pride Lands.

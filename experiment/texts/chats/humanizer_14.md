## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits a lot of what people have been feeling lately: uncertainty, exhaustion, and a sense that big parts of life are beyond their control. Its advice to focus on your own judgments and actions gives people something manageable to work with.

A few things help explain why it seems to be everywhere.

**It offers usable advice without requiring much background.** You can get something out of Epictetus or Marcus Aurelius without studying the history of philosophy. Pause before reacting. Expect setbacks. Think about whether the thing upsetting you is within your control. Those ideas are easy to remember and try, especially when work or the news feels overwhelming.

**It fits the way social media works.** Marcus Aurelius wrote many of his reflections as short notes to himself. That makes them unusually easy to turn into quote cards, short videos, and daily reminders. The format can introduce people to the philosophy, though it also strips away context. Once you engage with a few of those posts, a recommendation feed can make Stoicism look even more ubiquitous.

**Modern writers have built a large audience around it.** Ryan Holiday's books and *The Daily Stoic*, along with popular work by writers such as William B. Irvine and Donald Robertson, have made it accessible outside philosophy departments. There's now a steady supply of podcasts, newsletters, courses, and merchandise keeping it in circulation.

**It overlaps with familiar mental-health advice.** Examining your interpretations and distinguishing events from your reactions resembles parts of cognitive behavioral therapy, whose early developers drew on Stoic ideas. For some people, that makes ancient philosophy feel surprisingly practical. It isn't a substitute for treatment, but it can provide useful habits for handling everyday distress.

There's also a particular fit with hustle culture and online masculinity. Advice about discipline, endurance, and keeping your composure appeals to people under pressure to perform. Some influencers turn that into a message about becoming invulnerable or never showing emotion. Ancient Stoics were more concerned with good judgment, ethical conduct, and obligations to other people than that version suggests.

The timing helped, too. The pandemic and its aftermath gave the question "What can I control?" an immediate relevance. Stoicism's modern revival was already underway, but that period gave its central advice a much wider audience.

Part of the appeal is genuine relief: you don't have to solve everything before you can decide how to act today. The risk is that the same advice can become an excuse to tolerate conditions you could help change. That tension explains why Stoicism can feel both useful and very convenient for the culture promoting it.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

It's a good philosophy for everyday life, though I'd be wary of treating it as a complete guide to living. The self-help trend draws on some useful ideas and often leaves out the more demanding parts.

Stoicism is especially helpful when you're dealing with frustration, uncertainty, or other people's behavior. If someone is rude to you, you can separate what happened from your interpretation of it, then decide how to respond. If you're waiting for medical results, you can recognize that worrying won't determine the outcome while still making the appointments you need. That takes practice, but it's useful practice.

The ancient philosophy also asks more of you than the popular quotes suggest. For the Stoics, being calm wasn't enough to make you a good person. You had to act justly and take your responsibilities to others seriously. Someone who stays composed while treating people badly is failing by Stoic standards.

Where I'd be cautious:

- **The emphasis on judgment can become self-blame.** You can influence how you respond to grief, illness, or poverty, but that doesn't mean the suffering is your fault or that a better attitude will resolve it.
- **Acceptance can slide into passivity.** You don't control whether a protest succeeds or whether a difficult conversation changes someone's mind. You can still have good reasons to try. Stoicism allows for that, though simplified versions often lose it.
- **Its treatment of emotion can be hard to live with.** Ancient Stoics allowed for affection, joy, and involuntary emotional reactions, but they also thought many familiar emotions rested on mistaken judgments. You can reasonably find that too restrictive, especially around grief or anger.

There are deeper commitments you don't have to accept to benefit from it. Ancient Stoicism included a view of the universe as rationally ordered, and held that virtue alone was sufficient for a good life. The second claim is a tough sell if you think health, close relationships, and material security genuinely contribute to how well someone's life goes. I think they do.

For most people, I'd recommend trying particular Stoic practices without feeling obliged to become a Stoic: examine your first interpretation, put effort into what you can influence, and judge yourself partly by how you act when the outcome disappoints you. Keep room for grief, pleasure, dependence on other people, and efforts to change your circumstances. Those are substantial parts of an ordinary life, and any philosophy you adopt has to make sense of them.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair about some modern Stoicism, especially when it's sold as a way to stay productive under any conditions. If the response to an unreasonable workload is always "manage your reaction," the employer escapes scrutiny and the worker gets another responsibility.

The distinction between what you can and can't control can also be misleading if you take it too literally. You can't control whether your boss changes a policy, but you can challenge it, organize with coworkers, or look for another job. Most attempts to improve things involve outcomes we can't guarantee. Treating uncertainty as a reason to disengage would rule out much of politics, relationships, and ordinary problem-solving.

Stoicism does have resources for resisting that interpretation. Ancient Stoics regarded justice and duties to other people as central to a good life. Accepting that an injustice has happened doesn't commit you to letting it continue. You can work to change it while acknowledging that your effort might fail.

Still, I wouldn't let the philosophy entirely off the hook by blaming influencers. Its strong emphasis on your own judgments makes it easy to direct attention inward when a situation calls for collective action. And if someone is suffering, telling them that their judgment is the source of their distress can be both unkind and a poor explanation of what's happening.

A useful test is what the advice helps you do next. Does calming down help you have a difficult conversation, set a boundary, or join an effort to improve conditions? Or does it keep you functioning in a situation that's hurting you, indefinitely?

Sometimes staying put is necessary because leaving or resisting carries real costs. Stoic practices can help someone endure that without falling apart. The problem comes when endurance becomes an obligation, and the people who could improve the situation have no expectation placed on them.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control every outcome, and learning to question our first reactions can spare us needless distress. Its emphasis on acting well even when things go badly is worth taking seriously. But the advice becomes harmful when it makes suffering sound like a failure of attitude or encourages people to endure conditions they could help change. A calmer response can help you challenge an injustice; it shouldn’t become a reason to leave it alone.

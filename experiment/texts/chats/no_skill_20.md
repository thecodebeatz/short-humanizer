## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A few things have lined up really well for Stoicism: **it offers a sense of agency in an anxious world, and its advice is unusually easy to package for the internet.**

Here’s what seems to be driving the recent wave:

- **It speaks to feeling overwhelmed.** When you’re surrounded by political conflict, economic uncertainty, and nonstop bad news, “focus on what you can control” is a compelling message. The pandemic made that especially relevant, though the revival was already underway.

- **It’s very shareable.** A short passage from Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus can work as an Instagram post, a podcast opener, or a daily email. You can get something useful from it without first studying a whole philosophical system.

- **A publishing and influencer ecosystem has grown around it.** Writers such as Ryan Holiday have helped turn ancient philosophy into accessible books, newsletters, podcasts, and practical exercises. That accessibility brings in readers; commercial incentives also favor presenting Stoicism as a repeatable formula for success.

- **It fits the self-improvement world.** Discipline, resilience, managing your reactions, not depending on applause—those themes appeal to athletes, entrepreneurs, and productivity enthusiasts. They also fit some online masculinity spaces, though Stoicism’s audience is much broader than that.

- **It offers meaning without requiring religious belief.** For some people, it provides daily practices and an ethical framework without asking them to join a religion. Its practical techniques also resemble parts of cognitive behavioral therapy, which has historical connections to Stoic ideas.

There’s a catch, though: **the version that spreads fastest isn’t always the fullest version.** Ancient Stoicism wasn’t just “stay calm and become more productive.” It centered virtue, justice, obligations to other people, and examining your own judgments. And it wasn’t simply about suppressing emotion or accepting mistreatment.

So I’d distinguish between a genuine revival of interest in a useful philosophy and a marketable “Stoic aesthetic”—unbothered, disciplined, invulnerable. They reinforce each other, but they aren’t quite the same thing.

And once you’ve engaged with a few Stoicism posts, recommendation algorithms can make an already popular subject feel truly inescapable.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It’s a good practical philosophy for ordinary life—and it’s also become a self-help trend.** The commercial version isn’t entirely fake; it just tends to sell a narrower set of benefits than the philosophy actually offers.

The useful core is pretty down-to-earth:

- **Separate your choices from outcomes.** You can prepare for an interview, but you can’t make someone hire you. That distinction can help you put effort where it matters without treating every disappointment as a personal failure.
- **Question your first interpretation.** “My friend hasn’t replied” is a fact; “they don’t respect me” is a judgment. You don’t have to believe every judgment your mind produces.
- **Make character more important than status.** For the ancient Stoics, being fair, honest, courageous, and sensible mattered more than being rich, admired, or comfortable. That’s a more demanding—and less commercially convenient—message than “become mentally unstoppable.”

You don’t need an unusually difficult life, a business to run, or a taste for cold showers to benefit from those ideas. They’re useful in traffic, family arguments, work setbacks, and the ordinary business of getting older.

**Where it can go wrong is when resilience turns into self-silencing.** “I can choose how I respond” can be empowering. “If I’m distressed, I’m failing” usually isn’t. Similarly, accepting that something has happened doesn’t mean approving of it or declining to change it. You can accept that your workplace is unfair *and* organize with colleagues, complain, or leave.

There are also real reasons not to buy the whole system. Ancient Stoicism includes strong claims—for example, that virtue is the only true good, and that external circumstances cannot ultimately ruin a good life. You might find that inspiring, or think it understates how much illness, poverty, loss, and relationships matter. You don’t have to pretend that dispute is settled to learn from the practical exercises.

My take: **Stoicism is strongest as a framework for acting well under uncertainty, not as a promise that you can become invulnerable.** A good test is whether practicing it makes you more patient, engaged, and fair toward other people—or merely more determined to appear unaffected. The first is a worthwhile philosophy; the second is mostly branding.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **fair about some modern Stoic messaging, but not a fair description of everything Stoicism teaches**.

The key distinction is between **accepting reality and accepting the status quo**:

- Accepting reality: “My workplace is exploitative. I can’t undo what happened, and I can’t guarantee how management will respond.”
- Accepting the status quo: “Therefore, I should adjust my attitude and stop trying to change anything.”

The second doesn’t follow from the first. You could instead document abuses, organize with colleagues, look for another job, or pursue a complaint. A Stoic approach can support those actions: act courageously and fairly, while recognizing that you don’t control the outcome.

One problem is that **“you can’t control it” often gets confused with “you can’t influence it.”** Most worthwhile collective action concerns things no individual controls—working conditions, laws, discrimination. Not controlling the result isn’t a reason to withdraw.

But the criticism identifies a real weakness in how Stoicism gets marketed. If every problem becomes an exercise in managing your perceptions, then structural problems get reframed as personal attitude problems. A company offering resilience training while refusing to address unreasonable workloads is a good example: coping skills may help employees, but they don’t absolve the employer.

And I wouldn’t let ancient Stoicism entirely off the hook. Its claim that your moral character matters more than external circumstances can support principled resistance, but it can also make deprivation or oppression sound less consequential than they are. Emphasizing justice doesn’t automatically resolve that tension.

So my view is: **use Stoicism to keep hardship from dictating your conduct—not to convince yourself that hardship doesn’t matter.** Sometimes the wise response is endurance; sometimes it’s resistance, leaving, or asking for help. “Stay calm” doesn’t tell you which one is right.

A useful test is: *Does this advice help me face the situation and act, or does it mainly make the situation easier for someone else to keep imposing on me?*

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control every outcome, but we can examine our judgments and choose how we act. That’s a useful foundation for courage, patience, and integrity—not just staying calm. Where it can go wrong, especially in its modern self-help form, is treating suffering as an attitude problem and resilience as a substitute for changing unjust conditions. Some circumstances need more than a better mindset; they need resistance, support, or an exit. Stoicism is most useful when it helps us face reality and act well, not when it teaches us to tolerate whatever comes our way.

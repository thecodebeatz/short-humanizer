## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism is unusually well suited to the mood of the last decade—and to the way ideas spread online. It offers a simple promise: **you can’t control what happens, but you can work on how you respond.** In a stressful, unpredictable world, that’s appealing.

A few forces have helped it take off:

- **It gives people something to do with anxiety.** Political turmoil, the pandemic, economic insecurity, and constant bad news can make people feel powerless. Stoic practices—distinguishing what’s within your control, examining your judgments, remembering that setbacks are inevitable—offer a practical response.

- **It fits the self-improvement ecosystem.** Writers and podcasters, especially Ryan Holiday and his *Daily Stoic* platform, have packaged ancient philosophy into accessible books, newsletters, and exercises. Entrepreneurs, athletes, and productivity influencers then help circulate it.

- **It’s almost perfectly built for social media.** A short passage from Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus can feel profound without much context. Add a dramatic image, a caption about discipline, and you have highly shareable content. The algorithm-friendly version is often much simpler than the philosophy itself.

- **It offers guidance without requiring religious belief.** Some people want a framework for living but don’t belong to a religious tradition. Stoicism offers daily practices, moral language, and a way of thinking about suffering and death without requiring belief in a personal god—though ancient Stoicism did have its own theology.

- **It speaks to a demand for toughness, especially among men.** Parts of online male culture have embraced it as a language for resilience and self-command. That can be constructive, but it also produces a distorted version in which “being Stoic” means never expressing pain or needing help.

There’s an important distinction there: **classical Stoicism isn’t simply “suppress your feelings and grind harder.”** Its central concern is living well through wisdom, justice, courage, and moderation. Relationships, duties to others, and participation in society matter—not just personal performance.

So the popularity is partly a genuine response to uncertainty, partly successful publishing and marketing, and partly social media turning a complex tradition into a recognizable aesthetic. And once you’ve engaged with a few Stoicism posts, recommendation systems can make the trend feel even more universal than it is.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It can be a good philosophy for ordinary life—and its current popularity is also a self-help trend.** Those aren’t mutually exclusive. The useful question is which version you’re getting, and what you need it for.

At its best, Stoicism helps with very everyday problems:
- **Criticism:** “Is there something useful in this, even if I dislike how it was said?”
- **Uncertainty:** “What can I actually do here, rather than endlessly rehearse what might happen?”
- **Frustration:** “This is inconvenient, but does it have to ruin my whole day?”
- **Social comparison:** “Am I acting well, or just trying to look successful?”

That’s useful—not because it makes you invulnerable, but because it creates a little space between an event and your reaction. Some of those practices overlap with techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy, which was partly influenced by Stoicism. That doesn’t mean Stoicism as a whole is a clinically validated treatment.

Where it becomes less helpful is when **acceptance turns into resignation**. “I can’t control my boss” can help you stop obsessing over their moods. It shouldn’t mean “I must tolerate mistreatment.” You can set boundaries, look for another job, or organize with coworkers without being guaranteed success. Stoic acceptance is compatible with action.

There are also real philosophical trade-offs, beyond bad online interpretations. Strict Stoicism holds that virtue is the only true good, while things like health, money, and reputation are “preferred indifferents”—worth pursuing, but not what makes a life morally good. That can protect your dignity when circumstances go badly. It can also seem to understate how much poverty, illness, and loss genuinely affect a person’s life. You don’t have to accept that entire framework to find its exercises useful.

And emotional life matters. **Feeling grief, anger, or fear isn’t a personal failure.** Classical Stoics distinguished involuntary reactions from the judgments we endorse, but their ideal of freedom from destructive passions is still more demanding than “feel your feelings.” If Stoicism makes you ashamed of being upset—or contemptuous of people who struggle—it’s not serving you well.

My take: **use it as a tool for perspective and responsible action, not as a requirement to become emotionally bulletproof.** A good test is whether it makes you more patient, fair, and engaged with other people—not merely better at enduring things you ought to change.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism identifies a real danger, but it’s not the whole story. **Stoicism can help you act under difficult conditions—or help you rationalize putting up with them.** The difference is what you take “acceptance” to mean.

There are two very different ideas:
- **Accepting the facts:** “This is happening. I need to decide how to respond.”
- **Accepting the situation as legitimate or unchangeable:** “This is happening, so I shouldn’t resist it.”

Stoicism has a strong case for the first. The second doesn’t follow from it.

Take an exploitative workplace. You can’t directly control your manager, and you can’t guarantee that a complaint or collective action will succeed. But you can document problems, support coworkers, seek advice, negotiate, or leave if that’s feasible. **Not controlling the outcome doesn’t mean you have no influence—or no reason to act.** “Control what you can” becomes bad advice when it shrinks into “only work on your attitude.”

Still, it would be too easy to defend Stoicism by saying every passive interpretation is a misunderstanding. Its emphasis on inner freedom really can draw attention away from material conditions and collective solutions. And when an employer or influencer tells struggling people to become more resilient without addressing what’s harming them, the criticism is especially strong: a practice for coping has become an excuse not to fix anything.

Classical Stoicism also puts justice and duties to others at its center, which gives it resources for resisting that misuse. But the philosophy doesn’t automatically tell you which conditions are unjust or how best to change them. You still need practical judgment, knowledge, and often other people.

So my test would be: **Does this version of Stoicism help someone face reality and take worthwhile action, or does it teach them to reinterpret avoidable harm as a failure of their own mindset?** The former is valuable. The latter deserves criticism.

And sometimes enduring a bad situation really is the safest available option for now. That shouldn’t be mistaken for approving of it—or for a moral obligation to endure it forever.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control every circumstance, but we can practice responding with greater clarity, courage, and care. That perspective can interrupt needless worry and help us act when outcomes are uncertain. Where it falls short is in treating character as the only true good, which can understate how deeply health, relationships, and material conditions shape our lives. Its modern self-help version can also turn acceptance into resignation, asking people to adjust their attitudes rather than challenge avoidable harm. At its best, Stoicism strengthens our capacity to engage with the world—not merely endure it.

## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism is unusually well suited to the moment: it offers a simple response to uncertainty, it’s easy to turn into online content, and it promises resilience without requiring religious belief.

A few things have come together:

- **It gives people a sense of agency when life feels unstable.** Its best-known idea—focus on your judgments and actions rather than what you can’t control—has obvious appeal amid economic anxiety, political conflict, and the aftermath of the pandemic. You don’t have to believe everything will work out; you need a way to handle what happens.

- **It translates incredibly well to social media.** A line from Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus can stand alone as a memorable quotation. Pair it with a marble bust and dramatic music, and you have instantly recognizable content. The nuance doesn’t always survive, but the format travels.

- **Modern writers have made it accessible and marketable.** Authors and podcasters, notably Ryan Holiday, have presented Stoicism as a practical toolkit through books, newsletters, journals, and daily exercises. That’s helped turn an ancient philosophical school into a recognizable self-help category.

- **It fits productivity and performance culture.** Entrepreneurs, athletes, and military audiences are drawn to its emphasis on discipline, preparation, and steadiness under pressure. Sometimes that’s a genuine philosophical interest; sometimes it’s essentially “how to perform better” with an ancient pedigree.

- **It offers guidance without a large institutional commitment.** For people who aren’t religious but still want ethical structure, Stoicism supplies habits, role models, and a language for thinking about how to live.

There’s also a feedback loop: once you engage with a few Stoic posts, recommendation systems may show you many more. So there’s both a real popular revival and, potentially, an algorithm making it feel especially omnipresent.

**The important wrinkle is that popular “Stoicism” isn’t always much like the original philosophy.** Ancient Stoics weren’t simply advocating emotional suppression, relentless work, or indifference to other people. They placed virtue, justice, and obligations to others at the center of a good life. Online versions sometimes keep the self-control and drop the ethics.

So the short answer is: it meets a real need for steadiness, and it’s exceptionally easy to package and sell. Both help explain why it’s everywhere.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It can be a good philosophy for ordinary life—and its popularity is also a self-help trend.** Those aren’t mutually exclusive. The useful question is whether you’re getting a thoughtful approach to living or just “be tougher” dressed up in ancient quotations.

At its best, Stoicism offers a few genuinely useful habits:

- **Separate what you can do from what you can’t guarantee.** You can prepare for a job interview; you can’t make someone hire you. That distinction can help you act without treating every outcome as a verdict on your worth.
- **Don’t treat your first reaction as your final judgment.** Feeling insulted doesn’t necessarily mean someone intended an insult, and feeling afraid doesn’t prove something is dangerous. Creating that pause is valuable.
- **Judge success partly by how you act.** Honesty, fairness, and courage remain available even when recognition or good luck don’t. That’s a more stable foundation than always needing to win.

None of that requires being wealthy, unusually disciplined, or emotionally invulnerable. And some Stoic practices resemble techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy, though reading philosophy isn’t a substitute for treatment.

**Where it gets less convincing is when useful perspective becomes an absolute doctrine.** Classical Stoicism holds that virtue is the only true good, while things like health, money, and reputation are “preferred indifferents.” That doesn’t mean you shouldn’t pursue them—but it’s a demanding position. You can find Stoic exercises helpful without accepting that losing your home or becoming seriously ill doesn’t make your life worse in a morally important sense.

There are also ways to misuse it:

- “I can’t control other people” can become an excuse not to confront mistreatment.
- “My judgments shape my distress” can slide into blaming yourself for grief, trauma, or hardship.
- “Accept reality” can turn into tolerating conditions you could help change.

Those aren’t inevitable consequences of Stoicism. Its emphasis on justice and social duty can support standing up to abuse or working for change. **Acceptance of what has happened is not approval of it, or a promise to leave it unchanged.**

My take: Stoicism is a strong philosophy for keeping your bearings under pressure, but I wouldn’t treat it as the whole of a good life. Use it to become less ruled by fear, status, and frustration—not less capable of love, grief, pleasure, or asking for help. If it makes you steadier *and* kinder, it’s probably helping. If it makes you numb, self-punishing, or contemptuous of people who struggle, something has gone wrong.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **fair about some modern Stoicism, but not a decisive objection to Stoicism itself**. The key distinction is between accepting reality and accepting that reality ought to stay as it is.

Suppose your workplace is exploitative. A useful Stoic response would be: “This is happening. I can’t guarantee how management will respond, but I can document problems, set boundaries, look for another job, or organize with coworkers.” A passive response would be: “Management is outside my control, so I should just change my attitude.” Both can sound Stoic, but they lead to very different places.

The phrase **“focus on what you can control” is especially easy to misread**. Almost nothing important is entirely under one person’s control—not a relationship, an election, or a campaign for better working conditions. But we can influence things we cannot control. Uncertainty about success isn’t a reason not to act.

Ancient Stoicism also treated justice and our responsibilities to others as central virtues. On those terms, enduring injustice quietly isn’t automatically admirable; sometimes courage and justice require resisting it. Still, its record isn’t spotless: ancient Stoics lived within deeply hierarchical societies, and their emphasis on inner freedom didn’t consistently produce demands for structural reform.

The modern criticism gets strongest when Stoicism is used by someone who benefits from other people’s endurance. **An employer telling overworked staff to practice resilience is different from workers choosing practices that help them cope while pursuing better conditions.** The same technique can support agency or help preserve an unfair arrangement, depending on how it’s used.

So I’d judge it by a practical question: **Does this approach help people face the situation and act, or help them explain away why they shouldn’t?** Sometimes staying, coping, or waiting is the safest available choice, and that deserves respect too. But a philosophy that makes every bad situation a problem with your attitude is inadequate. We need ways to change circumstances as well as ways to endure what we can’t yet change.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control every outcome, but we can practice responding with clarity, courage, and fairness. That’s a useful corrective to a culture obsessed with status and success. Where it can go wrong—especially in its modern self-help form—is turning resilience into an obligation to tolerate bad conditions, or treating suffering as merely a failure of perspective. The best Stoicism helps us face reality without being ruled by it; the worst teaches us to adjust ourselves to circumstances we ought to change. The test is whether it makes us more capable of acting wisely and caring for others, not merely better at enduring.

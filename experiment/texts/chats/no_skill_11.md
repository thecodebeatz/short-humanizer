## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A lot of it is that **Stoicism offers a simple, usable response to a very contemporary feeling: “The world is chaotic, and I don’t have much control over it.”** But several things have helped make it feel ubiquitous.

- **It fits an anxious, uncertain era.** Political turmoil, economic insecurity, the pandemic, and nonstop bad news make the distinction between what you can and can’t control especially appealing. You can’t fix everything, but you can work on your judgments and actions.

- **It translates extremely well into internet content.** A short Marcus Aurelius quote can become an Instagram post, a podcast opening, or a morning-routine video. A complicated philosophy gets packaged as memorable advice: anticipate setbacks, remember life is short, don’t let other people dictate your mood.

- **Modern authors built a whole ecosystem around it.** Writers such as William Irvine, Ryan Holiday, and Donald Robertson helped bring Stoicism to large contemporary audiences. Books, newsletters, podcasts, apps, and online communities turned a philosophical tradition into a daily practice—and a recognizable brand. This resurgence was already underway before the pandemic.

- **It overlaps with familiar approaches to mental health.** Stoic ideas about examining judgments and distinguishing events from our interpretations resemble parts of cognitive behavioral therapy; some early CBT developers explicitly drew on Stoic philosophy. That gives it both practical appeal and a connection to modern psychology, though reading Stoicism isn’t a substitute for treatment.

- **It offers meaning without requiring religious belief.** For some people, it supplies something they miss from religion: rituals, moral guidance, a community, and a way to think about suffering and death.

- **It has found a particularly visible home in productivity and masculine self-improvement culture.** Discipline, resilience, and emotional steadiness are easy to market to entrepreneurs, athletes, and young men. That’s one influential corner of its popularity, not the whole audience.

There’s also a feedback loop: once you engage with a few Stoicism posts, recommendation systems may show you many more. So there’s a real revival, but your feed can amplify the feeling that it’s *everywhere*.

The important wrinkle is that **popular “internet Stoicism” isn’t always very close to the original philosophy**. Ancient Stoicism wasn’t just “be tough,” suppress your feelings, or optimize your workday. It put virtue, justice, and obligations to other people at the center. Its advice about accepting what happens was meant to coexist with acting responsibly—not passively accepting injustice.

So my short answer is: it meets a genuine need for steadiness and meaning, and it happens to be exceptionally easy to package and sell online.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It can be a good philosophy for ordinary life—and it’s also a self-help trend.** The useful distinction is between Stoicism as a serious ethical practice and Stoicism as a brand promising to make you unshakeable.

You don’t have to be an emperor or endure some great ordeal to benefit from its core habits:

- **Separate your actions from their outcomes.** You can prepare well for an interview, but you can’t guarantee an offer. That distinction lets you take responsibility without treating every disappointment as a personal failure.
- **Examine your first interpretation.** “My friend hasn’t replied” is an observation; “They don’t care about me” is a judgment. Pausing between them can prevent unnecessary distress.
- **Make character more important than status.** Asking “Was I honest, fair, and courageous?” gives you a steadier standard than “Did I win or impress everyone?”
- **Expect some discomfort.** Not every inconvenience needs fixing, and not every unpleasant feeling means something has gone wrong.

Those are useful practices for relationships, work, parenting, illness—fairly normal human territory.

**The trouble comes when those ideas become absolutes.** “Focus on what you control” can turn into ignoring conditions you could help change. “Examine your judgments” can become blaming yourself for suffering. And “Don’t be ruled by emotion” can get distorted into “Don’t feel anything.” That last version isn’t emotional maturity; it’s suppression.

There are also genuine reasons not to adopt the whole philosophy. Ancient Stoicism makes demanding claims—for example, that virtue is the only true good, while health and wealth are “preferred indifferents.” That doesn’t mean health is worthless or shouldn’t be pursued, but you can reasonably think that illness, deprivation, and loss matter more to a good life than that framework allows. You don’t have to accept the entire system to learn from it.

And while Stoic practices overlap with some techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy, **evidence for CBT isn’t evidence that the whole Stoic worldview is correct**, or that reading Marcus Aurelius will treat a mental health condition.

My take: **Stoicism works best as a discipline for your own responses, not as a verdict on anyone’s suffering.** “How can I meet this difficulty well?” is often helpful. “You’re upset only because you’re thinking about it wrong” usually isn’t.

A good test is whether practicing it makes you **more patient, fair, and engaged with other people**, rather than merely harder to hurt. If it mainly makes you emotionally distant, ashamed of needing help, or resigned to mistreatment, something has gone wrong—whether in the interpretation or in its fit for you.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **fair about some modern Stoicism, but too broad as a judgment of Stoicism itself.** The key distinction is between accepting that a situation exists and accepting that it ought to continue.

If your workplace is exploitative, for example:

- **Acceptance of reality:** “This is happening. I can’t undo it by wishing it weren’t.”
- **Resignation:** “It’s outside my control, so I should learn not to mind.”
- **Constructive action:** “I can’t guarantee change, but I can document problems, organize with colleagues, make a complaint, or look for another job.”

Stoicism can support that third response. Its emphasis on justice and courage gives you reasons to act, not just ways to stay calm. **Not controlling the outcome doesn’t mean you have no influence—or no responsibility.** A lot of worthwhile action, from raising children to political organizing, involves uncertain results.

But the criticism identifies a real danger. When Stoicism gets reduced to “manage your reaction,” it can turn a social problem into an individual coping assignment. Instead of asking whether working conditions are unreasonable, you ask whether you’re resilient enough to tolerate them. That’s especially troubling when the person recommending acceptance benefits from your staying put.

And I wouldn’t completely let the ancient philosophy off the hook. Its claim that your moral character matters more than external circumstances can help preserve dignity under hardship. But it can also understate how deeply poverty, illness, discrimination, and violence affect people’s lives. Justice is part of Stoicism, but that doesn’t make every Stoic framing adequate to those realities.

My test would be: **Does this practice help you face the facts and choose your response, or does it make endurance seem like your only respectable option?** Sometimes endurance is necessary, especially when changing things is dangerous or temporarily impossible. That deserves respect—not the suggestion that the situation is therefore acceptable.

The best version is: “Don’t let what you can’t control stop you from doing what you can.” The worst version is: “Learn to tolerate whatever happens to you.” Those are very different philosophies of life.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control everything that happens to us, but we can practice responding with greater patience, courage, and integrity. That’s a useful antidote to anxiety and the pressure to measure our worth by outcomes. Where it can go wrong—especially in its modern self-help form—is turning resilience into a duty to tolerate bad conditions, or treating suffering as merely a failure of perspective. The best Stoicism helps us face reality and act wisely; the worst teaches us to adjust ourselves to circumstances we should be trying to change.

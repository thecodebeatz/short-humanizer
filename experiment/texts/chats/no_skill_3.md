## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A big reason is that **Stoicism offers a simple, memorable response to a very modern feeling: “Everything is unstable, and I can’t control most of it.”** That makes an ancient philosophy surprisingly well suited to the last several years.

A few things have converged:

- **It gives people a sense of agency.** Pandemic disruption, economic uncertainty, political conflict, and constant bad news make the distinction between “what’s up to me” and “what isn’t” especially appealing. You may not control events, but you can work on your judgments and actions.

- **It fits the self-help world unusually well.** Stoicism has practical exercises: examine your reactions, anticipate setbacks, reflect on your day, remember that life is finite. You can start doing something immediately without first accepting a religious faith or studying a whole philosophical system.

- **It’s almost perfectly formatted for social media.** Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus offer short, striking passages that work as quotes, podcast clips, and daily reminders. There’s some selection bias here: the memorable advice circulates; the complicated ancient arguments usually don’t.

- **A modern publishing and influencer ecosystem has amplified it.** Writers such as Ryan Holiday and William B. Irvine helped make Stoicism accessible to large audiences. Its adoption by entrepreneurs, athletes, military figures, and productivity creators then gave it both visibility and an aura of practical credibility.

- **It provides an emotionally acceptable entry point for some people who resist other kinds of self-help.** Especially in some male-oriented online spaces, “train your judgment and build character” can be an easier sell than “talk about your vulnerability.” At its best, that opens a door to reflection. At its worst, it gets reduced to “don’t feel anything.”

There’s also a less flattering explanation: **Stoicism is easy to sell as a way to tolerate pressure without questioning where the pressure comes from.** “Control your response” can help someone through a difficult day, but it can also be used to imply that burnout or unfair treatment is merely an attitude problem. That’s a criticism of some modern uses, not a complete account of the philosophy.

And the popular version is often thinner than the original. Ancient Stoicism wasn’t just about being calm, tough, or productive; it put moral character, justice, and duties to other people at the center. Suppressing emotion or maximizing career success isn’t the same thing.

So I’d say the boom is **part genuine usefulness, part cultural timing, and part very effective packaging**. And once you’ve watched or read a little of it, recommendation algorithms can make a substantial trend feel truly inescapable.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It’s a good practical philosophy for ordinary life—and it’s also become a self-help trend.** The useful distinction is between Stoicism as a guide to living well and “stoicism” as a brand of relentless toughness.

You don’t have to adopt the entire ancient worldview to get real value from a few of its ideas:

- **Distinguish your choices from your outcomes.** You can prepare well for an interview without being able to guarantee the job. That distinction can reduce needless self-blame without excusing a lack of effort.
- **Don’t treat your first interpretation as a fact.** “My friend hasn’t replied” doesn’t necessarily mean “my friend doesn’t care.” Stoicism encourages you to examine the judgment before acting on it.
- **Make character more important than status.** Being fair, honest, and dependable is a more stable aim than being admired or successful.
- **Expect ordinary difficulties.** Delays, criticism, disappointment, and difficult people aren’t evidence that your life has gone wrong. They’re part of having one.

Those are useful habits for a parent, nurse, student, or office worker—not just an entrepreneur with a podcast.

**Where it can go wrong is when a helpful distinction becomes an absolute rule.** You influence much more than you fully control: relationships, working conditions, politics, health. “I can’t guarantee the result” shouldn’t become “there’s no point trying to change it.” Ancient Stoics did recognize social duties, but modern presentations sometimes neglect that side.

It can also turn into emotional self-policing: “If I were wiser, I wouldn’t be upset.” That adds shame to pain. The ancient Stoic account of emotions is more demanding and complicated than the popular slogan “feel your feelings, then choose your response,” but for everyday use, **acknowledging grief, anger, or fear is healthier than pretending you’re above them**. Philosophy isn’t a substitute for support or mental-health care.

And there’s a real philosophical question about its claim that virtue is the only true good. Health, affection, and security clearly matter to human lives. Stoics don’t simply tell you not to pursue those things, but their insistence that they aren’t necessary for a good life can be inspiring in adversity and too austere as a complete picture of flourishing.

My take: **Stoicism is a strong foundation for perspective and integrity, but not necessarily a complete philosophy for everyone.** Use it to become less reactive and more courageous—not less affectionate, less willing to ask for help, or more tolerant of mistreatment.

A good test is: *Is this making me kinder and more capable of acting, or just better at enduring things silently?* The first is a good sign. The second is a reason to reconsider how you’re using it.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism identifies a **real risk, but not an inevitable feature of Stoicism**. The key question is: *What are you being encouraged to accept?*

There’s a big difference between:
- **Accepting reality:** “My employer is treating me unfairly. I can’t undo what happened, and I can’t guarantee how they’ll respond.”
- **Accepting mistreatment:** “My employer’s behavior is outside my control, so I should stop objecting.”

The first can help you act clearly. The second can become a rationale for resignation. You could accept the facts while documenting what happened, organizing with coworkers, making a complaint, or looking for another job.

The popular “control what you can control” slogan sometimes blurs this distinction. **Most worthwhile action involves influence rather than control.** You can’t control whether a protest succeeds, a relationship improves, or a law changes. That doesn’t make participating pointless. You remain responsible for what you choose to do, even when success isn’t assured.

Ancient Stoicism also makes justice and duties to others central. On those terms, calmly tolerating someone else’s mistreatment just to preserve your own peace could be a moral failure, not a philosophical achievement. That said, ancient Stoics lived within—and often accommodated—deeply unequal societies. It would be too neat to present the tradition as inherently radical or immune to this criticism.

Where I find the criticism strongest is **when Stoicism is prescribed by the people benefiting from the situation**. An employee choosing Stoic practices to get through a hard week is exercising agency. A manager telling an overworked team that stress is merely a matter of perspective may be deflecting responsibility. The advice can sound identical while serving very different purposes.

So I’d judge a version of Stoicism by what it enables:
- Does it help you face facts without becoming overwhelmed?
- Does it support boundaries, collective action, and justified resistance?
- Or does it turn every external problem into a defect in your attitude?

**Acceptance is useful as a starting point for action; it’s dangerous when it’s treated as a substitute for action.** But endurance isn’t always surrender, either. Sometimes someone has few safe options, and finding a way to cope is valuable in its own right. A good philosophy should help them preserve their dignity without suggesting the bad situation is therefore acceptable.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t guarantee what happens to us, but we can practice responding with judgment, courage, and integrity. That’s a useful corrective to a culture obsessed with status and outcomes. Where it can go wrong—especially in its modern self-help form—is in turning resilience into resignation, as though unfair treatment or exhausting conditions were merely problems of attitude. Its emphasis on personal responsibility is valuable, but incomplete without attention to our ability to change things together. The best Stoicism helps us face reality and act well within it; the worst teaches us to endure what we ought to challenge.

## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits several pressures people have been feeling lately: uncertainty, exhaustion with online life, and the sense that a lot of what affects you is beyond your control. Its advice is easy to grasp: pay attention to your judgments and actions, and try to accept what you can’t control.

A few things have helped it spread.

**It gives people something to do with their anxiety.** When the news is frightening or your job feels precarious, “focus on what’s within your control” can be a useful instruction. You don’t need to settle a big religious or philosophical question before trying it.

**It travels well on social media.** Marcus Aurelius, Seneca, and Epictetus wrote plenty of short, memorable passages that work as screenshots or quotes over workout videos. Algorithms can turn an initial interest into the feeling that Stoicism is everywhere: watch a couple of clips and you’ll get a lot more.

**Modern authors have made it easy to pick up.** Ryan Holiday’s books and *The Daily Stoic*, along with podcasts and newsletters, have packaged ancient ideas into daily exercises and advice about work, setbacks, and relationships. You can encounter the practice without ever opening an ancient text.

**It suits the self-improvement market.** Entrepreneurs, athletes, and productivity influencers like its emphasis on discipline and responding calmly under pressure. There’s also an obvious business opportunity in selling books, courses, journals, and apps around those habits.

It has a particular appeal in some male online communities, too. It gives men a vocabulary for dealing with pain and uncertainty that feels compatible with strength and self-reliance. That can be helpful, though some versions slide into treating vulnerability or asking for help as weakness.

There’s a catch in all this: internet Stoicism often gets reduced to “be tougher, feel less, work harder.” The ancient philosophy makes substantial demands about justice, responsibility to other people, and examining your own beliefs. Its ideal of emotional discipline is also more involved than simply suppressing feelings.

So your impression probably reflects both a genuine revival and the way platforms amplify it. The philosophy offers useful ways to cope, and its most quotable pieces happen to be exceptionally easy to market.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

I think it’s a good philosophy for everyday life, with some limits. The self-help industry has made it more visible and often flattened it.

Its most useful insight is that you can’t reliably control outcomes, but you can work on your choices. You can prepare for an interview without being able to make someone hire you. Taking that distinction seriously can reduce needless anxiety while leaving you responsible for making an effort.

It’s also helpful about anger. The Stoic habit of pausing to examine your first interpretation, “Was that deliberately insulting, or am I assuming it was?”, can stop a bad moment from consuming your day. That’s a skill ordinary people can practice without adopting the whole philosophy.

Where I’d be more cautious is its demanding account of the good life. Classical Stoicism holds that virtue is the only true good; health, money, and even the lives of people you love don’t determine whether your life is morally good. Stoics still valued and cared for those things, but that position is harder to live with than a reassuring quote about resilience suggests.

There are a few practical risks:

- You can mistake acceptance for resignation. Having limited control over your workplace doesn’t mean you should tolerate mistreatment. You may still be able to organize, complain, or leave.
- You can turn emotional discipline into self-criticism: “If I were better at this, I wouldn’t be grieving or anxious.” Stoicism recognizes involuntary emotional reactions, but popular versions often lose that nuance.
- You can become so focused on your response that you underplay the conditions causing your distress. Poverty, illness, and discrimination call for material help and social action, too.

Those aren’t all fair descriptions of ancient Stoicism. Its emphasis on duties toward others gives you reasons to challenge injustice. But they are ways people can misuse it.

For most people, I’d recommend trying its practices before committing to its worldview. Pause before reacting, distinguish what you can influence from what you can’t, and judge yourself partly by the care you took rather than only by the result. Keep room for grief, pleasure, dependence on others, and efforts to change your circumstances.

You don’t have to become a Stoic to get something worthwhile from Epictetus or Marcus Aurelius. Reading them with some disagreement is a perfectly reasonable way to use philosophy.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair about some modern Stoic advice, especially when it treats every source of distress as a problem with your attitude. If your boss is exploiting you, learning to stay calm may help you cope, but you still have an exploitative boss.

“Focus on what you can control” can also be misleading if you divide everything into things you control completely and things you can’t affect at all. Most attempts to improve our lives sit between those extremes. You can’t guarantee that a complaint, a union drive, or a political campaign will succeed. Your participation can still change the odds. Uncertainty about the result is a poor reason to give up.

Stoicism has resources for making that distinction. Classical Stoics treated justice and duties to other people as central to a good life. Accepting that something has happened doesn’t settle what you should do next. You can acknowledge an injustice, work to stop it, and recognize that success isn’t entirely yours to determine.

But the criticism exposes a real limitation in how Stoicism is often sold. Advice about managing your reactions is useful to individuals and convenient for institutions that would prefer not to change. A workplace offering resilience training while ignoring unreasonable workloads deserves scrutiny. Who is being asked to adapt, and who has the power to improve the conditions?

I’d judge a particular version of Stoicism by its consequences. Does it help someone think clearly enough to set a boundary or take a difficult collective action? Or does it leave them ashamed of their anger and more willing to endure mistreatment? Anger can tell you that something needs attention, even when it isn’t a reliable guide to what you should do.

Staying calm while you challenge a bad situation can be useful. Making calmness the measure of whether you’re responding well can obscure whether anything is getting better.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something right about everyday life: we waste a lot of energy trying to control outcomes, and examining our reactions can help us act more thoughtfully. But its emphasis on self-command can become harsh when grief or anxiety starts to look like a personal failure. The popular advice to accept what you can’t control also risks overlooking how much people can change together. I find Stoicism useful for handling setbacks, but incomplete as a guide to when we should resist the conditions causing them.

## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits the mood of the past several years: people feel overwhelmed by events they can’t control, and it gives them a simple place to start. Pay attention to your judgments and actions; worry less about everything else. That’s appealing when your phone delivers a fresh reason to panic every fifteen minutes.

A few things have helped it spread:

- **It offers practical advice without requiring religious belief.** You can read Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus and find something to try that day: pause before reacting, examine an assumption, prepare yourself for setbacks. There’s little barrier to entry, even if the full philosophy is more demanding.

- **It fits social media unusually well.** A short passage from *Meditations* can work as a quote card, a podcast opener, or a video voiceover. Advice about discipline and handling criticism travels easily. Once you engage with some of it, recommendation systems can make it feel ubiquitous.

- **There’s an established business promoting it.** Writers and podcasters, particularly Ryan Holiday through *The Daily Stoic*, have introduced it to large audiences. Books, newsletters, courses, and journals give people repeated exposure. The recent boom has been building for years.

- **It appeals to people who feel pressure to stay composed.** Its popularity in fitness, entrepreneurship, sports, and male self-improvement circles makes sense: it offers a vocabulary for dealing with pain, failure, and insecurity that feels compatible with strength and competence. For some men, that can be an approachable way into thinking about their emotions.

- **Its ancient pedigree gives familiar advice extra credibility.** “Don’t let strangers’ opinions run your life” sounds like ordinary self-help. Reading a Roman emperor wrestling with that problem can make it feel more convincing, and less lonely.

There’s also a gap between ancient Stoicism and what gets circulated under its name. The ancient philosophy puts considerable weight on virtue, justice, and obligations to other people. Online versions often concentrate on productivity, endurance, and being unbothered. Some drift into emotional suppression or the suggestion that you should simply tolerate bad circumstances, which leaves out a lot.

So your impression probably reflects both a genuine resurgence and an internet that’s very good at amplifying it. Stoicism addresses a widespread feeling of helplessness, and its most portable ideas are exceptionally easy to package and sell.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

It can be a good philosophy for ordinary life, though I’d be selective about adopting it wholesale. Its popularity as self-help doesn’t make the useful parts less useful.

The strongest part is its advice about judgment and action. If you’re passed over for a promotion, for example, Stoicism encourages you to separate what happened from the conclusions you draw: “I didn’t get this job” doesn’t automatically mean “I’m a failure.” You can ask for feedback, challenge an unfair decision, or apply elsewhere without making your self-worth depend on the outcome. That’s a useful habit even if you never read a Stoic book.

It also asks a worthwhile question: how much of your life should you spend pursuing approval or status? The Stoic answer is to judge yourself primarily by how you behave. Taken seriously, that includes being fair and fulfilling obligations to others. Someone who stays perfectly calm while treating people badly is failing by Stoic standards.

I’d be more cautious about a few parts:

- The strict claim that virtue is the only true good is hard to accept. Health, safety, and relationships matter deeply to most people, even when losing them involves no moral failure.
- “Focus on what you control” can become an excuse to withdraw. Many things, including working conditions and relationships, are partly open to influence. You can act without being able to guarantee success.
- Constantly examining your reactions can turn into another way to judge yourself. Grief or anxiety needn’t become evidence that you’re doing philosophy badly. Ancient Stoicism has a more nuanced account of emotion than the online “feel nothing” version, but its ideal of freedom from destructive passions is still demanding.

There’s some overlap with techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy, especially examining the beliefs behind distress. That gives you a reason to take certain practices seriously; it doesn’t establish that the entire ancient philosophy is correct.

For most people, I’d treat Stoicism as a philosophy worth reading and testing, with permission to disagree. Try pausing before reacting and putting more effort into actions you can take. Keep room for dependence, vulnerability, and pleasure without feeling obliged to defend each of them in Stoic terms. If it helps you handle a difficult conversation more fairly, it’s serving you well. If it makes you ashamed of needing comfort, reconsider how you’re using it.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair about some modern Stoic advice, especially when it treats anger or dissatisfaction as a problem to fix inside yourself before asking whether the situation is unjust.

If someone is being mistreated at work, “you can’t control your boss, only your response” is incomplete advice. Their response could include documenting the abuse, finding allies, organizing, or leaving if they can afford to. None guarantees success. All are ways to influence what happens. The popular control-versus-no-control formula can obscure that middle ground, where most social change takes place.

Ancient Stoicism has resources for resisting that passivity. It treats justice as a virtue and sees people as having obligations to one another. Accepting that something has happened doesn’t require approving of it or letting it continue. You can recognize your circumstances accurately and still work to change them.

But I wouldn’t completely absolve the philosophy by blaming its online interpreters. Its emphasis on maintaining your character regardless of external circumstances can draw attention away from how much those circumstances matter. People need safe housing, decent treatment, and enough money to live. Calling those things “externals” has a technical meaning in Stoicism, but it can still encourage people to understate their importance.

There’s also the question of who is giving the advice. Choosing to practice acceptance when you have few options may help you endure something awful. A manager telling an overworked employee to practice acceptance is asking the employee to absorb the cost of a situation the manager may have power to change.

I’d judge Stoic advice by what it helps someone do. Does it help them think clearly enough to confront a problem, or does it make them feel unreasonable for objecting? Calm can support resistance. Anger can alert you to mistreatment and motivate action, even if you need to examine what it’s telling you. Neither feeling, by itself, tells you whether you should stay, leave, or fight for change.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets a lot right about how easily we make ourselves miserable by chasing approval or treating every setback as a verdict on our worth. There’s real value in pausing before reacting and putting effort into what we can influence. But its emphasis on accepting circumstances can slide into tolerating things that deserve resistance. Bad working conditions, injustice, and unhealthy relationships need more than a change in attitude. I’d take its advice on keeping perspective while leaving room for anger, grief, and the messy work of changing things.

## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits several needs people have right now, and it’s unusually easy to package for the internet.

Its central advice is appealing when life feels uncertain: pay attention to what you can control, accept what you can’t, and take responsibility for how you act. You don’t have to believe the world is fair or that things will work out. For someone worried about work, money, politics, or constant bad news, that can be a relief.

A few things have helped it spread:

- **There’s a publishing and social-media ecosystem around it.** Writers such as Ryan Holiday and William B. Irvine have introduced Stoicism to a large modern audience. Short passages from Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, and Seneca work well as daily emails, podcast topics, and quote cards. Once you start engaging with that material, recommendation feeds can make it feel ubiquitous.
- It fits the culture of self-improvement. Entrepreneurs, athletes, and productivity influencers can connect it to discipline, persistence, and performing under pressure. That gives an ancient philosophy a ready audience, though it also encourages people to reduce it to advice about getting more done.
- It offers a secular way to think about how to live. You can read a page in the morning, examine your behavior at night, and have a vocabulary for setbacks without joining a religious community. Ancient Stoicism had its own theology, but modern versions often leave that out.
- Some of its practices resemble familiar psychological tools. Examining your judgments and distinguishing an event from your interpretation of it overlap with cognitive behavioral therapy, whose early developers drew partly on Stoic ideas. That makes parts of it feel recognizable to people who already know therapy language.

There’s also something compelling about the sources themselves. Marcus Aurelius was an emperor reminding himself to get out of bed and deal patiently with difficult people. Epictetus had been enslaved. Their circumstances give their advice weight, and their everyday frustrations can feel surprisingly familiar.

What circulates online is often a narrow version, though: stay calm, work hard, don’t complain. Classical Stoicism also places substantial demands on how you treat other people, with justice and obligations to the wider community central to the philosophy. And it doesn’t ask you to stop feeling; it asks you to examine the judgments behind your feelings. Some popular versions blur that into emotional suppression.

So your impression probably reflects both a genuine revival and a very effective distribution system. Stoicism gives people practical help with uncertainty, while the internet repeatedly serves up the parts that fit on a screen.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

I think it’s a good practical philosophy for ordinary life, though I’d be wary of treating it as a complete guide to being human. Its popularity as self-help doesn’t cancel out its usefulness.

Some of its advice holds up well. Separating what you can influence from what you can’t can help you stop replaying an awkward conversation or checking news you can do nothing about. Examining your first interpretation of an event is useful, too: “My friend hasn’t replied” doesn’t automatically mean “My friend doesn’t care about me.” And judging yourself partly by how you acted, rather than entirely by whether you succeeded, makes sense when outcomes depend on luck and other people.

The harder question is what you’re being asked to accept.

Stoic acceptance can help you face a situation clearly enough to act. You can acknowledge that your boss is unfair while looking for another job or organizing with coworkers. But advice about controlling your response can also become a reason to tolerate mistreatment. Whether a problem is within your control isn’t always obvious, especially when people can change it by acting together.

There are also parts of classical Stoicism that many people reasonably reject. It holds that virtue is the only genuine good; health, money, and even the lives of loved ones fall into a different category, things you can reasonably prefer but that don’t determine whether your life is good. That’s a demanding position. You can learn from Stoic exercises without agreeing that losing someone you love leaves your well-being untouched.

And although Stoicism allows for affection, joy, and involuntary emotional reactions, its ideal involves a much deeper transformation of emotions such as grief and anger than “don’t bottle things up” suggests. Some people find that liberating. Others find it puts another burden on them: now they’re suffering *and* judging themselves for suffering.

For everyday use, I’d borrow these habits:
- Pause before treating your first reaction as the truth.
- Put effort into actions you can take, while recognizing that your influence has limits.
- Ask whether you behaved fairly, including when doing so cost you something.

I’d be more cautious with any teacher who makes grief sound like a reasoning error or suggests that needing help is weakness. Stoicism can be useful during a hard week. It doesn’t have to explain everything you value, and it isn’t a substitute for support, therapy, or changing bad circumstances.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair about some modern Stoicism, especially the version sold as workplace resilience. If the advice for an unreasonable workload is always “manage your reaction,” the employer’s decisions escape scrutiny. A useful coping technique can become a way to keep an unhealthy arrangement going.

The tricky part is that accepting a fact and accepting its continuation are different decisions. “This is happening” can be the starting point for a complaint, a strike, or leaving. You can work to change something while recognizing that you can’t guarantee success. That’s compatible with Stoicism’s emphasis on acting well without making your peace of mind depend entirely on the outcome.

Classical Stoicism also gives justice a central place. Its concern with duties to other people gives a Stoic reasons to oppose mistreatment, even when they aren’t personally affected. Still, the criticism can’t be dismissed just by pointing to the ancient texts. The emphasis on governing your judgments can encourage people to treat distress as a personal failure, particularly when their circumstances give them little room to act.

I’d judge the advice by what it helps someone do. Does it help them recover enough composure to set a boundary or join others seeking change? Or does every discussion of unfairness end with “you can only control yourself”? That second response is often misleading. People have influence over conditions they cannot individually control, and collective action can expand that influence.

There’s room for compassion here, too. Someone enduring a bad job because they need the paycheck may genuinely benefit from Stoic coping strategies. They shouldn’t have to fix the workplace for their coping to count as worthwhile. The problem comes when endurance becomes an obligation, or when people with the power to improve conditions prescribe acceptance to everyone else.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something right about everyday life: we often make ourselves more miserable by replaying what happened or trying to control how other people behave. Learning to pause, question our first reactions, and act fairly despite frustration is useful. But its emphasis on managing our judgments can put too much responsibility on the person who’s suffering. Grief doesn’t need to be reasoned away, and an unfair workplace may need changing. I’d borrow the practices that help me respond thoughtfully while staying suspicious of advice that makes endurance sound like a duty.

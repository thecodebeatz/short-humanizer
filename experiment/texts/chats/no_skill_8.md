## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

It’s a good fit for the mood of the last decade—and unusually easy to turn into internet content.

A few things are reinforcing one another:

- **It offers a sense of agency when life feels unstable.** The Stoic distinction between what’s up to you and what isn’t is reassuring amid political turmoil, financial pressure, pandemic fallout, and nonstop bad news. It gives people something to *do* without promising they can fix everything.

- **It’s practical without requiring religious belief.** Many people want guidance on how to live but don’t belong to a religious community. Stoicism offers exercises, moral language, and a bigger perspective without requiring faith in the supernatural—at least in its modern, often secularized form.

- **It fits self-improvement culture extremely well.** Discipline, resilience, habits, handling rejection: those themes appeal to entrepreneurs, athletes, productivity enthusiasts, and people simply trying to get through a hard week. Writers and podcasters, especially Ryan Holiday, have made the philosophy accessible and highly visible.

- **It’s almost perfectly packaged for social media.** A short line from Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus can feel profound without much context. Quotes become posts; posts sell books; books generate podcasts and videos. You don’t need to study an entire philosophical system to feel you’ve gotten something useful.

- **It overlaps with familiar therapeutic ideas.** Stoic attention to how our judgments shape our distress resembles parts of cognitive behavioral therapy, whose early developers drew on Stoic ideas. That makes some of its advice feel both ancient and psychologically credible—though reading Stoic quotes isn’t a substitute for treatment.

- **It provides an appealing model of strength, particularly for some men.** It gives people a vocabulary for managing pain and vulnerability that doesn’t necessarily feel like conventional wellness language. That can be genuinely helpful, but it can also slide into “never show emotion” messaging.

That last point gets at an important distinction: **popular “stoicism” isn’t always much like ancient Stoicism.** The ancient philosophy wasn’t just about being unbothered, productive, or tough. It treated virtue as the central good and emphasized justice, social responsibility, and our obligations to other people. Emotions weren’t simply things to bottle up.

There’s also probably a feedback loop in your feeling that it’s everywhere: once you engage with a few Stoic posts, recommendation systems can make a real trend look even more universal.

So the short answer is: **Stoicism offers a portable philosophy of steadiness for an anxious age—and the internet has made it into a very effective brand.** Its popularity reflects both a genuine need for guidance and a market that’s good at selling reassurance.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It’s a genuinely useful philosophy for ordinary life—but usually better as a toolkit than as a complete rulebook.** The self-help trend is built around something substantial; it just tends to flatten it.

The strongest parts are very practical:

- **Distinguishing your actions from their outcomes.** You can prepare for an interview; you can’t guarantee the offer. That distinction can help you act well without treating every disappointment as a personal failure.
- **Examining your first interpretation.** “My friend hasn’t replied” is a fact; “they don’t care about me” is an interpretation. Stoicism encourages creating a little space between the two.
- **Judging yourself by your conduct, not just your status.** Were you honest, fair, courageous? Those are often better measures of a life than income, applause, or winning an argument.
- **Accepting discomfort without immediately fleeing it.** Boredom, embarrassment, and uncertainty don’t always need to be eliminated before you can do something worthwhile.

None of that requires being a CEO, a soldier, or an unusually disciplined person.

**Where I’d be more cautious is with its stronger claims.** Classical Stoicism holds that virtue is the only true good, and that things like health, money, and reputation aren’t what ultimately make a life good—even though it generally says we should prefer health to illness and act to secure reasonable material needs. That can be liberating, but it’s also demanding. Illness, poverty, discrimination, and grief aren’t merely problems of attitude.

There are also common ways to misuse it:

- **Acceptance becomes resignation:** “I can’t control this” turns into an excuse not to challenge something you can influence.
- **Resilience becomes self-blame:** If you’re still suffering, you conclude you’ve failed to manage your mind.
- **Emotional discipline becomes emotional avoidance:** You learn to sound composed rather than acknowledge hurt or ask for help.

Those aren’t inevitable consequences of Stoicism, and ancient Stoics weren’t simply preaching passivity. But they’re real risks in how it gets marketed.

A useful test is: **Does practicing it make you more capable of facing reality and treating people well—or just more invested in appearing unbothered?** The first is valuable. The second is a persona.

So yes, it can be good for normal people. Borrow its habits of perspective, deliberate action, and integrity. You don’t have to accept its whole account of happiness—or pretend you shouldn’t need affection, security, and support—to benefit from it.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

**I think that criticism is fair about some modern Stoic messaging, but too broad as a judgment of Stoicism itself.** The crucial distinction is between accepting that a situation *exists* and accepting that it *should continue*.

If your boss treats you badly, for example:
- “This is happening; I need to think clearly about my options” is useful acceptance.
- “Their behavior is outside my control, so I should just endure it” is resignation.

You can’t dictate your boss’s behavior, but you may be able to document it, organize with coworkers, complain, or look for another job. **Not controlling the outcome doesn’t mean having no influence—or no reason to act.** The popular “control what you can control” slogan sometimes obscures that middle ground.

Ancient Stoicism also gives you reasons to act: justice and courage are central virtues. A Stoic response to wrongdoing could be to resist it, even at considerable personal cost, while recognizing that resistance might fail. That’s different from making personal tranquility the highest goal.

But the criticism does identify a real weakness in how Stoicism gets sold. When advice focuses exclusively on your reaction, it can quietly shift responsibility from whoever causes the harm to whoever suffers it. A workplace offering resilience tips instead of fixing unreasonable workloads is a good example. The tips might help individuals, while also serving as an excuse to leave the problem intact.

There’s a deeper philosophical disagreement, too. Stoics generally treat anger as a mistaken or destructive response, rather than something necessary for justice. Critics reasonably argue that anger can help us recognize mistreatment and motivate collective action. You don’t have to settle that debate to notice that “don’t let this consume you” is different from “you shouldn’t be upset.”

My view is that **Stoicism is strongest when it helps you face a bad situation without being overwhelmed, and weakest when it makes enduring the situation seem like the whole achievement.** Sometimes endurance is necessary, especially when someone has few safe options. But a philosophy worth using should leave room for both surviving injustice and trying to end it.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control everything that happens to us, but we can practice responding with judgment, courage, and integrity. That perspective can make setbacks less overwhelming and help us act without guarantees of success. Where it goes wrong—especially in its modern self-help form—is when emotional discipline becomes suppression, or acceptance becomes an excuse to tolerate injustice. Not all suffering is a mindset problem, and changing your response isn’t always enough; sometimes the situation itself needs to change. Stoicism is most useful when it helps us face reality and act within it, not when it teaches us to endure everything quietly.

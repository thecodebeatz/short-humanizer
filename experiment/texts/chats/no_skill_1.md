## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A few things have lined up to make Stoicism feel especially suited to this moment: **it offers a sense of agency in a world that often feels out of control, and its core ideas travel extremely well online.**

Here are the biggest reasons:

- **It speaks directly to uncertainty.** With pandemic disruption, political conflict, financial pressure, and a constant stream of bad news, “focus on what you can control” is an appealing principle. You don’t need to believe the world will improve to find it useful.

- **It’s practical and relatively accessible.** You can read a paragraph of Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus and immediately ask, “How would I apply this to my day?” Compared with some other philosophical traditions, the entry point can feel less academic or dependent on religious belief.

- **It fits self-improvement culture.** Discipline, resilience, managing your reactions, remembering that life is short—these themes overlap neatly with productivity, fitness, entrepreneurship, and sports. Writers and podcasters, especially Ryan Holiday and his *Daily Stoic* brand, have helped bring it to a much larger audience.

- **It’s almost perfectly formatted for social media.** A short quotation about adversity, paired with a Roman bust, makes a very shareable post. Platforms reward that compact, authoritative style—even when the quote is misattributed or the philosophy gets flattened.

- **It offers a language for emotional strength.** For some people, particularly in male-oriented self-help spaces, Stoicism makes reflection and emotional regulation feel approachable without the vocabulary of therapy. That can be a useful doorway, though it sometimes slides into the mistaken idea that strength means feeling nothing.

- **It can function as a secular moral framework.** For people who aren’t religious but still want guidance about character, responsibility, and how to live, Stoicism offers more than a collection of coping tips.

There’s also a feedback loop: once you engage with a few Stoic posts, recommendation systems can make it seem like the whole internet has become Roman.

The important distinction is that **popular “internet Stoicism” isn’t always the same as the ancient philosophy**. The original tradition put virtue, justice, and our obligations to other people at its center—not just staying calm, working harder, or tolerating a bad situation. It wasn’t simply “don’t have emotions” or “accept everything without trying to change it.”

So the appeal is genuine, but the packaging matters: an old philosophy has become both a useful response to modern anxiety and a highly marketable self-help product.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It can be a good philosophy for ordinary life—and it’s also become a self-help trend.** The useful question is which version you’re getting.

At its best, Stoicism offers a few genuinely helpful habits:

- **Separate your choices from outcomes.** You can prepare well for an interview without controlling whether you get the job. That distinction helps you act without making every disappointment a verdict on your worth.
- **Examine your first interpretation.** “My friend hasn’t replied” is a fact; “they don’t care about me” is a judgment. Giving yourself room between the two can prevent unnecessary suffering.
- **Put character ahead of status.** Being fair, courageous, and dependable matters more than appearing successful. That’s a substantial ethical commitment, not just a productivity trick.
- **Accept reality so you can respond to it.** Acknowledging that something has happened isn’t the same as approving of it or deciding not to change it.

None of that requires an unusually disciplined personality or an enthusiasm for cold showers.

**Where it gets less convincing is when useful practices become absolute claims.** Ancient Stoics argued that virtue is the only true good, and that things like health, money, and reputation aren’t what make a life morally good. There’s an important insight there: illness or poverty doesn’t make someone a lesser person. But taken as “your circumstances shouldn’t affect your well-being,” it can become harsh and unrealistic. Safety, relationships, and material conditions really do matter.

The popular version can also turn into:
- blaming yourself for every painful feeling;
- tolerating mistreatment because you’re supposed to “control your reaction”;
- treating grief, vulnerability, or needing help as failures;
- using personal resilience as a substitute for fixing bad conditions.

Those aren’t all fair descriptions of ancient Stoicism, but they’re real risks in how it’s marketed and practiced.

My take: **Stoicism is a useful foundation for some people and a useful set of tools for many more—but it doesn’t have to be your entire worldview.** You can borrow its approach to uncertainty while also believing that emotions carry information, relationships involve vulnerability, and some problems require collective action rather than a better attitude.

A good test is: *Does practicing it make you more capable, compassionate, and willing to engage with life—or just better at telling yourself nothing should hurt?* The former is worthwhile. The latter is mostly a tough-sounding trap.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **fair about some modern Stoic messaging, but not about Stoicism as a whole**. The key distinction is between accepting that a situation exists and accepting that it should continue.

If your workplace is abusive, for example, “I can’t control my boss” can lead in two very different directions:

- **Resignation:** “So I should stop being upset and put up with it.”
- **Agency:** “I can’t guarantee my boss will change, but I can document what’s happening, seek support, set boundaries, organize with coworkers, or look for another job.”

The second is compatible with Stoicism. You act where you have leverage without assuming you can guarantee the result. **Not controlling an outcome doesn’t mean you can’t influence it.** That’s where the slogan “focus on what you can control” often becomes misleading.

Ancient Stoicism also treated justice and duties to other people as central virtues. Being calm while allowing others to be mistreated isn’t automatically Stoic wisdom. Sometimes doing the right thing requires confrontation, risk, and sustained political action—not simply managing your feelings.

But the modern critique identifies a real problem: **advice about resilience can shift responsibility from harmful institutions onto the people experiencing the harm.** “Learn to handle stress” is useful advice for a worker; it’s inadequate as an employer’s answer to unsafe conditions. A practice that helps someone cope can still be used to excuse the conditions they’re coping with.

There’s also no need to make calmness a prerequisite for action. Anger can alert you to injustice, even if it doesn’t always tell you the best response. You can be upset and still act thoughtfully.

So my view is: **Stoicism is helpful when it supports effective action and humane endurance; it becomes harmful when endurance is presented as everyone’s duty.** And where someone genuinely has few options, coping isn’t a moral failure either. The question is whether the philosophy helps them see their options clearly—or teaches them not to look.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control every outcome, but we can choose how we respond and what principles guide us. That can make us more resilient, thoughtful, and less dependent on other people’s approval. Where it goes wrong—especially in its modern self-help form—is when emotional discipline becomes emotional suppression, or acceptance becomes an excuse to tolerate injustice. Our circumstances matter, and pain isn’t always a problem of perspective. The best version of Stoicism helps us face reality and act with courage; the worst teaches us to endure what we should be trying to change.

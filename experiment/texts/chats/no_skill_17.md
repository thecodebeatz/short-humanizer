## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism is a remarkably good fit for the mood—and media—of the last decade. Its central pitch is appealing: **you can’t control everything that happens, but you can work on how you respond.** In a period of uncertainty, that offers both relief and a sense of agency.

A few forces have helped it spread:

- **It gives people something to do with their anxiety.** Political conflict, economic insecurity, the pandemic, and constant bad news can make life feel unmanageable. Stoicism offers concrete practices: separate what’s up to you from what isn’t, examine your judgments, and prepare for setbacks.

- **It provides a secular framework for living.** You don’t need religious belief to take it seriously, but it still addresses questions that productivity advice often avoids: What makes a good life? How should I treat people? How do I face suffering and death?

- **It’s extremely easy to package online.** A short quotation from Marcus Aurelius can become a podcast opening, an Instagram graphic, or a daily email. Those bite-size ideas are accessible—even when they lose context in the process.

- **Modern authors and influencers built a distribution system around it.** Writers such as Ryan Holiday and William Irvine helped bring Stoicism to a mass audience. Its adoption in business, sports, and productivity circles then gave it additional visibility.

- **It offers a culturally acceptable entry point into emotional reflection, especially for some men.** Talking about judgment, discipline, and resilience may feel more approachable than talking explicitly about vulnerability. That can be useful, though it can also slide into the mistaken idea that being Stoic means having no feelings.

There’s also a feedback loop behind the feeling that it’s *everywhere*: once you engage with a few Stoicism posts, recommendation systems can serve you many more. So a genuine revival gets amplified by your particular media environment.

The important distinction is between **ancient Stoicism and its internet-friendly version**. Ancient Stoicism places virtue, justice, and obligations to other people at the center. Online, it’s sometimes reduced to “stay unbothered, work harder, become invulnerable”—which is much closer to a personal-performance brand.

So I’d put its appeal this way: it offers an old, credible language for a very current desire—to remain steady when the world feels unstable. Whether that becomes wisdom or just another self-optimization routine depends on which parts people pick up.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It’s a genuinely useful philosophy for ordinary life—and it’s also become a self-help trend.** Those aren’t mutually exclusive. The question is whether you’re getting a thoughtful approach to living or a motivational slogan dressed in a toga.

Stoicism is especially good at a few everyday things:

- **Keeping setbacks in proportion.** A rejection, a rude remark, or a delayed train can be unpleasant without having to define your day—or your worth.
- **Separating responsibility from results.** You can prepare well for an interview without controlling whether you get the job. That distinction can help you stay engaged without making every outcome a verdict on you.
- **Making values more important than approval.** “Was I honest, fair, and courageous?” is often a better guide than “Did I impress everyone?”
- **Creating a pause before reacting.** Noticing that your interpretation of an event contributes to your distress can give you room to respond differently. That overlaps with some techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy, though Stoicism isn’t a substitute for treatment.

But it has real limitations, especially when taken rigidly.

**“Focus on what you control” can become an excuse for resignation.** Many things—working conditions, relationships, politics—aren’t under your individual control but are still open to influence, particularly through collective action. Acceptance of reality doesn’t require acceptance of injustice. Ancient Stoicism’s emphasis on social duty matters here.

**Its account of emotions can also be too demanding.** Ancient Stoics weren’t simply advocating numbness, but they did argue that many distressing emotions rest on mistaken judgments. That can be illuminating; it can also encourage someone to treat grief, fear, or anger as a personal failure. Sometimes you need to feel something, seek comfort, or change your circumstances—not reason yourself out of it.

And the strict Stoic claim that virtue is the only true good is a hard sell. Health, affection, safety, and material security matter deeply to most people. You can find Stoic practices helpful without accepting the entire philosophical system.

So my take is: **Stoicism works best as a resource, not an emotional purity test.** Use it to become less reactive, more principled, and better able to act—not to convince yourself you need nothing and should never hurt.

A useful test is: *Does this practice make me more capable of caring and participating, or just better at distancing myself?* The former is a strong reason to keep it; the latter is a reason to reconsider.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **fair about some modern Stoic advice, but not a decisive objection to Stoicism itself**. The crucial distinction is between accepting that something is happening and accepting that it should continue.

If your workplace is exploitative, for example, “I can’t control my boss” could lead in two directions:

- **Resignation:** “So I should stop being upset and put up with it.”
- **Strategic action:** “I can’t guarantee my boss will change, but I can document what’s happening, talk with coworkers, seek advice, or look for another job.”

The second is compatible with Stoicism. You act because fairness matters, while recognizing that doing the right thing doesn’t guarantee success. **Acceptance can mean giving up the demand for a guaranteed outcome—not giving up the effort.**

But there’s a real problem with how Stoicism is sometimes marketed. Advice focused entirely on managing your reactions can quietly turn a social problem into an individual coping problem. “How can we make this workplace less harmful?” becomes “How can you become resilient enough to tolerate it?” That’s particularly troubling when the advice comes from someone who benefits from things staying as they are.

And I wouldn’t completely let ancient Stoicism off the hook. Its view that your moral character matters more than external circumstances can help people resist intimidation or hardship. But it can also understate how much poverty, illness, discrimination, and dependence constrain people’s lives. Those circumstances aren’t merely bad attitudes waiting to be corrected.

The strongest Stoic reply is that **justice is a virtue too**. Being calm isn’t the highest goal; acting well is. Sometimes acting well means enduring something unavoidable. Sometimes it means confronting someone, organizing, or leaving. You don’t have to be emotionally unruffled before you’re entitled to act.

So my test would be: **Does this version of Stoicism help people face reality and act within it, or teach them to need less from people and institutions that owe them better?** If it consistently does the latter, the criticism lands—even if its advocates can quote Marcus Aurelius.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control every outcome, but we can practice responding with judgment, courage, and integrity. That’s a useful antidote to anxiety and the exhausting pursuit of approval. Where it can go wrong—especially in its modern self-help form—is turning resilience into a duty to tolerate bad conditions, or treating grief and anger as failures of discipline. Not everything painful is a mindset problem. Stoicism is at its best when acceptance helps us face reality and act justly; it’s at its worst when acceptance becomes an excuse to leave things unchanged.

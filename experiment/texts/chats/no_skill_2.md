## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits the mood of the last few years remarkably well: **the world feels hard to control, and Stoicism offers a way to feel less controlled by it.** But its visibility also owes a lot to social media and self-help marketing.

A few forces have converged:

- **It offers a practical response to uncertainty.** Political turmoil, the pandemic, economic anxiety, and constant bad news make “focus on what you can control” an appealing principle. You don’t have to believe things will turn out well to find it useful.

- **It’s easy to turn into shareable content.** A short passage from Marcus Aurelius can become a quote card, podcast topic, or morning exercise. That makes Stoicism unusually well suited to platforms that reward concise, confident advice—even when the original philosophy is more complicated.

- **Modern popularizers have built a whole ecosystem around it.** Writers and podcasters such as Ryan Holiday have translated ancient texts into accessible books, newsletters, and daily practices. Once you engage with a little of that content, recommendation algorithms can make it feel ubiquitous.

- **It offers meaning without requiring religious belief.** For some people, Stoicism provides an ethical framework, daily rituals, and a way to think about suffering and death without asking them to accept supernatural claims.

- **It meshes with productivity and self-improvement culture.** Discipline, resilience, and freedom from other people’s opinions appeal to entrepreneurs, athletes, and people trying to manage demanding lives. It also has particular traction in some male-oriented online spaces because it presents emotional self-management in the language of strength and agency.

There’s an important catch: **popular “internet Stoicism” isn’t always very Stoic.** It can become “suppress your feelings, work harder, and tolerate everything.” Ancient Stoicism was more concerned with virtue, sound judgment, and obligations to other people—not simply becoming unbothered or successful. Its distinction between what we control and what we don’t also wasn’t meant to prohibit trying to improve the world.

So the popularity is partly a genuine response to real needs, and partly a very effective packaging job: **ancient authority + practical exercises + advice that travels well online.** The revival has been building for more than a decade, but recent upheavals—and the algorithms—have made it much more visible.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It can be a good philosophy for ordinary life—and it’s also a self-help trend.** The useful ideas aren’t invalidated by the marketing, but the marketing often strips away what makes them worthwhile.

Stoicism is particularly helpful for a few everyday problems:

- **Worrying about outcomes you can influence but can’t guarantee.** You can prepare for a job interview; you can’t make someone hire you. The Stoic move is to take the preparation seriously without treating the result as a verdict on your worth.
- **Reacting impulsively.** The practice is to notice “I’m having the thought that this person disrespected me” before treating that interpretation as a fact. That pause can make room for a better response.
- **Depending too much on approval, status, or possessions.** Stoicism asks whether you’re building your life around things that can be taken away—and what qualities you want to keep regardless.

That’s substantial advice, not just motivational decoration.

**Where it gets less convincing is when it becomes a complete explanation of suffering.** Ancient Stoics held that virtue is the only true good and that things such as health, money, and reputation don’t determine whether a life is good. There’s something liberating in that, but it’s also a demanding claim. Poverty, illness, discrimination, and loss aren’t merely problems of attitude; they materially affect people’s lives.

A few other limits matter:

- **Acceptance can slide into resignation.** “I can’t control other people” doesn’t mean “I shouldn’t set boundaries, organize for change, or leave a bad situation.”
- **Emotional discipline can turn into emotional avoidance.** Stoicism isn’t simply “feel nothing,” but its language can be used to shame normal grief, fear, or vulnerability.
- **Self-sufficiency can be oversold.** Needing affection, community, or professional support isn’t a failure of character.

The ancient tradition has resources for resisting those distortions—it emphasizes justice and duties to others—but not every problem comes from people misunderstanding it. You can reasonably disagree with some of its core claims.

My practical verdict: **use Stoicism as a toolkit before adopting it as a whole worldview.** Try its exercises, read some Epictetus or Marcus Aurelius beyond the quote cards, and judge it by the effects. Does it make you more patient, courageous, fair, and able to act? Or just better at telling yourself that nothing hurts?

A good version helps you care without being consumed. A bad version teaches you to pretend you don’t care.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **sometimes exactly right—but it depends on what “acceptance” is doing.** It can help people face reality and act effectively, or it can teach them to accommodate things they should resist.

There are two very different claims hiding in the word:

- **“This is happening, even though I wish it weren’t.”** That’s accepting a fact. It can be the starting point for change.
- **“This is happening, so I should tolerate it.”** That’s a judgment about what to do. It doesn’t follow from the first claim.

Suppose your boss routinely humiliates you. A useful Stoic response might be: “Their behavior doesn’t determine my worth. What options do I have—documenting it, setting a boundary, getting support, finding another job?” An unhelpful version is: “My distress is the real problem, so I need to become less sensitive.” The first preserves agency; the second can help keep you stuck.

**Modern Stoicism becomes especially questionable when people with power prescribe it to people with less power.** An employer offering resilience tips instead of fixing unsafe conditions is shifting responsibility onto workers. Coping skills may still help those workers, but they aren’t a substitute for changing the conditions. Both things can be true.

The ancient philosophy isn’t simply a doctrine of passivity: justice and duties to others are central to it. Also, “you can’t control the outcome” needn’t mean “don’t try.” Almost no meaningful collective effort guarantees success. You can work toward a change without making your willingness to act depend on certainty that you’ll win.

But that historical defense only goes so far. Stoicism’s emphasis on your own judgments can genuinely encourage people to underweight material conditions, justified anger, or their need for support. That’s a real tension, not just a misunderstanding by internet influencers.

My test would be: **Does this practice expand your ability to respond, or narrow it to enduring?** Sometimes enduring is necessary—especially when leaving or resisting isn’t currently safe or possible—and that deserves respect. But a philosophy that treats endurance as the *only* admirable response has become a philosophy of compliance, not wisdom.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control every outcome, but we can practice responding with greater patience, courage, and fairness. That can loosen the grip of anxiety and other people’s approval. Where it falls short is in treating virtue as sufficient for a good life, underestimating how deeply health, poverty, injustice, and relationships shape our well-being. Its modern versions can go further, turning emotional discipline into suppression and acceptance into resignation. At its best, Stoicism helps us face reality and act wisely; at its worst, it teaches us to endure what we should be trying to change.

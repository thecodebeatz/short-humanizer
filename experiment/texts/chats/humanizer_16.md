## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A lot of Stoicism’s recent popularity comes from how neatly it fits life online: you’re exposed to more bad news, comparison, and conflict than you can do much about. A philosophy that says “pay attention to what’s within your control” offers some relief.

Several things have helped it spread:

- **It gives people something practical to do with anxiety.** You can’t control the economy or someone else’s opinion, but you can decide how to respond. That’s an appealing idea during periods of uncertainty. The pandemic probably made it especially relevant, though the revival was already underway.

- **It’s easy to package for social media.** A short passage from Marcus Aurelius can work as a quote card, a podcast opening, or a daily reminder. The historical context often gets lost, but the advice travels well.

- **There’s now a whole business built around it.** Writers and podcasters, especially Ryan Holiday and *The Daily Stoic*, have made ancient philosophy accessible through books, newsletters, and videos. Repeated exposure helps explain the feeling that it’s everywhere.

- **It fits self-improvement culture.** Discipline, resilience, and managing your reactions appeal to athletes, entrepreneurs, and productivity audiences. Some versions promise that you can train your mind much as you train your body.

- **It can give men a socially comfortable way to talk about distress.** For some, discussing fear or disappointment through Marcus Aurelius feels more approachable than using the language of therapy. That can open a useful door, although it can also encourage emotional suppression when the advice gets reduced to “toughen up.”

There’s also an appealing sense of continuity: someone living almost two thousand years ago was dealing with vanity, anger, difficult colleagues, and the fear of death. Reading that can make your own problems feel less isolating.

The version that spreads online is selective, though. Ancient Stoicism treated virtue as the basis of a good life and put substantial weight on our obligations to other people. Posts about becoming unbothered or outperforming your rivals often leave those commitments out. Stoics also distinguished between involuntary feelings and the judgments we make about them, which is more nuanced than simply refusing to feel anything.

And some of the “everywhere” effect may be your feed. Once you watch a few Stoicism clips, recommendation systems can serve you more, making a genuine revival look even more universal than it is.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

Stoicism can be a good philosophy for ordinary life, even though a lot of what’s sold under the name is generic self-help. Its usefulness depends on how you apply it and what you expect it to do.

The most useful part is learning to separate your choices from outcomes you can only influence. You can prepare for a job interview; you can’t guarantee an offer. Taking that distinction seriously can help you put in the effort without treating every rejection as a verdict on your worth. It also leaves room for disappointment.

Stoicism is also good at getting you to examine your first interpretation of an event. “My friend hasn’t replied” is an observation. “They don’t care about me” is a judgment that might be wrong. Pausing before acting on that judgment is a useful habit. Related techniques appear in cognitive behavioral therapy, whose early developers drew partly on Stoic ideas, though that doesn’t establish the whole philosophy as a treatment.

There are some real limitations:

- Acceptance can become resignation. A bad workplace may be something you can leave or help change. “I can’t control other people” is true, but you can still set boundaries, organize with others, or ask for help. Stoic acceptance concerns the outcome; it doesn’t rule out action.
- Self-discipline can turn into self-blame. Illness, poverty, trauma, and exhaustion constrain people’s options. Advice about choosing your response becomes cruel when it ignores those constraints or treats distress as a character flaw.
- The ancient philosophy makes demanding claims. Classical Stoics held that virtue alone is sufficient for a good life, and that things like health and wealth are preferable but not genuinely good in themselves. You can find their exercises helpful while thinking that this understates how much loss and hardship matter.

The popular version often pares all this down to staying calm and being productive. Ancient Stoicism asks a harder question: are you acting justly, including toward people who can do nothing for you? That makes it more demanding than a collection of coping tips.

My view is that it’s worth trying as a set of practices before adopting it as a complete worldview. Examine an upsetting assumption. Choose a worthwhile action without demanding a particular result. Notice whether those habits help you deal with your life. If practicing Stoicism leaves you ashamed of needing comfort or support, something has gone wrong with the way you’re using it.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair, especially when modern Stoic advice treats every problem as a matter of personal attitude. If someone is being mistreated at work, “you control your response” may help them get through the day. It becomes bad advice if it discourages them from documenting the abuse, finding allies, or leaving.

The phrase “outside your control” causes some confusion. Most things worth doing involve outcomes you can’t control: asking for a raise, challenging an unfair rule, raising a child. You still have influence, and acting with other people can increase it. Taken too literally, the control distinction would give you a reason to withdraw from almost everything.

Classical Stoicism has resources for resisting that withdrawal. It treats justice as a virtue and emphasizes duties toward other people. Some ancient Stoics opposed political rulers, sometimes at considerable personal cost. Accepting that an effort might fail is compatible with believing you have a duty to make it.

Still, I wouldn’t defend every problem in Stoicism by blaming watered-down social media versions. Its emphasis on keeping your character independent of circumstances can understate how deeply material conditions affect people. And advice directed at an individual often leaves collective action out of the picture. A workplace full of people managing their own frustration may stay a bad workplace.

A useful test is what the advice makes possible. Does becoming calmer help you confront your boss, support a colleague, or make a considered decision to leave? Or does it help you endure the same harm indefinitely while blaming yourself whenever it hurts?

There are situations people genuinely can’t change, at least for now, and getting through them with less suffering matters. Nobody should have to fight constantly to prove they haven’t surrendered. But advice about acceptance deserves scrutiny when it comes from someone who benefits from your staying put.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something right about how much energy we spend fighting things we can’t control. Learning to question our assumptions and choose our responses can make everyday life less overwhelming. But its emphasis on acceptance can become an excuse to tolerate conditions we could change, especially together. A bad workplace or an unjust policy deserves more than a calmer attitude. Stoicism is useful when it helps us act thoughtfully under pressure; it falls short when it treats distress as a personal failure or overlooks the circumstances causing it.

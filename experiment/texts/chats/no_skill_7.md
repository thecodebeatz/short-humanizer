## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A lot of it is that **Stoicism offers a simple, practical response to a very modern feeling: “Too much is happening, and I can’t control most of it.”** That’s a compelling pitch in an era of constant news, economic uncertainty, and social-media comparison.

A few forces have helped it spread:

- **It fits an anxious, unpredictable period.** The pandemic, political conflict, and worries about work and money made its central distinction—what’s up to you versus what isn’t—especially appealing. It gives people something to do without promising that the world will become fair or predictable.

- **It’s easy to package online.** A line from Marcus Aurelius can work as a quote card, a podcast topic, or a thirty-second video. The underlying philosophy is complex, but its practical advice is unusually portable: pause before reacting, examine your judgments, remember that life is finite.

- **A publishing and podcast ecosystem has built up around it.** Writers such as Ryan Holiday and William B. Irvine, along with entrepreneurs, athletes, and other public figures, have introduced Stoic ideas to audiences who wouldn’t normally pick up ancient philosophy. Their popularity then feeds more books, newsletters, and content.

- **It overlaps with familiar mental-health tools.** Stoicism’s emphasis on how our interpretations shape our distress resembles parts of cognitive behavioral therapy, which was influenced in part by Stoic thought. That makes it feel practical and psychologically credible—though philosophy isn’t a substitute for treatment.

- **It offers a framework without requiring religious belief.** Modern versions can supply daily practices, moral guidance, and a way to think about suffering and death without requiring someone to join a religion. Ancient Stoicism had a much richer theological and metaphysical framework than popular versions usually acknowledge.

- **It meshes with self-improvement culture.** Discipline, resilience, and less dependence on other people’s approval appeal to people interested in productivity, fitness, or leadership. In some male-oriented online spaces, it also provides a vocabulary for handling distress that feels more acceptable than explicitly therapeutic language.

There’s a catch, though: **popular “Stoicism” sometimes means something quite different from the ancient philosophy.** It can get reduced to “suppress your feelings, work harder, and tolerate everything.” The ancient Stoics were concerned with wisdom, justice, and our obligations to other people—not just personal toughness. Accepting that you can’t guarantee an outcome doesn’t mean you shouldn’t try to change it.

And some of the “everywhere” feeling may be algorithmic: once you engage with a few Stoic posts, platforms have an enormous supply of adjacent material to show you.

So it’s both a genuine revival and a particularly successful form of internet self-help: **ancient authority, actionable advice, and a message well suited to uncertain times.**

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It’s a serious philosophy that can be useful in ordinary life—and it’s also become a self-help trend.** The useful parts aren’t invalidated by the marketing, but the marketing often leaves out what makes it a philosophy rather than a collection of motivational quotes.

For everyday life, a few Stoic habits hold up well:

- **Separate your choices from the outcome.** You can prepare for a job interview, but you can’t make someone hire you. That distinction can help you put effort where it matters without treating every disappointment as a personal failure.
- **Question your first interpretation.** “My friend hasn’t replied” is a fact; “they don’t respect me” is an interpretation. Giving yourself space between those two can prevent a lot of unnecessary distress.
- **Decide what matters before you’re under pressure.** For the Stoics, being fair, honest, and sensible matters more than looking successful. That’s a useful counterweight to status anxiety.

None of that requires being unusually tough, wealthy, or free of everyday responsibilities. And it doesn’t require becoming emotionally blank: **feeling grief or anger isn’t a failure of character.** The practical aim is to avoid letting your first emotional reaction dictate everything you do.

Where I’d be more cautious is with the stronger claims of classical Stoicism. The Stoics held that virtue is the only true good, and that things such as health, money, and reputation don’t determine whether you live a good life. That can be liberating—but it’s also a demanding, debatable position, not an established psychological fact. Health, safety, relationships, and material circumstances profoundly affect people’s lives; “change your attitude” can become cruel advice when it ignores that.

There’s also a real risk of turning acceptance into passivity. **“I can’t control this” shouldn’t become “I have no influence or responsibility here.”** You can’t control whether your workplace becomes fairer, for example, but you can still organize, complain, support colleagues, or leave. Stoic concern for justice supports action, not merely enduring mistreatment.

My take: **Stoicism is a good practical toolkit and a worthwhile philosophy to think with, but you don’t have to adopt it as a complete worldview.** Pair its emphasis on judgment and character with room for emotional support, pleasure, attachment, and efforts to change bad circumstances.

A good test is what it does to you: if it makes you steadier, kinder, and more willing to act, it’s probably helping. If it makes you ashamed of needing people, dismissive of suffering, or proud of tolerating mistreatment, you’re getting a distorted—or at least unhelpful—version.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **fair about some modern Stoic messaging, but not the whole philosophy.** The key question is: *What are you being asked to accept?*

There’s a crucial difference between:
- **Accepting reality:** “This unfair situation exists, and I can’t guarantee I’ll change it.”
- **Accepting its legitimacy:** “This situation is fine, or I ought to put up with it.”

The first can help you act effectively. It doesn’t imply the second. You can acknowledge that your boss is mistreating you while documenting it, supporting coworkers, looking for another job, or pursuing a complaint. You don’t have to believe success is guaranteed to think action is worthwhile.

Classical Stoicism has resources for that distinction. Justice and duties to other people were central to it; personal calm wasn’t the sole objective. **Sometimes doing the right thing means entering a conflict, not making yourself comfortable with it.** Still, ancient Stoicism wasn’t a modern movement for social equality, and its emphasis on inner freedom can leave too little emphasis on changing external conditions. It shouldn’t get a free pass just because its popularizers simplify it.

The criticism becomes especially persuasive when Stoicism is promoted by someone who benefits from your endurance. “Don’t let things outside your control upset you” means something different when you choose it as a coping practice than when management says it instead of fixing unsafe conditions. A useful personal discipline can become a convenient excuse for institutional inaction.

I’d also revise the popular “control versus no control” framing. Most important things fall into three categories:

- What you can decide directly.
- What you can influence, especially with other people.
- What you can’t meaningfully affect.

Putting everything outside your direct control into the third category is a recipe for passivity. Voting, organizing, parenting, and maintaining relationships all involve acting without controlling the result.

So my view is: **Stoicism is helpful when it reduces helpless rumination and frees you to act; it’s harmful when it teaches you to reinterpret avoidable harm as merely an attitude problem.** Being less consumed by an injustice and working to end it are perfectly compatible.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control every outcome, but we can take responsibility for our judgments and actions. That can make us steadier in uncertainty and less dependent on other people’s approval. Where it can go wrong—especially in its modern self-help form—is treating suffering as an attitude problem and acceptance as a reason to tolerate conditions we could change. Its emphasis on inner freedom is valuable, but incomplete without attention to relationships, material circumstances, and collective action. The best Stoicism helps us face reality and act with courage and justice; the worst teaches us to endure what we should challenge.

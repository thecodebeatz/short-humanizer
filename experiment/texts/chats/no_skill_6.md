## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A lot of it is that **Stoicism offers a simple, practical response to a very modern feeling: “Everything is chaotic, and I don’t have much control.”** That makes it especially appealing right now—even though the philosophy itself is ancient.

A few forces have come together:

- **It fits an age of anxiety and instability.** The pandemic, economic uncertainty, political conflict, and constant bad news make the Stoic distinction between what you can and can’t control feel immediately useful. You don’t need to adopt an entire worldview to get something out of it.

- **It’s almost perfectly suited to social media.** Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus wrote ideas that can be presented as short, memorable quotations. “You can’t control events, only your response” travels much more easily than a complicated philosophical argument. Once you engage with a few posts, recommendation algorithms can make it feel genuinely ubiquitous.

- **Modern authors and creators have built a big ecosystem around it.** Ryan Holiday’s books and *The Daily Stoic*, along with podcasts, newsletters, and productivity channels, have made Stoicism accessible and marketable. There’s now a clear entry point—and a steady stream of content—for people who wouldn’t otherwise read ancient philosophy.

- **It bridges self-help and intellectual respectability.** Advice like “don’t obsess over other people’s opinions” sounds more substantial when it comes from a Roman emperor than from a motivational influencer. The ancient pedigree gives familiar advice a sense of depth and durability.

- **It appeals to achievement culture.** Athletes, entrepreneurs, military figures, and productivity enthusiasts can use Stoic practices to frame setbacks as training and discipline as freedom. That can be genuinely helpful, though it can also turn the philosophy into “how to tolerate more pressure and keep performing.”

- **It offers some people a language for emotional difficulty that doesn’t feel exposing.** This is part of its appeal in male-oriented online spaces: talking about resilience, judgment, and self-command can feel more approachable than talking about vulnerability. But Stoicism’s audience is broader than that.

There’s also a crucial distinction between **ancient Stoicism and the internet version**. The ancient philosophy wasn’t simply “be tough, feel nothing, get rich.” It treated virtue as the central good and emphasized justice, responsibility to others, and our shared humanity. It aimed to examine and reshape destructive judgments—not just suppress emotions.

So I’d say its popularity comes from a combination of **real usefulness, a strong publishing-and-influencer ecosystem, and excellent compatibility with the internet**. And some of what looks like a Stoic revival is older self-help advice repackaged in Roman clothing.

The interesting tension is that it can help you stop wasting energy on what you can’t control—but, oversimplified, it can also encourage you to accept situations you actually could help change.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It’s a serious philosophy with useful ideas for everyday life—and a self-help trend that often strips those ideas down.** Those aren’t mutually exclusive.

You don’t need to be an emperor, entrepreneur, or endurance athlete to benefit from it. Some of its best applications are ordinary: handling criticism, sitting in traffic, worrying about a medical result, or dealing with a difficult colleague.

### What’s genuinely good about it

- **It helps separate action from rumination.** “What can I do here?” is often a better question than “How do I make sure nothing bad happens?” You can prepare for an interview without being able to guarantee the job.
- **It puts some distance between a feeling and a reaction.** Feeling insulted doesn’t mean you have to send the angry message. The useful move is examining your interpretation, not pretending you aren’t hurt.
- **It makes your standards less dependent on applause.** Trying to be honest, fair, and courageous gives you a more stable measure of success than reputation, income, or winning every argument.
- **It takes ordinary responsibilities seriously.** At its best, it’s about being a decent friend, parent, neighbor, or coworker—not becoming an invulnerable lone wolf.

Some Stoic practices also resemble techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy, especially examining the judgments that intensify distress. That doesn’t make Stoicism a substitute for therapy, but the overlap helps explain its practical appeal.

### Where it can fall short

The biggest issue isn’t just bad internet interpretations. **Classical Stoicism itself makes demanding claims that you don’t have to accept.** It holds that virtue is the only true good, while things like health, money, and reputation are “indifferents”—though some are naturally worth preferring. That can help protect your dignity during hardship, but it’s reasonable to think that illness, poverty, and bereavement affect a good life in ways that aren’t reducible to your judgments.

And its everyday advice has predictable failure modes:

- “Accept what you can’t control” can become **giving up on things you can influence**.
- “Examine your judgments” can become **blaming yourself for being distressed**.
- “Don’t depend on approval” can become **dismissing useful feedback or emotional needs**.
- “Practice endurance” can become **staying in an unhealthy job or relationship**.

Those aren’t inevitable outcomes, but they’re worth watching for—especially if you already tend to minimize your own needs.

**My take: Stoicism is a good resource for normal people, but it needn’t be your entire operating system.** Use it to become less reactive and more principled, not less emotionally available. Pair self-command with affection, pleasure, mutual support, and efforts to improve your circumstances.

A useful test is: **Does practicing it make you more capable of caring and acting—or just better at not complaining?** The first is a real benefit. The second may be self-help branding dressed up as wisdom.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism identifies a real risk—especially in the productivity-oriented version of Stoicism—but it’s not the whole story. **Accepting that a situation exists is different from accepting that it should continue.** Good Stoic advice helps you make that distinction; bad Stoic advice blurs it.

Take an unfair workplace. There are at least two ways to apply “focus on what you can control”:

- **Passive version:** “I can’t control my boss, so I should change my attitude and endure it.”
- **Active version:** “I can’t guarantee my boss will change, but I can document what’s happening, set boundaries, organize with coworkers, make a complaint, or look for another job.”

Both acknowledge limits. Only the second takes your agency seriously. And it recognizes that **you can influence things you don’t fully control**. That middle category gets lost in a lot of online Stoicism.

The criticism gets stronger when you ask who’s delivering the advice. If you choose a Stoic practice to help yourself through hardship, that can be empowering. If an employer tells overworked staff to build resilience rather than fixing working conditions, the same language can deflect responsibility. The advice may reduce distress while leaving its cause untouched.

Ancient Stoicism offers some resistance to that misuse: justice and responsibility to other people were central virtues, not optional extras. If a situation is unjust, responding well may require challenging it—even when success is uncertain. But I wouldn’t completely let the philosophy off the hook. Its emphasis on inner freedom and treating external circumstances as secondary can make endurance seem more important than material change. That tension is real, not merely an influencer’s misunderstanding.

A useful way to judge a piece of Stoic advice is to ask:

- **Does it help me see my options, or make me dismiss them?**
- **Does it acknowledge the harm, or imply that my distress is the real problem?**
- **Does it support collective action, or assume everything must be solved inside my own head?**

So my view is: **Stoicism is valuable when it helps you act without needing a guarantee of success. It becomes limiting when it teaches you not to want change.** Sometimes endurance is necessary, especially when your options are constrained. But endurance isn’t proof that a situation is acceptable—and leaving or resisting can be just as courageous as staying.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control every outcome, but we can take responsibility for how we act. It offers useful tools for handling setbacks, questioning snap judgments, and choosing principle over approval. Where it goes wrong—or where its modern promoters often take it wrong—is in treating resilience as a substitute for change. Unfair conditions aren’t merely tests of attitude, and distress isn’t always a failure of perspective. At its best, Stoicism helps us face reality and act with courage; at its worst, it teaches us to endure what we should be trying to change.

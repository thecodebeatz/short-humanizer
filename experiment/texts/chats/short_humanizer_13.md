## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism offers a simple, appealing way to deal with feeling overwhelmed: focus on what you can control, accept what you can't, and pay attention to how you act. That travels well in a period of political conflict, economic uncertainty, and constant exposure to other people's lives.

A few things have helped it spread:

- It's easy to turn into internet content. A short passage from Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus can work as a quote, a video, or a daily reminder. You can get something useful from it without first studying a whole philosophical system.

- Modern authors have made it accessible and commercially successful. Ryan Holiday, in particular, has helped make Stoicism a recognizable category of self-help through books, newsletters, and The Daily Stoic. Podcasts and social media give those ideas repeated exposure.

- It fits productivity and fitness culture. Discipline, persistence, and tolerating discomfort already matter in those spaces. Stoicism gives those habits a philosophical vocabulary and a connection to ancient figures.

- It offers guidance without requiring religious belief. Some people want a framework for how to live but don't belong to a religious community. Modern presentations of Stoicism offer exercises and ethical advice that people can adopt selectively, even though ancient Stoicism included substantial religious and metaphysical commitments.

- It appeals to some men who find conventional self-help uncomfortable. Language about composure, duty, and self-command can make reflection feel approachable. Some online versions also lean into masculine toughness and emotional suppression, which gives a distorted picture of the philosophy.

The pandemic likely strengthened its appeal: people were dealing with loss, disrupted plans, and unusually obvious limits on their control. Its overlap with ideas used in cognitive behavioral therapy also makes some Stoic exercises feel familiar and practical.

As for why it feels like it's everywhere, recommendation systems can amplify that impression. Once you watch a few clips about discipline or Marcus Aurelius, platforms have plenty of similar material to show you. It's hard to separate a genuine rise in interest from the concentrated visibility of a very successful self-help niche. Buying a book of Stoic quotations, watching productivity videos, and seriously practicing Stoic ethics can all get counted as the same trend.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

It's a useful philosophy for everyday life, and its current popularity includes a lot of self-help packaging. You can benefit from its practices without accepting its whole account of how people should live.

The strongest parts are practical:

- Distinguishing what you can influence from what you can't. This helps with everyday frustrations, such as waiting for a decision or dealing with someone else's opinion of you. Your choices matter even when you can't guarantee the result.
- Examining your judgments. "That person didn't reply" is an observation. "They don't respect me" is an interpretation you can question. That pause can prevent a lot of unnecessary distress.
- Taking your conduct seriously. Stoicism asks whether you acted fairly and responsibly, including when doing so costs you something. Ancient Stoics treated obligations to other people as part of a good life.

Its limits matter too. Classical Stoicism makes a demanding claim: virtue is the only true good, while health, wealth, and even relationships aren't what ultimately make a life good. Stoics still valued those things, but many people reasonably think that losing someone they love damages their well-being in a way that good character cannot fully answer.

Popular advice to "control your reaction" can also become harsh. Anxiety, grief, and trauma don't simply yield to a decision. Treating every painful feeling as a failure of judgment can add shame to suffering. Likewise, accepting what you can't control requires care: you may have little power over an unfair situation alone but meaningful power alongside other people.

For most people, I'd recommend borrowing its exercises before adopting its strongest claims. Use it to question an angry interpretation or prepare for a difficult conversation. Be cautious with any version that tells you to suppress grief, tolerate mistreatment, or blame yourself for struggling.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair about some modern Stoic advice. "Focus on what you can control" can become a reason to endure an unfair workplace or stop paying attention to politics. If every problem becomes a question of your attitude, the people with power escape scrutiny.

The useful distinction is between accepting the facts of a situation and accepting that it should continue. You can acknowledge that your employer has power over your livelihood while organizing with coworkers to change the conditions. You don't control the outcome, but you can help influence it. The language of "control" gets misleading when it makes anything short of certainty seem outside your responsibility.

Classical Stoicism has resources for that kind of action. It treats justice and duties to other people as central to a good life. A Stoic can have good reason to resist abuse or challenge an unjust policy, even when success looks unlikely. Its emphasis on acting well despite uncertain results can help people persist through setbacks.

Still, the concern goes deeper than social media oversimplification. A philosophy that locates your good chiefly in your own character can understate how much material conditions matter. Resilience helps someone cope with an unsafe workplace; changing those conditions requires action from workers, employers, or regulators. Advice that focuses entirely on the worker's response leaves those responsibilities out.

My test would be what the advice helps you do. If it helps you tolerate the fear and frustration of confronting a problem, it's useful. If it tells you that being upset about mistreatment is the real problem, I'd be wary of it.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets a lot right about everyday life: questioning our first reactions and accepting the limits of our influence can spare us unnecessary distress. Its emphasis on fairness and responsibility also gives us reasons to act when success is uncertain. But it can understate how deeply loss and material hardship affect our well-being. Modern versions sometimes make this worse by treating suffering as a failure of attitude, or urging people to adjust to conditions they could work together to change.

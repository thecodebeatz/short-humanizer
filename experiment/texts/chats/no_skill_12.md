## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A few trends have converged—and Stoicism happens to be unusually well suited to both modern anxieties and the way the internet spreads ideas.

**1. It offers a sense of agency when life feels unstable.**  
Its best-known idea—distinguish what you can control from what you can’t—is appealing amid political conflict, economic uncertainty, pandemic aftereffects, and nonstop bad news. It gives people something to *do* with their anxiety without promising that everything will work out.

**2. It’s practical without requiring religious belief.**  
You can pick up Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus and find advice about anger, setbacks, status, and death. For people who want a framework for living but don’t belong to a religious tradition, that can fill a real gap.

**3. It fits self-improvement culture extremely well.**  
Discipline, resilience, less dependence on other people’s approval: those themes travel easily through fitness, entrepreneurship, sports, and productivity communities. Writers and podcasters—especially Ryan Holiday and *The Daily Stoic*—have helped turn an ancient philosophy into an accessible daily practice and a recognizable brand.

**4. It’s almost perfectly formatted for social media.**  
A short passage from an emperor about ignoring insults makes a great quote card. Add a marble bust and dramatic music, and you’ve got a highly shareable video. The catch is that the most portable bits often lose the surrounding argument.

**5. Some of its ideas resemble familiar psychological tools.**  
Stoic attention to how judgments shape emotions has genuine connections to cognitive behavioral therapy, whose developers drew partly on Stoic thought. That makes it feel compatible with contemporary approaches to coping—though reading Stoic quotes isn’t equivalent to therapy.

There’s also a reason it may feel *especially* ubiquitous: once you engage with a few philosophy or self-improvement posts, recommendation systems can serve you a lot more of them.

The important wrinkle is that **popular “internet Stoicism” isn’t always much like the full philosophy**. It can become “suppress your feelings, work harder, never complain.” Ancient Stoicism was also deeply concerned with justice, ethical character, and obligations to other people—not merely becoming unbothered or successful.

So the short version is: it offers an old, credible language for dealing with very current feelings of uncertainty—and it’s easy to package, market, and share. That doesn’t make its appeal fake, but it does help explain why it seems to be everywhere.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It can be a good philosophy for ordinary life—and the self-help industry also sells a simplified version of it.** Those aren’t mutually exclusive.

What makes Stoicism genuinely useful is that it asks you to practice things that matter on an unremarkable Tuesday:
- **Separate your effort from the outcome.** Prepare well for a job interview without treating rejection as proof you’re worthless.
- **Examine your first interpretation.** “My friend hasn’t replied” doesn’t necessarily mean “my friend doesn’t care about me.”
- **Take responsibility for your conduct.** You can’t make a difficult colleague reasonable, but you can decide how to respond—and whether to set a boundary.
- **Keep status and possessions in perspective.** They can be worth pursuing without becoming the measure of your life.

That’s more substantial than motivational quotes. Classical Stoicism also makes ethical character—being just, courageous, wise, and temperate—the point of life, rather than productivity or looking invulnerable.

**Where I’d be cautious is taking its strongest claims as the whole truth.** Strict Stoicism says that virtue is the only genuine good, and things like health, money, and reputation aren’t what make a life good or bad, even though some are reasonably preferred. That can help loosen their grip on you, but it’s a demanding position. Illness, poverty, abuse, and grief are not simply attitude problems, and applying Stoic slogans carelessly can minimize real suffering.

It’s also easy to mistake:
- accepting reality for tolerating mistreatment;
- examining emotions for suppressing them;
- personal responsibility for blaming yourself for everything.

Those aren’t fair summaries of the tradition, but they are common ways its advice can go wrong. Accepting that something has happened doesn’t mean accepting that it should continue.

My take: **Stoicism is a useful toolkit and a serious ethical philosophy, but it needn’t be your entire worldview.** You can learn its steadiness while still valuing vulnerability, pleasure, close relationships, and efforts to change unfair circumstances.

A good test is whether practicing it makes you **more thoughtful, kinder, and better able to act**—or merely better at telling yourself and others not to feel things. The former is worth keeping; the latter is usually the branding, not the wisdom.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **fair about some modern Stoic advice, but too broad as a verdict on Stoicism itself.** The key is what you’re being asked to accept.

There’s a difference between:
- **Accepting the facts:** “My workplace is exploitative. I can’t undo what’s already happened or guarantee how my boss will respond.”
- **Accepting the arrangement:** “Therefore I should stop objecting and learn not to mind.”

The first can help you act clearly. The second doesn’t follow from it.

At its best, Stoicism says: don’t let anger, fear, or wishful thinking dictate your response; work out what justice requires and act, even when success isn’t assured. **You don’t control whether a protest succeeds, but you can still have good reasons to protest.** Not controlling an outcome isn’t the same as having no influence over it—or no responsibility to try.

But the criticism identifies a real problem in how Stoicism gets marketed. If every problem becomes an opportunity to improve your mindset, attention shifts away from conditions that need changing. Advice to “manage your reaction” can help an employee get through a difficult day; it becomes evasive if it’s the employer’s answer to unsafe working conditions. The same advice can support someone’s agency or excuse someone else’s failure to act.

And I wouldn’t let the ancient philosophy entirely off the hook by blaming everything on internet influencers. Its strong emphasis on inner freedom can understate how much people’s ability to live well depends on material security, relationships, and institutions. Its emphasis on justice and our duties to others pushes against that tendency, but the tension is real.

So my test would be: **does this practice help you face the situation and choose a response, or teach you to stop seeing the situation as a problem?**

Sometimes a wise response is endurance, especially when someone lacks safe alternatives. Sometimes it’s leaving, setting a boundary, organizing with others, or resisting. A useful Stoicism should help you distinguish those options—not prescribe endurance in advance.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control everything that happens to us, but we can practice responding with clarity, courage, and care. Its mistake—or at least the mistake of many modern versions—is to treat inner resilience as a sufficient answer to external problems. An exploitative workplace or an unjust society needs more than a better mindset. At its best, Stoicism helps us face reality without being overwhelmed by it; at its worst, it makes resignation look like wisdom. The goal should be steadiness that helps us act, not calm that teaches us to put up with anything.

## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits several things people are looking for right now: a way to handle uncertainty, advice that feels practical, and some relief from the pressure to react to everything. Social media has also made it unusually easy to package and sell.

A few reasons it seems to be everywhere:

- **Its central idea is immediately useful.** You can’t fully control your job prospects, other people’s opinions, or world events. You can work on your judgments and actions. That distinction has obvious appeal when the news is exhausting or your own life feels unstable. The pandemic likely gave it extra traction.

- **It travels well online.** A sentence from Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus can fit on a quote card or a short video. You don’t need to read a whole philosophical argument to get something out of it. Though once those sentences are detached from their context, almost any advice about discipline can get called “Stoic.”

- **There’s a substantial publishing and marketing industry behind it.** Writers such as Ryan Holiday, along with podcasts, newsletters, and accounts like Daily Stoic, have brought the philosophy to a much wider audience. Books lead people to videos, which lead them to more books. Once you engage with a little of that content, recommendation feeds can make it feel ubiquitous.

- **It fits the self-improvement world.** Athletes, entrepreneurs, and productivity creators can use Stoic ideas to talk about persistence, setbacks, and working under pressure. The ancient pedigree also gives familiar advice a certain authority: “accept what you can’t change” sounds different when it comes from a Roman emperor.

There’s a particular appeal for some men, too. Stoicism offers a language for dealing with distress that feels compatible with strength and self-reliance. That can be a useful entry into thinking seriously about emotions. Some online versions, though, turn it into “never show weakness,” which is a pretty narrow reading of the philosophy.

The older tradition asks much more of people than the motivational version usually does. It treats virtue as the basis of a good life, takes duties toward other people seriously, and examines how our judgments produce anger, fear, and desire. That’s more demanding than staying calm so you can get more done.

So your impression probably reflects both a real revival and the way feeds amplify a recognizable style of advice. Its popularity makes sense: it gives people something they can practice when much of life feels beyond their influence. Whether that’s helpful depends a lot on which version they encounter.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

It can be a good philosophy for everyday life, though I’d be cautious about adopting the whole system. Some of its practices are useful even if you don’t accept its stronger claims.

The most useful habit is separating what you can influence from what you can’t. Before a job interview, you can prepare and listen carefully; you can’t guarantee an offer. Afterward, Stoicism gives you a reasonable way to stop treating the outcome as a verdict on your worth. It also encourages you to pause before accepting your first interpretation: “My friend hasn’t replied” doesn’t automatically mean “My friend doesn’t care about me.”

Those are workable habits for ordinary problems. Related ideas appear in cognitive behavioral therapy, whose developers drew partly on Stoicism. That connection doesn’t establish that the entire ancient philosophy is correct, but it helps explain why some of its exercises feel practical.

The harder part is its claim that virtue is the only true good. Strictly speaking, health, money, and even the survival of people you love don’t determine whether your life is good, although Stoics say you should reasonably prefer and care for those things. That’s a demanding position. You can admire its resilience while still believing that illness, poverty, and bereavement genuinely make a life worse.

There are also risks in how people apply it:

- Acceptance can become an excuse to tolerate a bad situation. You may not control your employer, but you can still negotiate, organize, or leave if you have the means.
- Examining your emotions can turn into policing them. Grief and anger deserve attention; feeling them isn’t evidence that you’ve failed.
- Self-discipline can become self-blame when someone overlooks the constraints of illness, money, or caregiving.

Ancient Stoics did take social duties seriously, so passivity isn’t an inevitable result of the philosophy. Still, the risk matters, especially when online advice reduces every problem to your attitude.

My view is that Stoicism works well as one influence on a life. Use it to handle disappointment and act more thoughtfully. Keep room for dependence on others, pleasure, and the recognition that some circumstances really are unjust or awful. You don’t need to become invulnerable to benefit from practicing steadiness.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair, especially when Stoicism is sold as advice for the workplace. “You can’t control what happens, only your response” can help someone get through a rough day. From a boss responding to unreasonable workloads, the same advice can become a way of avoiding responsibility.

The distinction between what you control and what you don’t also gets drawn too sharply. You don’t control whether your employer changes a policy, but you might influence it by raising the issue or organizing with coworkers. Most attempts to improve things involve acting without control over the outcome. If “outside your control” becomes “not worth trying to change,” the advice discourages useful action.

There are resources within Stoicism for resisting that reading. Ancient Stoics treated justice and duties toward others as central to a good life. Accepting that something has happened doesn’t require approving of it or allowing it to continue. You can acknowledge that you’ve been treated unfairly, decide how to respond, and pursue a change while recognizing that you might lose.

Still, pointing to the ancient texts doesn’t entirely answer the criticism of modern Stoicism. A version focused almost exclusively on personal resilience can leave people carrying burdens that an employer, institution, or government ought to address. Coping skills help people survive those conditions; they can also make the conditions easier for everyone else to ignore.

I’d judge the advice by what it helps you do. Does it help you recover enough composure to set a boundary, ask for help, or challenge something? Or does it leave you feeling that being upset about mistreatment is your main problem? In that second case, I think the criticism lands. Anger can distort your judgment, but it can also alert you to something that needs changing.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets a lot right about handling disappointment: our first reaction isn’t always our best judgment, and worrying about outcomes we can’t control can wear us out. But its emphasis on managing our responses can become an excuse to tolerate conditions we should try to change. Bad working conditions, poverty, and unfair treatment won’t disappear with a better attitude. I find Stoicism most useful when it helps me stay calm enough to act; I’m less convinced when it treats distress as a failure of perspective.

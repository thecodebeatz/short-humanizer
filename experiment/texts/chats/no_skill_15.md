## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Because Stoicism is unusually well suited to both **the anxieties of modern life and the way advice spreads online**. It offers a reassuring message—*you can’t control everything, but you can work on how you respond*—in a format that fits perfectly into a podcast, a daily email, or an Instagram quote.

A few things have come together:

- **It gives people a sense of agency amid uncertainty.** Political conflict, economic insecurity, the pandemic, and constant bad news make the distinction between what you can and can’t control feel immediately useful. You don’t have to believe the world is going well to find a way to live in it.

- **It’s practical and relatively easy to enter.** You can read a page of Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus and find something to try that day. There’s no requirement to join an institution or adopt a supernatural worldview, which appeals to people seeking guidance outside religion.

- **Modern promoters built a very effective ecosystem around it.** Writers such as Ryan Holiday and William B. Irvine helped turn ancient philosophy into accessible books, newsletters, podcasts, and daily practices. Entrepreneurs, athletes, and productivity influencers then amplified it.

- **It overlaps with familiar therapeutic ideas.** Examining your judgments, noticing what’s within your control, and pausing before reacting resemble techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy—which was itself partly influenced by Stoic thought. That makes Stoicism feel both ancient and psychologically current. It isn’t, however, a substitute for mental-health care.

- **It offers an appealing language for resilience, especially in some male-oriented spaces.** Talking about discipline, adversity, and self-command can make emotional reflection approachable to people who might resist other self-help language. The downside is that some versions turn it into “don’t feel anything,” which badly misrepresents the philosophy.

- **It’s extremely easy to package.** A striking quotation from a Roman emperor has built-in authority and shareability. And once you engage with a few Stoicism posts, recommendation algorithms can make it feel like the entire internet has discovered Marcus Aurelius.

There’s also a tension beneath the popularity: **ancient Stoicism was about becoming a good person, not merely an unbothered or successful one.** Justice, responsibility to others, and acting ethically were central. The online version sometimes keeps the emotional-control techniques and drops the moral obligations.

So I’d call the boom a mixture of genuine need, genuinely useful ideas, and very effective branding. Stoicism promises a foothold in a world that feels hard to influence—and that’s a powerful offer.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It’s a good practical philosophy for ordinary life—but not a complete answer to every problem, and its online version often oversells it.** The self-help trend is built around something substantial.

Where Stoicism earns its keep is in everyday situations: being criticized, getting stuck in traffic, facing rejection, worrying about an uncertain outcome. It encourages you to ask:

- What’s actually happening, and what am I adding through interpretation?
- What can I do here, even if I can’t guarantee the result?
- What would be fair or courageous, rather than merely satisfying right now?

Those are useful questions whether or not you identify as a Stoic. And the original tradition wasn’t just for powerful men: Epictetus had been enslaved, while Marcus Aurelius was an emperor. Its appeal was partly that circumstances don’t have to determine your character.

**The trouble starts when useful principles become absolute rules.**

“Focus on what you control” can help you stop ruminating—but it can also become an excuse not to challenge an unfair workplace or seek help. You may not control an outcome, but you can still influence it, especially with other people.

“Examine your judgments” can create breathing room—but not every painful feeling is a reasoning error. Grief, fear, anger, and attachment can tell you something important. Stoicism is not simply emotional suppression, though its strict ancient ideal of freedom from certain passions is more demanding than modern promoters sometimes admit.

And the claim that virtue alone is enough for a good life is genuinely controversial. Health, safety, relationships, and material security matter enormously to most people. Stoics don’t generally say you shouldn’t pursue those things, but their insistence that they aren’t necessary for flourishing can be hard to accept—and easy to weaponize against someone who’s suffering.

My recommendation would be: **use Stoicism as a practice, not a test of whether you’re handling life correctly.** Keep the reflection, perspective, ethical responsibility, and willingness to act without guaranteed success. Be wary of anything that teaches you to tolerate mistreatment, feel ashamed of needing others, or turn every hardship into a productivity exercise.

A good sign it’s helping: you become less reactive **and more engaged with people**. A bad sign: you become emotionally shut down and call that wisdom.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **fair about some modern Stoicism, but not the whole philosophy**. The key distinction is between accepting that something is happening and accepting that it ought to continue.

If your workplace is exploitative, for example, you might use Stoic ideas in two very different ways:

- **Resignation:** “Other people’s decisions aren’t under my control, so I should stop being upset and put up with this.”
- **Clear-headed resistance:** “I can’t guarantee that management will change. But I can document what’s happening, support coworkers, organize, or look for another job.”

The second is compatible with Stoicism. Not controlling the outcome doesn’t mean you have no influence—or no responsibility to act. Ancient Stoicism treated justice and duties toward others as central virtues, not optional extras.

But that doesn’t entirely defeat the criticism. **A philosophy can encourage action in principle while being taught in ways that encourage passivity.** When modern Stoicism focuses almost exclusively on managing your reactions, it can quietly turn a social problem into a personal adjustment problem. “Why are these conditions harmful?” becomes “Why are you letting these conditions bother you?” That’s especially troubling when the advice comes from someone who benefits from your endurance.

There’s also a genuine tension in the ancient philosophy: its emphasis on finding freedom through your own judgments can make external conditions seem less important than they really are. Inner resilience is valuable, but it isn’t a replacement for safety, resources, rights, or collective action.

So my view is: **accept reality as the starting point for action, not as a verdict that nothing should change.** Sometimes that means enduring something you genuinely can’t change; sometimes it means leaving, protesting, or asking for help. You don’t have to become perfectly calm before you’re entitled to resist injustice.

The test I’d use is: *Is this practice helping me act more effectively and ethically—or just helping me tolerate what someone else would prefer I tolerate?*

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control every outcome, but we can practice responding with greater clarity, courage, and fairness. That’s a useful antidote to rumination and impulsive reactions. Where it can go wrong—especially in its modern self-help form—is in treating suffering mainly as a problem of attitude, rather than something rooted in conditions that may need changing. Accepting reality needn’t mean accepting injustice, and resilience shouldn’t require emotional suppression. Stoicism is most valuable when it helps us engage with life more wisely, not merely endure it more quietly.

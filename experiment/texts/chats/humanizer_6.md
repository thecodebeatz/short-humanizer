## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A few things have come together: Stoicism gives people practical advice for dealing with uncertainty, and it packages unusually well for the internet.

The most appealing idea is simple: put your effort into what you can control, and try to loosen your grip on what you can't. When the news is exhausting, work feels insecure, and other people's lives are constantly on display, that advice has an obvious appeal. The pandemic likely gave it another push.

Several things help explain why it seems to be everywhere:

- **It fits social media.** A line from Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus can stand on its own in a post. You don't need to read a whole philosophical argument to feel you've gotten something useful from it. Unfortunately, this also makes misattributed quotes and oversimplifications easy to spread.
- **Popular writers built an audience around it.** Ryan Holiday's books and *The Daily Stoic*, along with podcasts and newsletters, have made Stoic ideas familiar to people who wouldn't normally browse ancient philosophy.
- **It slots into self-improvement culture.** Advice about discipline and handling setbacks appeals to athletes and entrepreneurs. It also fits men's self-help spaces, where the language of strength and composure can make talking about distress feel more acceptable.
- **It offers some of what people seek from religion.** You get daily practices and a framework for how to live, without having to join an institution or accept a particular modern religious identity. Ancient Stoicism did have religious and metaphysical commitments; popular versions often leave those out.

There's also a feedback loop: once you watch a few videos about Stoicism, recommendation systems can make it seem as though everyone has suddenly become a Roman emperor.

Some of the popularity depends on a selective reading, though. Ancient Stoics cared about virtue, justice, and obligations to other people. Online Stoicism often concentrates on personal composure and productivity, sometimes slipping into "don't feel anything" or "just tolerate your circumstances." That can be easier to sell, but it leaves out a lot of the philosophy.

So I think the appeal is genuine: people want a way to stay steady when life feels hard to influence. Its visibility also owes a lot to how easily that promise becomes a quote, a daily habit, or a product.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

It's a useful philosophy for everyday life, though I’d be cautious about adopting it as a complete answer to how to live. The self-help trend draws on genuinely helpful ideas, but it often narrows them to discipline and emotional control.

The most useful Stoic habit is separating what you can do from the outcome you want. You can prepare for a job interview; you can't guarantee an offer. That distinction can help you act without treating every disappointment as a personal failure. It’s also useful to pause before accepting your first interpretation of an event: "My friend hasn't replied" doesn't automatically mean "My friend doesn't care about me."

Those are ordinary skills worth practicing. Related ideas appear in cognitive behavioral therapy, although that doesn't mean the whole Stoic philosophy has been clinically validated.

The trouble comes when you push its claims further:

- Some things you can’t control still deserve your grief or anger. Losing someone, getting sick, or being mistreated can hurt even when you respond wisely. Stoicism allows for involuntary feelings, but its ideal of freedom from destructive passions is more demanding than the advice to pause before reacting.
- Control is rarely all-or-nothing. You can't single-handedly fix a bad workplace, but you might influence it with coworkers or leave it. A careless reading of "accept what you can't control" can make you give up too soon.
- Classical Stoicism makes a strong claim that virtue is the only true good. Health, money, and even the welfare of loved ones fall into a different category: things worth preferring, but not what makes a life good. You can understand the reasoning and still find that too austere.

For most people, I’d recommend borrowing its practices before committing to its worldview. Use it to interrupt a spiral of worry or help you behave decently under pressure. Be wary if it starts making you ashamed of needing comfort, asking for help, or caring deeply about an outcome.

A useful test is whether practicing it makes you more patient with other people as well as yourself. If it mainly makes you better at enduring avoidable misery, you may need to change your circumstances or seek support.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair about some modern Stoicism, especially when “focus on what you can control” turns into “stop complaining about things you can’t fix yourself.”

That advice can mistake limited power for no power. You might have little influence over your employer on your own, but more if you organize with coworkers. You can’t guarantee that a complaint, protest, or difficult conversation will succeed. You can still have good reasons to try. Uncertainty about the outcome doesn’t settle whether action is worthwhile.

Ancient Stoicism has resources for that objection. It treats justice as a virtue and takes duties to other people seriously. On those terms, accepting that an injustice has happened leaves you with the question of what you owe the people affected. A Stoic can work to change things while acknowledging that the effort may fail.

Still, the tension goes deeper than bad social media advice. If you hold that virtue is the only true good and that external circumstances can’t make you morally worse, there’s a risk of underplaying how much poverty, abuse, or discrimination can damage a person’s life. Stoics have answers to that, but the concern is reasonable.

I’d judge the advice partly by who’s giving it and what options it leaves open. Someone using Stoicism to get through a situation they can’t safely leave may find real relief. A boss telling exhausted employees to manage their reactions is shifting responsibility onto them. The same language can do very different work.

For personal use, I’d ask: “What can I influence, who could help, and what would trying cost?” That gives you more to work with than dividing everything into “under my control” and “outside my control.” Acceptance can help you see a situation clearly enough to act; it shouldn’t settle the decision to stay in it.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets a lot right about how easily we exhaust ourselves trying to control outcomes we can only influence. There’s value in pausing before reacting and accepting that even a good effort can fail. But its emphasis on inner discipline can underplay how much our circumstances matter. Grief isn’t a failure of perspective, and an unfair workplace may call for collective action. I find Stoicism most useful when it helps me stay steady enough to act, and least convincing when it makes enduring a bad situation sound like wisdom.

## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits several needs people have right now, and it’s unusually easy to package for the internet.

A lot of its appeal comes from feeling overwhelmed. When your attention is constantly pulled toward wars, politics, money worries, and other people’s opinions, “focus on what’s within your control” is a relief. It gives you somewhere to put your effort. The pandemic made that advice feel especially relevant, though the modern Stoic revival was already underway.

It’s also accessible as self-help. You can read a few lines from Marcus Aurelius and try applying them that afternoon. You don’t need to adopt a religion or work through a whole philosophical system first. Some Stoic practices, such as examining your judgments and separating events from your reactions, also overlap with techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy. That gives the philosophy a familiar, practical feel.

The internet rewards the parts that travel well. A short passage about handling setbacks works as a quote card, a podcast clip, or a caption under a marble bust. Writers and businesses, especially Ryan Holiday’s *The Daily Stoic*, have built a large audience around making those ideas accessible. Once you engage with a little of that material, recommendation feeds can make it feel ubiquitous.

There’s a particular appeal in male-oriented self-improvement spaces, too. Stoicism offers a vocabulary for discussing fear, disappointment, and emotional discipline that can feel comfortable to men who are wary of conventional self-help. Some of that is useful. Some turns into “never show emotion” or “become invulnerable,” which misrepresents the philosophy.

And Stoicism fits neatly into productivity culture: handle criticism, keep working, don’t let setbacks derail you. That helps explain its popularity, but it also makes it easy to sell a narrowed version focused on personal performance. The ancient Stoics cared deeply about justice, duties to others, and living virtuously. They weren’t simply teaching people to tolerate a stressful job more efficiently.

So I’d separate the appeal of the philosophy from the visibility of the brand. Its advice genuinely helps some people deal with uncertainty. The quote accounts, books, and algorithms then amplify the most marketable pieces, which are only part of what Stoicism has to say.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

I think it’s a good philosophy for everyday life, provided you’re selective about how you use it. The self-help industry has exaggerated it, but the appeal has substance.

Its most useful idea is to distinguish what you can influence from what you can’t. You can prepare for an interview; you can’t guarantee an offer. You can raise a problem with someone; you can’t choose their response. That distinction can help you act without making your peace of mind depend entirely on the outcome.

Stoicism also encourages you to examine your first interpretation of an event. “My friend hasn’t replied” is a fact. “They don’t care about me” is a judgment that might be wrong. Creating a little space between those two is useful in ordinary relationships.

Where I’d be cautious is its stricter claim that virtue is the only true good, while things like health, money, and reputation are “indifferents.” Ancient Stoics still preferred health to illness and thought people should fulfill their responsibilities. But it’s reasonable to think that losing your home or someone you love harms your life in ways that aren’t fully captured by asking whether you retained your good character.

There are also ways to misuse it:
- Treating grief or anger as evidence that you’ve failed.
- Calling something “outside my control” when you could still influence it, perhaps with other people.
- Using endurance to avoid leaving a bad situation or asking for help.

Those aren’t all fair readings of ancient Stoicism, but they’re foreseeable problems with how it’s taught online. You can’t simply choose not to feel distressed, and accepting that something has happened doesn’t settle what you should do about it.

For most people, I’d recommend borrowing its practices before committing to its whole worldview. Read Epictetus’s *Enchiridion* or some Marcus Aurelius, try applying the advice to a recurring frustration, and notice the results. If it helps you respond more thoughtfully, it’s earning its place. If you start shaming yourself for having feelings or putting up with avoidable harm, reconsider how you’re using it.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair about some modern Stoicism. Advice like “control your reaction” can help someone get through a bad day. When it becomes the entire response to an exploitative workplace or an abusive relationship, it can leave the source of the harm untouched and put all the responsibility on the person suffering.

The distinction between control and influence matters here. You can’t personally guarantee better working conditions, but you can document problems, support a colleague, look for another job, or organize with others. Outcomes that depend on other people still give you reasons to act. A rigid “outside my control” rule can obscure that.

Ancient Stoicism has resources for answering this criticism. It treats justice as a virtue and emphasizes duties to other people. A Stoic can oppose mistreatment while accepting that the effort might fail. Accepting the facts of a situation doesn’t require approving of them or agreeing to their continuation.

Still, I wouldn’t dismiss every objection by saying people have misunderstood the ancient texts. **The way a philosophy is promoted matters.** If a company teaches employees resilience while ignoring workloads it could reduce, it’s using Stoic ideas to shift responsibility. And an emphasis on individual judgment can underplay how much our choices depend on money, safety, and support.

My test would be: does this advice help someone see their options and act, or does it make them feel guilty for being hurt and reluctant to object? Sometimes enduring a situation is the safest available choice, especially while preparing to leave. That deserves respect. But Stoicism becomes limiting when endurance is treated as the right answer regardless of what could change.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control every outcome, and learning to question our first reactions can save us a lot of needless distress. But its emphasis on managing ourselves can become an excuse to tolerate conditions we should try to change. A bad workplace doesn’t become acceptable because you’ve learned to stay calm in it, and grief isn’t a failure of discipline. Stoicism is useful when it helps us act thoughtfully under pressure; it becomes limiting when we use it to measure how much suffering we can quietly endure.

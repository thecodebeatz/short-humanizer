## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism is unusually well suited to the mood of the last decade—and to the way ideas spread online. It offers a simple, appealing promise: **you can’t make the world predictable, but you can get better at handling it.**

A few things have helped it take off:

- **It speaks to a feeling of lost control.** Pandemic disruption, political conflict, economic insecurity, and constant bad news make “focus on what’s up to you” feel useful. It gives people something to do when larger problems seem beyond their reach.

- **It’s self-help with historical credibility.** Advice about managing anger or accepting setbacks can sound generic. Attribute it to Marcus Aurelius, an emperor writing to himself nearly 2,000 years ago, and it feels more substantial—and more interesting.

- **It fits social media extremely well.** Short, memorable passages about discipline, adversity, and mortality make excellent quote cards, podcasts, and short videos. The ideas that travel best aren’t necessarily the most representative, though: “stay disciplined” spreads more easily than a nuanced discussion of moral responsibility.

- **Popularizers have built an accessible ecosystem around it.** Writers such as Ryan Holiday, along with podcasts, newsletters, apps, and daily journals, have turned an ancient philosophy into a practical routine. You don’t have to study philosophy to start using it.

- **It overlaps with familiar mental-health tools.** Distinguishing an event from your judgment about it resembles techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy, whose early developers drew partly on Stoic ideas. That makes Stoicism feel practical rather than purely abstract—although it isn’t a substitute for treatment.

- **It appeals to achievement-oriented culture.** Entrepreneurs, athletes, and productivity influencers like its emphasis on discipline and resilience. Some male-oriented online communities also present it as a model of strength without emotional chaos. That has widened its audience, but sometimes reduced it to “work harder and don’t complain.”

There’s also a visibility loop: once you engage with a few Stoicism posts, recommendation systems can make it seem as though everyone is suddenly reading Seneca.

The important wrinkle is that **popular “stoicism” isn’t always the same as ancient Stoicism**. The ancient philosophy wasn’t just about staying calm or suppressing feelings. It put virtue—wisdom, justice, courage, and moderation—at the center, including duties to other people. You weren’t supposed to become better at enduring a bad world *instead of* trying to act justly within it.

So the popularity is partly a genuine response to uncertainty, partly effective packaging, and partly an internet-friendly simplification. Its strongest appeal is agency; its weakest version turns that into “whatever happens, just toughen up.”

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It’s a good philosophy for ordinary life, but a less convincing all-purpose answer to life.** The self-help trend draws on something genuinely useful; it also tends to strip away the parts that make Stoicism more than a resilience technique.

Where it earns its keep is in everyday situations:
- **Separating what you can do from what you can’t guarantee.** You can prepare for an interview, not ensure you get the job. That distinction helps you act without making your peace of mind depend entirely on the outcome.
- **Putting a pause between a feeling and a response.** “I’m angry” doesn’t have to mean “I should send this message.” That isn’t emotional suppression; it’s making room for judgment.
- **Keeping setbacks in proportion.** Embarrassment, criticism, and inconvenience can feel catastrophic without actually being so.
- **Asking what kind of person you’re becoming.** Ancient Stoicism cares about whether you act fairly and courageously, not just whether you stay productive and unbothered.

None of that requires being wealthy, powerful, or unusually disciplined. It can be useful while parenting, dealing with coworkers, caring for someone, or managing an uncertain future.

**The problems start when useful practices become absolute rules.** Classical Stoicism makes demanding claims—for instance, that virtue alone is sufficient for a good life, while health, money, and reputation aren’t genuinely good in the same sense. There’s a coherent argument behind that, but you don’t have to accept it. Material security, relationships, and health really do matter to people; treating distress over them as merely a mistake in judgment can become harsh.

There are also some common misuses:
- “Accept what you can’t control” becomes **put up with mistreatment**.
- “Examine your judgments” becomes **your suffering is your fault**.
- “Don’t be ruled by emotions” becomes **don’t feel or ask for help**.

Those aren’t fair summaries of the whole ancient philosophy, but they’re real risks in how it gets taught. A situation can be outside your complete control and still be worth resisting or changing with other people.

My take: **use Stoicism as a practice, not a test of whether you’re handling life correctly.** It can help you be steadier, less reactive, and more principled. It shouldn’t require you to be invulnerable or detached from the people you love.

A good test is: *Does this make me more able to face reality and act well—or just better at telling myself I shouldn’t be upset?* The first is valuable philosophy. The second is self-help pressure in a toga.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **partly right—especially about how Stoicism gets marketed—but acceptance and passivity aren’t the same thing.**

There are two very different messages that can hide inside “accept what you can’t control”:

- **Accept the facts:** “This is happening. I can’t undo it by wishing otherwise. What can I do now?”
- **Accept the arrangement:** “This is how things are, so I shouldn’t resist.”

The first can help people change a bad situation. The second can help keep them stuck in it.

Suppose your workplace is exploitative. A useful Stoic response might be: recognize the situation clearly, don’t let your boss’s approval define your worth, and decide whether to document problems, organize with coworkers, negotiate, or leave. A bad version is: “Your boss’s behavior is outside your control, so work on your attitude.” **Not having control over an outcome doesn’t mean having no influence—or no reason to act.**

Ancient Stoicism has resources for that distinction. Justice is a central virtue, and its emphasis on duties to others gives reasons to oppose mistreatment, not just endure it calmly. But it would be too convenient to say all the problems are modern distortions. Its strong emphasis on inner freedom can genuinely underplay how much social and material conditions matter. And ancient Stoics weren’t a uniformly radical or egalitarian group in the modern sense.

The modern packaging can sharpen that weakness. Advice about changing your mindset is easier to sell as a journal, course, or productivity habit than advice about changing institutions. That doesn’t make the advice worthless, but it can turn a public problem into an individual assignment: *learn to cope better*. A person may need coping skills **and** better working conditions, support, or collective action.

So my view is: **Stoicism is helpful when it makes your actions less dependent on guaranteed success; it’s harmful when it makes action seem unnecessary.** You can accept that you might lose and still fight for something worth changing. Calm isn’t the opposite of resistance, and anger isn’t automatically a failure of wisdom.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control everything that happens to us, but we can practice responding with clarity, courage, and fairness. That can make us less reactive and more willing to act without a guarantee of success. Where it goes wrong—especially in its modern self-help form—is when acceptance becomes resignation, or suffering gets treated as a failure of mindset rather than a response to real hardship. The best Stoicism helps us face reality and change what we can; the worst teaches us to tolerate what ought to change.

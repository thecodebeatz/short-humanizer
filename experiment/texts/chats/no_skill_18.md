## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A few trends have converged to make Stoicism especially well suited to the current moment—and to the internet.

**1. It offers a sense of agency amid uncertainty.**  
A central Stoic distinction is between what’s up to you—your judgments and choices—and what isn’t. When news, politics, work, and money feel unpredictable, that’s an appealing framework: *you don’t have to control everything to respond well.*

**2. It’s practical and easy to enter.**  
You can read a paragraph of Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus and find something to apply that day. Unlike philosophies that require a lot of background, Stoicism can feel immediately useful for dealing with criticism, disappointment, anxiety, or ambition.

**3. It fits the language of modern self-help.**  
Stoicism overlaps with familiar ideas about reframing thoughts, building habits, and questioning automatic reactions. There’s also a genuine historical connection: Stoic ideas influenced some founders of modern cognitive therapies. That makes it sound both ancient and psychologically contemporary—though reading Stoicism isn’t equivalent to getting therapy.

**4. A large publishing and social-media ecosystem promotes it.**  
Writers and entrepreneurs such as Ryan Holiday have helped turn Stoicism into an accessible modern genre: books, podcasts, newsletters, journals, and daily quotations. Its short, memorable sayings travel exceptionally well online. Once you engage with a few, recommendation algorithms can make it feel ubiquitous.

**5. It appeals to several different audiences.**  
Some people find an ethical philosophy; others find tools for resilience. Entrepreneurs and athletes often encounter it as a performance mindset. Parts of masculine self-improvement culture embrace its emphasis on discipline and endurance, sometimes presenting it as an alternative to more emotionally expressive forms of self-help.

**The catch is that popular “Stoicism” can be much thinner than the original philosophy.** Ancient Stoicism wasn’t just “stay calm, work hard, don’t complain.” It also emphasized justice, obligations to other people, and the idea that character matters more than wealth or status. Nor did it simply teach suppressing feelings or passively accepting mistreatment.

So the short answer is: **Stoicism supplies a portable framework for living with uncertainty, and modern media has packaged it extremely effectively.** Some of its popularity reflects a genuine philosophical revival; some reflects very old advice being sold as a productivity system.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It can be a good philosophy for ordinary life—and its popularity is also a self-help trend.** Those aren’t mutually exclusive. The useful question is whether you’re getting a thoughtful way to live or just slogans about being unbothered.

At its best, Stoicism gives you a few genuinely useful habits:

- **Separate effort from outcome.** You can prepare for an interview; you can’t guarantee an offer. That helps you act without making every result a verdict on your worth.
- **Question your first interpretation.** “My friend hasn’t replied” is a fact; “they don’t care about me” is an interpretation. Creating some space between the two can prevent unnecessary suffering.
- **Treat character as more important than applause.** Being fair, honest, and dependable is a steadier aim than always being admired or successful.
- **Remember your responsibilities to others.** Classical Stoicism is not just private toughness. It asks what you owe your family, neighbors, and wider community.

None of that requires an extraordinary life—or waking up at 4 a.m. and taking cold showers.

**But there are real limitations.** The strict ancient claim that virtue alone is enough for a good life, while things like health and material security are not genuinely good, is hard to accept. Poverty, illness, abuse, and grief aren’t merely failures of perspective. Stoics recognized reasons to seek health and security, but their account can still understate how much circumstances matter to human flourishing.

And the popular version can go wrong in predictable ways:
- “Focus on what you control” becomes **don’t challenge unfair conditions**, even though collective action can influence conditions.
- “Examine your emotions” becomes **suppress your emotions**, rather than understand them.
- “Be resilient” becomes **blame yourself for struggling**, or tolerate something you should leave.

Those aren’t necessary consequences of Stoicism, but they’re important risks—especially when it’s presented as a universal answer.

My assessment: **Stoicism is a useful foundation or corrective, not something you have to accept as a complete package.** It’s especially helpful if you tend to catastrophize, chase approval, or get consumed by outcomes. It may need balancing if you already minimize your needs, overcontrol your feelings, or put up with too much.

A good test is: *Does practicing it make you more capable of caring and acting—or merely better at pretending nothing hurts?* The first is valuable. The second is not much of a philosophy for living.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism identifies **a real danger, but not an inevitable feature of Stoicism**. The key distinction is between accepting reality and accepting that reality *ought to stay as it is*.

If your workplace is exploitative, for example:
- **Accepting reality** means acknowledging what’s happening, rather than assuming hard work will magically make management fair.
- **Resignation** means concluding that management is outside your control, so you should just adjust your attitude.
- **Constructive action** might mean documenting problems, organizing with colleagues, making a complaint, or looking for another job—while recognizing that none guarantees success.

Stoicism can support that third response. You don’t control the outcome, but your choices still matter. **“I can’t guarantee change” is not the same as “I can’t contribute to change.”** The popular “control what you can” slogan often obscures that middle ground: things we can influence without controlling.

There’s also a reason to take the criticism seriously beyond simple misunderstanding. Stoicism puts enormous weight on a person’s judgments and character, and comparatively less on their circumstances. That can make it easier to frame suffering as a problem of attitude rather than a problem requiring material or political change. When a powerful person tells someone with less power to “be Stoic,” it’s worth asking whose interests that advice serves.

Classical Stoicism does have resources against passivity: justice, social responsibility, and doing the right thing despite personal costs are central to it. But pointing to those teachings doesn’t automatically excuse how the philosophy gets used today.

My view is that **Stoicism is most defensible when acceptance is the starting point for action, not a substitute for it**. Sometimes enduring a situation is necessary, especially when acting carries serious risks; that isn’t a moral failure. But a philosophy that helps you survive a bad situation should also leave room to name it as bad, seek help, and try to change it.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control every outcome, but we can take responsibility for our choices, question our first reactions, and put character ahead of status. Where it falls short is in treating circumstances as less important to a good life than they really are—and, especially in its modern self-help form, turning resilience into an obligation to tolerate the intolerable. Poverty, exploitation, and grief aren’t simply attitude problems. The best use of Stoicism is to help us face reality clearly and act with courage, not convince ourselves that nothing needs to change.

## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits several things people are dealing with right now: uncertainty, burnout, constant comparison, and a news feed full of problems they can’t personally fix. Its most portable idea, “focus on what’s within your control,” gives people a way to decide where to put their attention.

A few things have helped it spread:

- **It’s easy to encounter in small doses.** A line from Marcus Aurelius can fit in an Instagram post or a short video. You can get something useful from it without first learning the whole philosophy. That makes Stoicism especially suited to social media, even when the quotes lose their context.

- **There’s now a large publishing and podcast business around it.** Writers such as Ryan Holiday, through *The Daily Stoic* and his books, have introduced it to people who would never browse the ancient philosophy section. Entrepreneurs, athletes, and productivity influencers have helped circulate the same ideas.

- **It offers a sense of agency during instability.** Pandemic disruption, economic insecurity, and political conflict make advice about handling uncertainty feel relevant. Stoic practices, such as examining your judgments and anticipating setbacks, give people something concrete to do when circumstances won’t cooperate.

- **It fits the culture of self-improvement.** Discipline, resilience, and managing your reactions translate easily into advice about work, fitness, and competition. That has given Stoicism a particular foothold in some male-oriented online spaces, where it can provide a socially acceptable way to talk about distress.

- **It offers ethical guidance without requiring religious belief.** For someone who wants a daily practice or a framework for living but doesn’t belong to a religion, an ancient philosophy can be appealing. Its age also gives it credibility: advice from a Roman emperor feels less disposable than another productivity tip.

The version that spreads online is often selective. Ancient Stoicism placed considerable weight on virtue, justice, and duties to other people. Popular versions tend to emphasize personal composure and performance, sometimes sliding into “don’t feel anything” or “put up with whatever happens.” The Stoics did distinguish between what we can and can’t control, but they still expected people to act responsibly within their circumstances.

And part of the “everywhere” feeling is probably your feed. Once you engage with a few Stoic posts, recommendation systems can show you a lot more. There’s a real popular revival, amplified by platforms that are very good at turning a passing interest into your entire afternoon.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

I think it’s a good practical philosophy for ordinary life, though I’d be wary of making it your whole worldview. The self-help trend draws on something substantial, but often presents a narrow version of it.

Some Stoic habits are genuinely useful:

- Separating what you can influence from what you can’t can help with worry. You can prepare for a job interview; you can’t guarantee an offer. That gives you somewhere useful to put your effort.
- Examining your first interpretation can prevent unnecessary distress. “My friend hasn’t replied” is a fact; “they don’t care about me” is a judgment you might need to reconsider.
- Deciding how you want to behave before things get difficult can help you keep your temper or admit a mistake when you’d rather defend yourself.

Those practices have some overlap with cognitive behavioral therapy, whose early developers drew partly on Stoic ideas. That doesn’t mean reading Marcus Aurelius is equivalent to getting therapy, or that every Stoic claim has scientific support.

The trouble comes when you apply the philosophy too rigidly. Trying to govern your responses can become blaming yourself for every painful feeling. Accepting what you can’t control can become staying in a situation you could leave or help change. And rehearsing possible losses, a practice some Stoics recommended, may feed rumination if you’re already prone to anxiety.

There are also demanding claims in the original philosophy. The Stoics held that virtue is the only true good; health, wealth, and even close relationships don’t determine whether your life is good in the deepest sense. They still thought those things were generally worth caring for, but you don’t have to accept their entire account of a good life to find their exercises useful.

For everyday use, I’d take “pause before believing your first reaction” and “put effort where you have some influence.” I’d be much more cautious about advice that makes needing support, grieving, or protesting unfair treatment sound like a failure of discipline. Stoicism is useful when it helps you respond thoughtfully while still taking your feelings and circumstances seriously.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair, especially when Stoicism is presented as workplace or productivity advice. “Control your reaction” can help someone get through a difficult day. If it’s the only advice offered about an abusive boss or an unsafe workplace, it leaves the source of the problem untouched and puts the burden on the person suffering.

There’s a useful distinction between accepting that something is happening and deciding to tolerate it indefinitely. You can acknowledge that your boss is unlikely to become reasonable and use that judgment to start looking for another job, document misconduct, or organize with coworkers. You don’t control the outcome of those efforts, but you can still undertake them.

Classical Stoicism has room for that. It treats justice as a virtue and gives people duties toward others. “I can remain calm while this happens” wouldn’t, by itself, settle whether you ought to intervene. Still, the criticism can’t be dismissed entirely as a misunderstanding: Stoic emphasis on adapting your judgments to circumstances can encourage accommodation, especially when detached from those ethical duties.

The question I’d ask is: **does this advice help the person choose what to do, or does it make them feel obliged to endure?** Who is giving the advice matters, too. Choosing a Stoic practice for yourself is different from hearing “manage your response” from someone who has the power to improve your conditions.

Sometimes enduring a situation is the safest available choice, and people deserve ways to cope while their options are limited. But composure isn’t evidence that a situation is acceptable, and anger isn’t necessarily a failure of judgment. Anger can tell you that something needs attention, even if you need time to decide how to act on it.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets a lot right about how easily our first reactions can mislead us, and how much energy we spend worrying about things we can’t control. Pausing to examine a judgment or choosing where to put our effort can make daily life more manageable. But its emphasis on composure can become a demand to endure conditions that deserve to change. Grief, anger, and needing other people are ordinary parts of life, and treating them as failures of discipline makes the philosophy less humane. I find Stoicism most useful when it helps us decide how to act, while leaving room to feel upset and ask for help.

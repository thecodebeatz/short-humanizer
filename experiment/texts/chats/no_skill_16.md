## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Partly because Stoicism offers a very marketable answer to a very current feeling: **“The world is chaotic, and I need a way not to be overwhelmed by it.”** A few trends have helped it spread.

- **It gives people a sense of agency.** The Stoic distinction between what you can control—your judgments and actions—and what you can’t is appealing amid economic uncertainty, political conflict, and constant bad news. The pandemic made that appeal especially obvious.

- **It’s easy to turn into practical advice.** You don’t need to work through an entire philosophical system to get something out of “pause before reacting” or “don’t make your happiness depend entirely on other people’s approval.” Marcus Aurelius’s *Meditations*, originally private reflections, often feels unusually direct and personal.

- **It fits social media almost perfectly.** Short quotations, memorable rules, journaling prompts, and “five habits for mental toughness” travel well. Writers and podcasters—especially Ryan Holiday and the wider self-improvement ecosystem—have built a large audience around it. That popularization was already underway in the 2010s, rather than appearing only in the last few years.

- **It overlaps with modern psychological ideas.** There are real historical connections between Stoic thinking and cognitive behavioral therapy, especially the idea that our interpretations influence our emotional responses. That doesn’t make Stoicism a substitute for therapy, but it helps it feel practical rather than merely ancient.

- **It offers a framework for living without requiring religious belief.** For some people, it fills a gap: daily reflection, ethical guidance, a way to think about suffering and death. Ancient Stoicism did have a religious cosmology, but modern versions often leave that out.

- **It has been adopted by productivity and masculine self-help cultures.** Its language of discipline and resilience appeals to entrepreneurs, athletes, and people looking for an alternative to feeling helpless. Sometimes that’s useful; sometimes it turns into branding about becoming invulnerable.

There’s an important catch: **popular “Stoicism” is often narrower than the actual philosophy.** Ancient Stoics weren’t just teaching people to endure stress and get more done. They emphasized justice, moral character, obligations to others, and participation in society. Nor was the goal simply to suppress every emotion.

So your impression makes sense: there’s a genuine revival, amplified by platforms that reward its most quotable parts. The version that’s everywhere is often less “a complete philosophy of a good life” and more “a toolkit for staying steady”—which is exactly what a lot of people currently want.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It can be a good philosophy for ordinary life—and the self-help trend often sells a stripped-down version of it.** Those aren’t mutually exclusive.

What makes it useful isn’t the “be unbreakable” image. It’s a handful of habits that can make everyday life less exhausting:

- **Separate your effort from the outcome.** You can prepare well for a job interview without being able to guarantee an offer. That distinction helps you act without treating every disappointment as a personal failure.
- **Don’t automatically believe your first interpretation.** “My friend hasn’t replied” is a fact; “they don’t care about me” is a judgment. Giving yourself room to question the second is valuable.
- **Decide what kind of person you want to be.** Stoicism asks whether you acted honestly, fairly, and courageously—not just whether you won, impressed people, or felt good.
- **Remember that other people matter.** In the ancient philosophy, being dependable, just, and useful to your community is part of living well, not a distraction from personal development.

But there are real limitations, not just misunderstandings.

**Its ideal of emotional independence is demanding.** Classical Stoics held that virtue is the only true good; things like health, money, and even the survival of loved ones are not what ultimately makes a life good or bad. They still thought those things were worth caring for, but you don’t have to be emotionally repressed to find that position too austere. Love and vulnerability may be more closely connected than the Stoic ideal comfortably allows.

**Acceptance can also slide into resignation.** “I can’t control this” is helpful when it means letting go of a guaranteed result. It’s harmful when it means tolerating mistreatment or deciding that social problems aren’t your concern. Stoicism can support determined action, but slogans about acceptance don’t always convey that.

And **it isn’t a complete answer to psychological distress.** Reflection and reframing may help, but grief, trauma, or depression aren’t failures of discipline.

My take: **Stoicism is a strong guide to how to respond, but a more debatable account of what makes life worth living.** Its advice about judgment, effort, and character is often excellent. Its stronger claim—that a good life can be secured by virtue regardless of external circumstances—is much harder to accept.

For a normal person, the sensible approach is to use it as a practice, not a purity test: *What can I do here? What am I assuming? What would a decent response look like?* You can benefit from those questions without deciding that needing comfort, grieving a loss, or wanting circumstances to improve makes you insufficiently philosophical.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **fair about some modern Stoicism, but not a necessary consequence of Stoicism itself**. The key distinction is between accepting reality and accepting that reality *ought to stay as it is*.

If your workplace is exploitative, for example:

- **Useful acceptance:** “This is happening. I can’t guarantee how management will respond, but I can document it, set boundaries, look for another job, or organize with coworkers.”
- **Resignation dressed as wisdom:** “Other people’s behavior is outside my control, so the problem is my attitude.”

The second turns a real external problem into a personal failure to cope. That’s a serious weakness in self-help messaging that treats resilience as the answer to everything.

The Stoic distinction between what you control and what you don’t can also be misleading when reduced to a slogan. **Very little is entirely under our control, but a great deal is open to our influence.** You can’t single-handedly control working conditions, housing policy, or an abusive person’s behavior. That doesn’t make efforts to change those things pointless. Collective action, especially, gets lost when the focus is exclusively on individual self-management.

Ancient Stoicism has resources for resisting that passivity: justice is a central virtue, and obligations to other people matter. On that account, refusing to challenge an injustice could be a failure of character—not an achievement of inner peace. Still, it would be too convenient to say every passive interpretation is simply “not real Stoicism.” Its emphasis on remaining morally intact despite external circumstances can genuinely pull attention away from how much those circumstances affect people’s lives.

That also helps explain why a watered-down version appeals to institutions: **teaching people to tolerate stress is often cheaper than removing avoidable causes of stress.** Coping advice isn’t necessarily bad, but it’s inadequate when offered instead of safer conditions, fair pay, or accountability.

So my test would be: **Does this practice help someone see clearly and act, or does it make them feel guilty for wanting things to change?** A useful Stoicism helps you withstand the uncertainty and frustration involved in changing a bad situation. A harmful version teaches you to become comfortable staying in it.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t guarantee outcomes, but we can examine our judgments, choose our actions, and try to act with courage and fairness. That’s a useful antidote to anxiety and the exhausting need to control everything. Where it falls short—especially in its modern self-help form—is when acceptance becomes resignation, or resilience becomes an excuse to leave harmful conditions unchanged. Our attitudes matter, but so do our circumstances. The best use of Stoicism is to help us face reality and change what we can, not to convince ourselves that suffering is merely a failure of perspective.

## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A lot of its appeal comes from how neatly it speaks to a common feeling: “There’s too much happening, I can’t control most of it, and I still have to get through my day.”

A few things have helped it spread:

- **It gives people a manageable response to uncertainty.** The Stoic distinction between what you can control and what you can’t is easy to apply to work stress, politics, money worries, or an endless news feed. It gives you somewhere to put your effort when worrying feels useless.

- **It fits the way social media works.** Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, and Seneca wrote plenty of short, memorable passages that travel well as quotes. A few lines from *Meditations* can feel surprisingly personal, even on a phone screen between ads.

- **There’s now a substantial business promoting it.** Writers and podcasters such as Ryan Holiday have made Stoicism accessible through books, newsletters, and daily exercises. Their success has helped turn a philosophical tradition into a recognizable self-help category.

- **It appeals to people who dislike the language of self-help.** Advice about discipline, judgment, and endurance can feel more credible when it comes from an ancient emperor or a formerly enslaved philosopher. Some of the advice is familiar, but the historical setting gives it a different emotional weight.

- **It has found an especially receptive audience in male self-improvement culture.** Its emphasis on composure and responsibility fits conversations about masculinity, fitness, and professional success. That can introduce people to worthwhile ideas, though online versions sometimes reduce it to “feel nothing and work harder.”

The pandemic probably accelerated its appeal: people had unusually direct reasons to think about mortality, disrupted plans, and limited control. But the current revival was already underway before then.

There’s also a gap between ancient Stoicism and what gets shared online. The ancient Stoics cared about ethical character, obligations to other people, and examining your judgments. Much of today’s content concentrates on staying calm and productive. Suppressing emotion isn’t the whole philosophy, and neither is accepting every bad situation without trying to change it.

So your impression makes sense. Stoicism has ideas that fit current anxieties, and it has a publishing and social-media ecosystem very good at keeping those ideas in view. Once you engage with a little of it, recommendation feeds can make it feel even more ubiquitous.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

I think it’s a good practical philosophy for ordinary life, though I’d be cautious about making it your entire worldview. The self-help trend draws on some genuinely useful ideas and leaves out much of the ethics.

Its strongest advice concerns how we respond when things go badly. If someone insults you, you can examine whether their judgment deserves any weight before reacting. If you’re waiting for medical results, you can attend the appointment and seek support while recognizing that worrying won’t determine the result. That distinction can help, even if putting it into practice takes work.

Stoicism also asks a worthwhile question about success: how much should your character depend on whether you get what you want? You can act honestly and competently and still lose a job or fail at something. There’s relief in judging yourself partly by how you acted, since the outcome may depend on luck or someone else’s decision.

The difficulties start when those ideas become rigid:

- “Focus on what you control” can become an excuse to withdraw. Many things we can’t control, such as working conditions, are still things we can influence together.
- Examining your emotions can slide into policing them. Grief and anger don’t necessarily mean you’ve made a philosophical mistake. They may need time, care, or action.
- The strict Stoic claim that virtue is the only true good is demanding. It treats health, money, and even close relationships as things that don’t ultimately determine a good life, although Stoics could still value and pursue them. You can reasonably think those things matter more than that.

Ancient Stoicism does have resources against some of these problems: it emphasizes duties to others, and its ideal isn’t simply to feel nothing. But that doesn’t make every criticism a misunderstanding. Its ideal of emotional independence can be too severe for people who see vulnerability and attachment as essential parts of living well.

For everyday use, I’d take its habits of reflection and its attention to how you act, while leaving room to need other people and to be upset when something hurts. You don’t have to accept the whole system to benefit from reading Epictetus or Marcus Aurelius. And if practicing it makes you ashamed of having feelings, that’s a good reason to reconsider how you’re using it.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is often fair, especially when Stoicism gets packaged as workplace or productivity advice. If someone’s being mistreated and the advice is always “manage your reaction,” it puts the burden on the person being harmed and leaves the behavior causing the harm unexamined.

The ambiguity is in the word “accept.” Recognizing that a situation exists can help you respond effectively. Resigning yourself to its continuation is a separate decision. You can acknowledge that your boss is abusive, that you can’t force them to change, and still document what’s happening, organize with coworkers, or look for another job.

The popular “control what you can control” slogan can obscure this because control is a very high bar. You don’t control whether a complaint succeeds or a political campaign wins. You can still contribute to the outcome. Most efforts to change society involve acting under exactly that uncertainty.

Ancient Stoicism gives some support for that kind of action. It treats justice as a virtue and emphasizes duties to other people. A Stoic has reasons to oppose mistreatment even when success is unlikely. But its emphasis on adapting your judgments to circumstances also creates a genuine tension: it can make endurance seem like the answer when resistance would be appropriate. The criticism deserves more than “that’s not real Stoicism.”

There’s value in endurance, too, particularly when someone genuinely has few options. Getting through a bad situation may be all they can safely do for now. We shouldn’t turn the demand to change things into another way of blaming them.

I’d judge the advice by what it helps someone do. Does it give them enough composure to assess their options and act? Or does every problem end with them being told to lower their expectations? If an employer promotes resilience while refusing to address conditions it could change, skepticism is warranted.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something right about living with uncertainty: we can act thoughtfully without being able to guarantee the outcome, and worrying harder won’t give us more control. But its emphasis on managing our reactions can become an excuse to tolerate conditions we should try to change. An abusive workplace calls for more than a calmer employee. I find Stoicism useful when it helps me pause, judge a situation clearly, and decide what to do. I’m less convinced when it makes grief seem like a failure of judgment or treats needing other people as a weakness.

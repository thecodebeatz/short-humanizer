## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits a lot of what people are dealing with: uncertainty, constant comparison, and the feeling that you should be doing more. Its most portable advice, "focus on what you can control," offers a manageable response when the news, your job, or other people feel impossible to influence.

A few things help explain why it seems to be everywhere now.

**It works unusually well on social media.** A line from Marcus Aurelius can fit on a quote card or over a video of someone working out. You don’t need much background to find it useful. Platforms reward that kind of short, emotionally satisfying advice, so you can encounter Stoicism constantly without ever seeking out philosophy.

It also has a well-developed publishing and podcast business around it. Ryan Holiday’s books and *The Daily Stoic*, among others, have made ancient texts accessible and turned them into a daily habit. Once you follow one of those accounts, recommendation feeds can make the subject feel even more widespread.

There’s also a close fit with self-improvement culture. Stoic exercises about handling setbacks, examining your judgments, and accepting discomfort appeal to athletes, entrepreneurs, and people trying to build discipline. Some men seem especially drawn to a vocabulary of resilience and self-command that lets them approach emotional difficulties without the language of therapy. That can be useful, though it can also encourage the mistaken idea that being Stoic means suppressing feelings.

The pandemic likely gave its appeal another push. Illness, disrupted plans, and reminders of mortality made the distinction between what we can influence and what we can’t feel immediately relevant. The same advice remains attractive amid financial insecurity and political anxiety.

There’s a catch to the popular version: it often reduces a whole philosophy to personal coping and productivity. Ancient Stoicism placed substantial weight on virtue, duties to other people, and participating in society. Accepting what you cannot control was compatible with working to change things you could influence.

So I think the popularity comes from a genuine need meeting a very effective format. The advice can help people get through a difficult day, and it’s exceptionally easy to package, sell, and share. How much you see it is probably also partly your feed learning that you’ll stop and look.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

It can be a good philosophy for everyday life. The self-help trend draws on genuinely useful ideas, though it often leaves out the parts that are harder to sell.

The most useful Stoic habit is separating what happens from the judgment you make about it. If someone is rude to you, the event is real; “everyone disrespects me” is an interpretation you can examine. That pause can keep a bad interaction from ruining your day. It doesn’t require pretending you weren’t hurt.

Its emphasis on what you can control is also helpful, provided you allow for things you can *influence*. You can prepare for an interview without controlling the hiring decision. You can work to improve your workplace without being able to fix it alone. That distinction helps you decide where to put your effort and when to stop blaming yourself for an outcome.

The fuller philosophy also asks you to think about how you treat people. For the ancient Stoics, being successful or feeling calm wasn’t enough: you had to act justly. That gives you a reason to be honest or help someone even when doing so is inconvenient.

Where I’d be cautious:

- **Emotional suppression.** Ancient Stoic accounts of emotion are more complicated than “don’t feel things,” but their ideal of freedom from destructive passions is demanding. In everyday use, it can become an excuse to avoid grief, vulnerability, or asking for help.
- **Accepting too much.** “Control your response” can help with an obnoxious colleague. In an abusive relationship or exploitative workplace, you may need support, boundaries, or a way out. Coping advice is inadequate on its own.
- **Making hardship a personal test.** You can do everything reasonably well and still struggle. A philosophy of self-discipline can become cruel if you use it to judge yourself, or others, for suffering.

There are also larger commitments you don’t have to accept just to use a few exercises. Classical Stoicism holds that virtue is sufficient for a good life and treats health, wealth, and even reputation as things that don’t determine your moral worth. The moral-worth point is appealing; the stronger claim that virtue alone makes a life good is much harder to accept when you consider severe illness or deprivation.

My view is that Stoicism works well as a set of practices, with some reservations as a complete philosophy. Try it on ordinary frustrations: examine your first interpretation, choose a response you can respect, and judge your effort fairly. If it helps you handle conflict while remaining willing to speak up and seek support, it’s useful. If you start treating every feeling as a failure of discipline, ease off.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair, especially when Stoicism is offered by someone who benefits from other people putting up with a bad situation. A boss telling an overworked employee to “focus on your response” deserves more skepticism than an employee using that advice to get through a difficult shift.

The advice can help you manage distress while leaving its cause untouched. If low pay, discrimination, or impossible workloads get treated as tests of your mindset, responsibility shifts toward the person suffering. You can end up feeling that both the situation and your unhappiness about it are your fault.

But accepting that something is happening doesn’t require agreeing that it should continue. You might acknowledge that your workplace is unfair, give yourself time to calm down, and then document what’s happening or organize with coworkers. Emotional steadiness can make action easier. Anger can also tell you that something needs attention; you don’t have to dismiss it to avoid acting impulsively.

Classical Stoicism has resources for resisting injustice: it treats justice as a virtue and takes duties to other people seriously. Still, “the ancient version was better” doesn’t settle the criticism. Its emphasis on inner freedom can encourage accommodation, particularly when people treat circumstances as outside their control before considering what they could accomplish together. One employee may have little power; a group may have considerably more.

I’d judge a particular version of Stoicism by what it encourages you to do. Does it help you assess your options, tolerate the discomfort of speaking up, and act despite uncertainty? Or does every problem somehow end with you adjusting your attitude?

Sometimes enduring a situation is the safest available choice, especially when leaving would threaten your income or safety. That deserves respect. The problem is turning endurance into a moral obligation, or assuming that because someone can learn to bear something, no one needs to change it.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something right about everyday life: we often make a difficult situation worse by reacting before we’ve examined our assumptions. Learning to pause and focus on what we can influence can help us act more thoughtfully. But the popular version goes wrong when it treats every hardship as a test of attitude. Some situations call for grief, support, or collective action, and staying calm won’t fix an unjust workplace. I find Stoicism useful as a way to steady myself, though I’m wary of advice that makes enduring a bad situation feel like a moral duty.

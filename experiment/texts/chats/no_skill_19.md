## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

It’s a pretty good fit for the moment: Stoicism offers **a way to feel less helpless in a world that keeps demanding your attention**. And its core ideas are easy to turn into podcasts, short videos, and daily habits.

A few things have come together:

- **A lot feels outside people’s control.** Pandemic disruption, political conflict, economic insecurity, and relentless news make the Stoic distinction between what you can control and what you can’t especially appealing. “Focus on your judgments and actions” gives people something concrete to do.

- **It speaks directly to the attention economy.** Social media constantly invites comparison, outrage, and concern about other people’s opinions. Stoicism says your well-being shouldn’t depend entirely on status, praise, or external events. That can feel like an antidote—even when you encounter it on the same apps causing the stress.

- **It’s philosophy with a low barrier to entry.** You can read a short passage from Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus and apply it to a difficult meeting that afternoon. You don’t need to master an elaborate philosophical system to get something useful from it.

- **Modern promoters made it into a recognizable self-help brand.** Authors and podcasters, especially Ryan Holiday, have packaged Stoic ideas into accessible books, newsletters, and routines. Tech, business, sports, and military-adjacent audiences have helped spread them. Quotes and simple exercises travel especially well online.

- **It offers a secular framework for living.** Some people want more than productivity tips but don’t identify with a religion. Stoicism supplies practices, moral language, and a sense of continuity with something older than the latest wellness trend.

- **It overlaps with familiar therapeutic ideas.** Examining your interpretations rather than treating every thought as a fact resembles techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy, whose early developers drew partly on Stoic philosophy. That overlap helps Stoicism feel practical and psychologically plausible—though it isn’t a substitute for mental-health treatment.

There’s also a particular appeal in some male-oriented spaces: Stoicism gives men a socially acceptable vocabulary for talking about distress, discipline, and emotional regulation. That can be constructive, but it can also get flattened into “never show feelings” or “just endure everything.”

**That flattening is the big catch.** Ancient Stoicism wasn’t merely about being unbothered, productive, or tough. It emphasized virtue, justice, responsibilities to other people, and responding wisely to emotions—not simply suppressing them. Some popular versions preserve that; others turn it into a technique for tolerating a bad situation without questioning it.

And part of the “everywhere” feeling may be algorithmic: once you engage with a few Stoicism posts, platforms can make a real resurgence look like a universal cultural takeover.

In short: it combines ancient credibility, practical advice, and excellent internet packaging—and it addresses a very current feeling: **“I can’t make the world settle down, so how do I steady myself?”**

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It’s a serious philosophy that can be useful for ordinary life—and it’s also become a self-help trend.** Those aren’t mutually exclusive. The important distinction is between Stoicism as a way to live well and Stoicism as a way to become endlessly tough and efficient.

The useful parts address very ordinary problems:

- **Separating your effort from the outcome.** You can prepare well for an interview without controlling whether you get the job. That distinction can reduce pointless self-blame without excusing poor preparation.
- **Questioning your first interpretation.** “My friend hasn’t replied” is a fact; “they don’t care about me” is an interpretation. Creating space between those can prevent unnecessary suffering.
- **Acting on principles rather than moods.** Being fair, honest, and considerate even when you’re irritated is a worthwhile ambition, whether or not you call it Stoic.
- **Accepting that a good life includes discomfort.** Disappointment and uncertainty aren’t necessarily signs you’ve done life wrong.

But there are real limitations—not just problems with influencers’ versions.

**Classical Stoicism makes demanding claims.** It holds that virtue is the only true good, while things like health, wealth, and reputation are “preferred indifferents”: worth pursuing, but not what makes a life morally good. That can protect your dignity when life goes badly. Taken too rigidly, though, it can understate how much illness, poverty, grief, and supportive relationships matter to human well-being.

It can also be misused in a few predictable ways:

- **Acceptance becomes resignation:** “I should manage my reaction” replaces “I should leave this situation or help change it.” Yet accepting the facts doesn’t mean approving of them, and Stoic concern for justice can support action.
- **Emotional regulation becomes suppression:** feeling sad or angry gets treated as a personal failure. A healthier use is to examine what an emotion is telling you before deciding how to act—not pretend you don’t feel it.
- **Responsibility becomes self-blame:** people start treating every difficulty as an attitude problem, ignoring material circumstances or the need for help.

So my assessment is: **Stoicism is a good practical resource, but you don’t have to accept it as a complete account of human life.** You can borrow its exercises while also believing that relationships, pleasure, vulnerability, and changing unfair conditions are genuinely important.

A useful test is what it does to you: **does it make you steadier, kinder, and more able to act—or merely better at enduring things you shouldn’t have to endure?** The first is a strong reason to keep practising it. The second is a reason to reconsider how you’re using it.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **sometimes right about modern Stoicism, but too broad as a criticism of Stoicism itself**. The key question is: *What are you being asked to accept?*

There’s a crucial difference between:
- **Accepting reality:** “This is happening. Wishing it weren’t won’t change it.”
- **Accepting legitimacy:** “This is fine, deserved, or not worth resisting.”

The first can help you change a bad situation. The second can keep you trapped in it. You can acknowledge that your workplace is exploitative without deciding that you should quietly tolerate it.

Likewise, “focus on what you control” can mean two very different things. A constructive reading is: **you can’t guarantee success, but you can choose how to respond—including organizing, protesting, leaving, or asking for help.** A limiting reading is: “If you can’t fix it single-handedly, stop worrying about it.” That second reading wrongly treats *lack of total control* as *lack of influence*. Most meaningful social change happens through people acting together without any guarantee of success.

Ancient Stoicism also treated justice and obligations to others as central virtues. It wasn’t simply a philosophy of personal comfort. But that doesn’t make it immune to criticism: its emphasis on inner freedom and the limited importance of external circumstances can make it easier to downplay material suffering or accommodate existing hierarchies. Ancient Stoics were not uniformly reformers, and modern defenders shouldn’t pretend otherwise.

The criticism is especially persuasive when **the person recommending resilience benefits from your endurance**. An employer telling an overworked employee to manage their reactions, rather than fixing unreasonable conditions, is shifting responsibility from the institution to the individual. The advice might contain a useful coping technique and still serve an unfair arrangement.

At the same time, coping isn’t necessarily surrender. Someone may need to endure a bad situation temporarily because leaving is costly or unsafe. Helping them stay steady can preserve their ability to make a plan; it needn’t imply that the situation is acceptable.

So I’d judge a version of Stoicism by this test: **Does it help people see clearly and choose their response, or does it prescribe endurance regardless of circumstances?** Sometimes wisdom means tolerating discomfort. Sometimes it means refusing it. A worthwhile philosophy needs room for both.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control everything that happens to us, but we can practise responding with greater clarity, courage, and fairness. That’s a useful antidote to anxiety and impulsive reactions. Where it can go wrong—especially in its modern self-help form—is turning emotional discipline into suppression and acceptance into resignation. Poverty, injustice, and harmful relationships aren’t merely tests of attitude; they’re conditions worth changing. Stoicism is most valuable when it helps us face reality and act wisely, not when it teaches us to endure whatever comes our way.

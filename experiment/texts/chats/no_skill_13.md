## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A big reason is that Stoicism offers a simple, reassuring answer to a very current feeling: **“The world is chaotic, and I don’t have much control over it.”** Its response—focus on your choices, act according to your values, and loosen your grip on outcomes—is easy to find useful.

A few things have helped it spread:

- **It fits an anxious, uncertain era.** Pandemic disruption, political conflict, economic insecurity, and constant bad news make advice about handling uncertainty feel practical. You don’t have to believe everything is going to work out to find value in deciding how you’ll respond.

- **It’s extremely compatible with social media.** Marcus Aurelius, Seneca, and Epictetus supply short, memorable passages that work well as quotes, videos, and daily reminders. A complex philosophical tradition becomes something you can encounter in thirty seconds—though sometimes stripped of context or falsely attributed.

- **Modern authors have made it accessible—and built a big market around it.** Writers such as Ryan Holiday and William B. Irvine have presented Stoicism as an everyday practice rather than an academic subject. Books, podcasts, newsletters, journals, and courses reinforce one another, giving it a much larger cultural footprint.

- **It overlaps with familiar mental-health advice.** Distinguishing events from your judgments about them, questioning automatic reactions, and accepting what you can’t change resemble techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy, whose early developers drew partly on Stoic ideas. That makes it feel both ancient and psychologically credible, although philosophy isn’t a substitute for treatment.

- **It slots neatly into productivity, fitness, and masculine self-improvement culture.** Discipline, resilience, and responsibility are easy to market to entrepreneurs, athletes, and young men looking for direction. Some versions offer a genuinely constructive model of strength; others reduce it to “don’t complain, don’t feel anything.”

- **It provides meaning without requiring religious belief.** It offers an ethical framework, daily exercises, and a way to think about suffering and death. People can adopt those practices without joining an institution or accepting the ancient Stoics’ whole worldview.

There’s also a visibility effect: once you engage with a few Stoicism posts, recommendation systems can make it seem as though everyone is talking about it.

The important distinction is between **Stoicism as a philosophy** and **“stoic” as an internet aesthetic**. The philosophy emphasizes wisdom, justice, courage, self-restraint, and duties to other people—not merely being unbothered or getting more work done.

So its popularity is partly a response to genuine needs, and partly very effective packaging. It’s an old philosophy that happens to fit both the problems of the moment and the platforms through which people look for answers.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It’s a genuinely useful philosophy for everyday life—and it’s also become a self-help trend.** The trendy version often takes its most marketable ideas and leaves out the ethical commitments that make them more than motivational advice.

You don’t need to be an emperor or endure extraordinary hardship to benefit from it. Some of its best applications are very ordinary:
- **Before a job interview:** prepare carefully, but don’t treat the hiring decision as a verdict on your worth.
- **During an argument:** distinguish what someone actually said from the motives you’re attributing to them.
- **After making a mistake:** take responsibility and repair what you can, rather than endlessly punishing yourself.
- **When someone needs help:** do the decent thing even if it’s inconvenient or gets you no recognition.

That last example matters. Classical Stoicism isn’t primarily about feeling calm; it’s about **being a good person even when circumstances make that difficult**. Calm is a possible benefit, not the whole point.

But there are real limitations:

**1. “Accept what you can’t control” can become an excuse for passivity.**  
A better interpretation is: *you can’t guarantee results, but you’re still responsible for your efforts.* You can oppose unfair treatment without assuming you’ll win. Acceptance of reality doesn’t require approval of it.

**2. It can turn into emotional suppression.**  
Stoic practice asks you to examine the judgments behind your reactions—not pretend that grief, fear, or anger aren’t happening. Still, the ancient ideal of freedom from disruptive passions is demanding, and it won’t suit everyone. If practicing “Stoicism” makes you ashamed of having feelings or reluctant to seek support, it’s probably doing you harm.

**3. Some of its strongest claims are hard to accept.**  
Ancient Stoics held that virtue alone is sufficient for a good life, and that things such as health, wealth, and reputation aren’t what ultimately make a life good or bad. There’s a valuable insight there—circumstances aren’t the measure of your character—but taken strictly, it can understate how much poverty, illness, abuse, and loss affect a person’s well-being.

**4. It isn’t a complete answer to every human need.**  
People also need affection, play, belonging, material security, and sometimes professional help. A philosophy of personal responsibility becomes misleading when it treats every problem as a mindset problem.

My take: **Stoicism is a good toolkit and a serious ethical tradition, but you don’t have to accept it as a total worldview.** Keep the practices that make you more thoughtful, courageous, fair, and able to live with uncertainty. Be wary of versions that merely make you more productive, emotionally armored, or tolerant of being mistreated.

A useful test is: *Is this helping me engage with life and other people more fully—or just helping me feel less affected by them?* The first is a much better sign.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism identifies a real danger, but **accepting reality and accepting how things are run aren’t the same thing.** Stoicism can help you change a bad situation—or help you rationalize staying in it. The distinction depends on how it’s taught and used.

The useful version says: *“This is happening. I can’t guarantee what happens next. What action is available to me, and what would be right?”* That can support leaving an abusive relationship, challenging a boss, organizing with coworkers, or campaigning against an unjust law. None of those actions guarantees success; all require tolerating uncertainty.

The passive version says: *“Your distress comes from your judgments, so change your attitude rather than your circumstances.”* That’s where the criticism lands. If someone is being exploited, focusing exclusively on their reaction can obscure the exploitation—and shift responsibility from whoever is causing harm onto whoever is suffering it.

**The key distinction is control versus influence.** You don’t control whether an employer improves working conditions. But you may influence that outcome by documenting problems, negotiating, reporting violations, or acting collectively. “I don’t control the result” does not mean “there’s nothing worth doing.”

Classical Stoicism also treats **justice as a central virtue**, so a version concerned only with keeping yourself calm is missing something important. Still, it would be too easy to defend the tradition by saying every passive interpretation is simply a misunderstanding. Its strong emphasis on inner freedom can lend itself to accommodation, and ancient Stoics weren’t uniformly opponents of the unjust institutions around them. The tension is real.

A practical way to judge the advice is to ask:

- **What am I being asked to accept?** The fact that harm has happened—or the idea that it must continue?
- **Whose behavior is being scrutinized?** Only the suffering person’s reaction, or also the conduct causing the problem?
- **Does this advice expand my options?** Does it help me think, seek support, and act—or just become quieter?

Sometimes enduring a situation is the safest available choice, especially when someone lacks money, power, or support. That isn’t a failure of courage. But it shouldn’t be dressed up as proof that the situation is acceptable.

So my view is: **Stoicism is valuable when it helps people face reality without surrendering their agency. It becomes harmful when it turns resilience into an obligation to tolerate preventable harm.**

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t control everything that happens to us, but we can practice responding with judgment, courage, and integrity. That’s a useful antidote to anxiety and the urge to measure ourselves by outcomes we can’t guarantee. Where it falls short—especially in its modern self-help form—is when acceptance becomes resignation, emotional discipline becomes suppression, or injustice gets recast as a problem of personal attitude. The best Stoicism helps us face reality and act within it; the worst teaches us to endure what we should be working to change.

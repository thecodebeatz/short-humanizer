## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits a lot of what people are dealing with: uncertainty, constant comparison, and a sense that huge parts of life are beyond their control. Its basic advice, to put your effort into your own judgments and actions, is easy to grasp and useful when you feel overwhelmed.

A few things have helped it spread:

- **It travels well on social media.** A short passage from Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus can stand on its own. Advice about handling insults or worrying less about other people’s opinions fits neatly into a post, even if the philosophy behind it gets lost.
- **There’s a whole publishing and podcast ecosystem around it.** Ryan Holiday’s books and *The Daily Stoic*, along with writers such as William Irvine and Donald Robertson, have made ancient texts accessible to people who wouldn’t normally pick up a philosophy book. Once those audiences grew, platforms had more Stoic content to recommend.
- **It fits self-improvement culture.** Entrepreneurs, athletes, and productivity influencers can use it to talk about discipline and handling setbacks. Some of that is thoughtful; some reduces it to advice about working harder and never complaining.
- **It offers practical guidance without requiring religious belief.** You can try a Stoic exercise without joining a community or accepting a whole account of how the universe works. Ancient Stoicism did have its own theology, but popular versions often leave that out.
- **It overlaps with familiar ideas from therapy.** The thought that our interpretations affect our distress has a real connection to cognitive behavioral therapy: early CBT developers drew on Stoic ideas. That makes parts of Stoicism sound familiar to readers who already know some therapeutic language.

The pandemic probably helped too. Advice about living with uncertainty had an obvious audience, though the revival was already underway before 2020.

There’s also a reason to be cautious about what gets called “Stoicism.” In ordinary speech, *stoic* often means suppressing emotion or enduring everything silently. The ancient philosophy asks much more about how to judge well, treat other people fairly, and live responsibly. Online versions can lose those obligations and turn it into a tough-guy aesthetic.

And the “everywhere” feeling can partly be your feed: once you pause on a few Stoic posts, recommendation systems may serve you many more. So you’re seeing both a genuine revival and, potentially, a concentrated dose of it.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

It can be a good philosophy for ordinary life. The useful parts hold up even if you have no interest in the books, podcasts, or lifestyle built around it. Whether you’d want to accept the whole philosophy is a harder question.

The most useful Stoic habit is separating what you can do from the outcome you hope for. You can prepare for a job interview; you can’t make someone hire you. That distinction can help you act without spending the rest of the day mentally negotiating with something you can’t control. It doesn’t mean the outcome stops mattering.

Stoicism also encourages you to examine your first reaction. If a friend hasn’t replied, “They don’t care about me” is an interpretation you can question. You can feel hurt while leaving room for another explanation. That’s useful without requiring you to become unusually calm or disciplined.

The reservations matter, though:

- You can use “accept what you can’t control” to excuse situations you could influence with help from other people. A bad workplace, for example, may require collective action. Having limited control doesn’t make action pointless.
- Watching your judgments can turn into policing your feelings. Grief and anxiety don’t reliably disappear because you’ve reasoned correctly, and their persistence isn’t a moral failure.
- Ancient Stoicism makes a demanding claim: virtue is the only genuine good; things such as health, money, and reputation are preferable, but don’t make a life morally better. That can help someone preserve their dignity through misfortune. It can also seem to understate how much illness and deprivation damage people’s lives.

The ancient Stoics also understood people as having duties to others. A version focused entirely on personal toughness leaves out a substantial part of the philosophy.

My take is that Stoicism is worth trying as a set of practices before treating it as a complete worldview. Use it to pause before reacting, tolerate uncertainty, and follow through on your responsibilities. If it makes you ashamed of needing comfort or reluctant to ask for help, reconsider how you’re applying it. You don’t have to accept every Stoic claim to benefit from reading Epictetus or Marcus Aurelius.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair about some modern Stoic advice. If the response to an unreasonable workload is always “manage your reaction,” the workload never gets questioned. Advice that helps someone cope can also make an unfair arrangement easier to sustain, especially when it comes from the person who benefits from that arrangement.

There’s a genuine ambiguity in “focus on what you can control.” Very little is entirely under anyone’s control. You can ask for a raise, organize with coworkers, or challenge a policy without being able to guarantee success. If “I can’t control the outcome” becomes “there’s no point trying,” it encourages passivity.

Stoicism can also support taking those risks. You decide that speaking up is worth doing and accept that you might lose, face criticism, or feel afraid. Ancient Stoic ethics included justice and duties toward other people, so private peace of mind isn’t an adequate account of the philosophy. Still, ancient Stoics lived with, and often accommodated, hierarchies we would rightly challenge today. It would be too convenient to credit the tradition with every courageous act and blame every accommodation on a misunderstanding.

The question I’d ask is: **What is this advice helping someone accept?** Accepting that a harmful situation exists, or that changing it will be difficult, can help you act realistically. Accepting that you ought to put up with it indefinitely needs a separate justification.

Timing matters, too. Someone who can’t safely leave a bad job may need ways to get through the day. That relief has value even if it changes nothing about the workplace. The problem comes when coping advice crowds out discussion of better conditions, outside support, or a way to leave.

So I’d judge a version of Stoicism partly by what it encourages people to do about preventable harm. If every problem ends with the less powerful person adjusting their attitude, the criticism has landed.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something right about how much energy we spend fighting outcomes we can’t guarantee. Focusing on our own actions can help us handle uncertainty and act with care even when things go badly. But the advice becomes damaging when it treats distress as a failure of judgment or encourages people to endure conditions they could work together to change. There’s value in learning to cope with a bad situation, provided we leave room for anger, support from others, and the possibility of doing something about it.

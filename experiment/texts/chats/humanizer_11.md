## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A lot of modern Stoicism fits the mood of the last few years: people feel overwhelmed by events they can’t influence, and Stoicism gives them a manageable question: “What can I do about this?”

A few things have helped it spread:

- **It offers relief from uncertainty.** The pandemic, political conflict, financial pressure, and constant bad news have made the distinction between what you can control and what you can’t especially appealing. You can’t settle every crisis, but you can decide how to spend your afternoon or respond to a setback.

- **It’s easy to turn into internet content.** A line from Marcus Aurelius can fit on an image, in a newsletter, or over a video of someone lifting weights. You don’t need much philosophical background to get something from it. Social platforms reward that kind of portable advice, even when the context disappears.

- **There’s an established business around it.** Writers and podcasters, especially Ryan Holiday and *The Daily Stoic*, have brought it to a much larger audience through books, emails, videos, and products. Once you encounter some of that material, recommendation algorithms can make it feel ubiquitous.

- **It fits productivity culture.** Advice about discipline, enduring discomfort, and staying focused appeals to entrepreneurs, athletes, and people trying to get their lives in order. Some versions also offer men a socially acceptable way to talk about distress: through the vocabulary of resilience and self-command.

- **It offers ethical guidance without requiring religious belief.** Stoicism gives people practices, role models, and a way to think about living well. That can appeal to someone who wants more direction than ordinary self-help provides but doesn’t belong to a religious tradition.

There’s also a gap between ancient Stoicism and the version that circulates online. Ancient Stoics cared about virtue, justice, and obligations to other people. Some popular content narrows that to staying calm, working harder, and ignoring criticism. And “stoic” in everyday speech often means suppressing emotion, which is a misleading summary of the philosophy.

So your impression probably reflects both a genuine revival and the way online feeds amplify it. Its central promise is well suited to an anxious time: you can practice responding better even when you can’t make circumstances better.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

It can be a good philosophy for ordinary life, though you don’t have to accept the whole system to benefit from it. The self-help trend draws on some genuinely useful ideas and sometimes pushes them too far.

The most useful Stoic habits are fairly practical:

- Distinguish what you can influence from what you can’t. That helps when you’re waiting for medical results, dealing with a difficult colleague, or replaying an argument. You still take action where you can.
- Examine your first interpretation. “My friend hasn’t replied” is an observation; “my friend doesn’t care about me” is a conclusion you might need to reconsider.
- Judge yourself partly by how you act. If success depends on other people or luck, making outcomes your only measure of worth leaves you vulnerable. Honesty and fairness are things you have more say over.

Those ideas overlap with techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy, whose developers drew partly on Stoicism. That doesn’t establish the truth of the entire philosophy, but it helps explain why some of its practices are useful.

Where I’d be more cautious is its stronger claim that virtue is the only true good, and that things such as health, money, and reputation aren’t what make a life good or bad. Ancient Stoics still preferred health to illness and allowed for grief and affection. Even so, their ideal of remaining inwardly free regardless of circumstances is demanding. You can reasonably think that losing someone you love makes your life worse without having made a philosophical mistake.

There’s also room to misuse the advice. “Accept what you can’t control” can become an excuse to tolerate mistreatment or avoid trying to change something difficult. Most situations involve partial influence: you can’t control your workplace, but you might negotiate, organize with colleagues, or leave. And if practicing Stoicism makes you ashamed of feeling sad, frightened, or dependent on others, it’s worth reconsidering how you’re applying it.

My view is that it’s a useful starting point, especially for rumination and frustration. I’d be wary of treating it as a complete answer to grief, relationships, or injustice. Try its practices and notice whether they help you act more thoughtfully and treat people better, including yourself. Feeling less is a poor measure of whether it’s working.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair about some modern Stoicism. Advice to “control your response” can help someone get through a bad situation, but it can also shift all the responsibility onto the person suffering. An exploitative workplace becomes a test of your attitude; anger at unfair treatment becomes a personal failing.

That’s especially troubling when the advice comes from someone who benefits from your staying put. A boss telling employees to be resilient deserves more scrutiny than an employee choosing a practice that helps them cope.

Ancient Stoicism has resources for resisting that interpretation. It treats justice as a virtue and emphasizes duties to other people. Working to change an unfair rule is compatible with Stoicism, even if you can’t guarantee success. You’re responsible for deciding whether to act and how to act; the result will also depend on circumstances and other people.

Still, the criticism points to a genuine tension in the philosophy. If you place enormous weight on inner freedom and treat external circumstances as secondary, you can understate how much poverty, illness, and oppression damage people’s lives. Popular versions make that easier when they focus on personal discipline and leave out justice.

The distinction between “controllable” and “uncontrollable” also needs care. Many things are open to influence, especially through collective action, even though no individual can control them. An employee may have little bargaining power alone and considerably more alongside coworkers. Calling working conditions “outside your control” too early can close off possibilities worth pursuing.

I’d judge the advice by what it helps someone do. Does it help them face the situation clearly, decide what’s worth changing, and endure the uncertainty of trying? Or does it make them feel guilty for objecting? Stoic acceptance can mean acknowledging the conditions you have to work with while you try to change them. But some modern Stoic content does encourage resignation, and invoking ancient philosophy doesn’t excuse that.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets a lot right about how easily we exhaust ourselves chasing approval or dwelling on things we can’t change. Its advice to examine our judgments and take responsibility for our actions can make everyday frustrations easier to handle. But it puts too much weight on our ability to remain inwardly unaffected by hardship. Poverty, grief, and unfair treatment genuinely hurt, and accepting them can become an excuse to leave them unchallenged. I’d keep its tools for thinking clearly while making room for anger, dependence on others, and the work of changing our circumstances.

## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits a lot of what people have been feeling lately: uncertainty, exhaustion, and the sense that events far outside their control can wreck their day. Its advice to focus on your own judgments and actions gives people something manageable to work with.

A few things have helped it spread:

- **It’s easy to turn into online content.** A short passage from Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus can stand on its own in a newsletter, podcast, or Instagram post. You don’t need much background to find it useful, even if you miss some of the philosophy around it.
- **There’s a large business promoting it.** Writers and podcasters such as Ryan Holiday have introduced Stoicism to huge audiences through books, daily emails, and products. Once you engage with that content, recommendation feeds can make it feel ubiquitous.
- **It speaks to anxiety without requiring people to call it that.** “Practice responding calmly to setbacks” can appeal to someone who would never pick up a book about emotional regulation. That has helped it travel through business, fitness, and some male-oriented self-help circles.
- **It suits a culture focused on performance.** Athletes, entrepreneurs, and productivity writers like its emphasis on discipline and handling failure. Ancient Roman quotations also give familiar self-help advice a sense of authority.
- **Recent crises gave its advice an immediate use.** The pandemic, economic insecurity, and a relentless news cycle made the distinction between what you can influence and what you can’t feel especially relevant.

There’s also a historical coincidence that helps: Marcus Aurelius wrote privately about frustration, mortality, and dealing with difficult people. Reading him can feel surprisingly like encountering someone having the same bad workday nearly two thousand years ago.

The version circulating online is often narrower than the ancient philosophy. Stoics cared about virtue, justice, and duties to other people, as well as composure. Popular accounts sometimes reduce that to staying productive and being unbothered. Stoicism also doesn’t ask you to feel nothing, though the everyday meaning of “stoic” makes that misunderstanding easy.

So your impression makes sense: there’s genuine demand for its advice, a publishing industry supplying it, and feeds that keep serving it to people who show interest.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

I think it’s a useful philosophy for everyday life, though I’d be cautious about making it your whole worldview. The self-help trend draws on ideas that genuinely help, but it often leaves out the harder questions about what makes a good life.

The most useful Stoic habit is separating what you can do from outcomes you can’t guarantee. You can prepare for a job interview; you can’t make someone hire you. That distinction can reduce needless worry while leaving you responsible for making an effort.

Another useful habit is examining your first interpretation of an event. “My friend hasn’t replied” is a fact; “they don’t care about me” is an interpretation. You don’t have to accept the interpretation just because it arrived with a strong feeling. There’s overlap here with techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy, which was partly influenced by Stoic thought. That doesn’t mean every Stoic teaching has clinical evidence behind it.

Where I’d be more skeptical:

- It can become a reason to tolerate things you should try to change. You don’t fully control your workplace or your relationships, but you may still have influence, allies, or the option to leave. Accepting that something has happened doesn’t settle what you should do next.
- Its account of emotions is demanding. Ancient Stoics treated passions such as anger and fear as involving mistaken judgments. They allowed for involuntary reactions and certain good feelings, but their ideal of emotional life is stricter than “feel your feelings, then choose how to act.” Some people find that helpful; others end up policing themselves for being upset.
- Its central moral claim is a lot to accept: virtue is the only true good. Health, money, and even the welfare of loved ones don’t determine whether you have a good life in the Stoic sense, although Stoics generally thought you should care for and pursue those things. You can appreciate its coping practices without agreeing with that claim.

For someone who ruminates or gets knocked off course by criticism, Stoic exercises may be especially useful. Someone who already suppresses their feelings or accepts mistreatment might need more room for grief, anger, and asking other people for help.

My recommendation would be to borrow its practices and question its promises. Try pausing before reacting, checking your assumptions, and judging yourself by the care you took rather than solely by the result. Keep noticing whether those habits help you engage with your life, or whether you’re using them to withdraw from it.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair, especially when Stoicism is marketed as a way to endure work or social conditions that deserve to change. If someone responds to an unreasonable workload with “You can’t control your boss, only your reaction,” they may be giving you one useful coping tool while ignoring the possibility of negotiating, organizing with colleagues, or leaving.

There’s an important ambiguity in the word “accept.” Recognizing that a bad situation exists can help you decide what to do. Believing you ought to put up with it is a separate judgment. You can acknowledge that your boss is treating you badly and still file a complaint. You don’t have to believe the complaint will succeed for it to be worth making.

Ancient Stoicism leaves room for that kind of action. Justice was one of its central virtues, and Stoics understood people as having obligations to others. Some participated in politics or resisted rulers, sometimes at considerable personal cost. Their emphasis on accepting outcomes was compatible with trying to change them.

Still, I wouldn’t completely let the philosophy off the hook. When you teach that your character matters more than your circumstances, and that distress depends heavily on your judgments, you create a risk: people may start treating their suffering as a failure of attitude. Modern self-help can make that worse by focusing almost entirely on the individual. Questions about who has power, who benefits, and what people could accomplish together disappear from view.

A useful test is to ask what the advice helps you do. Does it help you stay steady enough to confront a problem? Or does it keep sending you back to work on your attitude while the problem goes untouched? And who is offering it? Advice you choose to help yourself through hardship deserves a different level of scrutiny from the same advice offered by someone who benefits from your compliance.

I’d keep the Stoic idea that you can act without being guaranteed success. I’d be much more wary of any version that treats being upset about injustice as the main problem to solve.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something right: we can’t guarantee outcomes, and judging ourselves by the care we take can spare us a lot of needless anxiety. Its advice to pause and question our first reactions is useful in ordinary life. But its emphasis on our judgments can make suffering seem like a failure of attitude, especially in modern self-help versions. Bad working conditions or unfair treatment may call for collective action, and anger can help us recognize that something needs to change. I’d borrow Stoicism’s tools for staying steady while remaining skeptical of the idea that peace of mind is always the best response.

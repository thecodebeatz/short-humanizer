## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits the mood of the last few years: lots of uncertainty, a sense that institutions are unreliable, and a steady stream of things to be angry or anxious about. Its most familiar advice, to focus on what you can control, gives people something manageable to do with all that.

A few things have helped it spread:

- **It’s easy to package for the internet.** A passage from Marcus Aurelius can fit on an Instagram card or become a thirty-second video. You don’t need much background to get something out of it, and quotes about handling setbacks travel well.

- **There’s a publishing and podcast business behind it.** Ryan Holiday’s books and *The Daily Stoic* have done a lot to bring Stoicism to a general audience. Business, sports, and self-help podcasts give it repeated exposure, often through the same handful of ideas.

- **It appeals to people who want practical guidance without religion.** Stoicism offers exercises for dealing with anger, loss, status anxiety, and mortality. Although the ancient Stoics had religious and metaphysical beliefs, modern versions often leave those out.

- **It fits productivity culture.** Advice about discipline and staying steady under pressure appeals to entrepreneurs, athletes, and people trying to improve their lives. That also makes it easy to sell as a way to perform better at work, even when the ancient philosophy was concerned with whether your ambitions were worth pursuing.

- **It gives some men an accessible way into emotional self-help.** Reading a Roman emperor about grief or fear can feel more approachable than picking up a book marketed around vulnerability. The military and athletic associations help, though they can also encourage the mistaken idea that Stoicism means suppressing your feelings.

There’s also a feedback loop in your sense that it’s “everywhere.” Once you engage with a few Stoicism posts, recommendation systems may serve you more, alongside adjacent content about habits and self-discipline. So a real rise in visibility can feel even bigger in your particular feed.

The version that spreads most easily is selective. Ancient Stoicism put moral character and obligations to other people at the center of a good life. Online, it often gets reduced to “stay calm and get on with it.” That’s useful advice in some situations, but it leaves out much of what made Stoicism a philosophy in the first place.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

Yes, Stoicism can be a good philosophy for ordinary life. Its advice about frustration, fear, and disappointment is useful even if you don’t accept the whole system. The self-help industry has picked up on something genuinely helpful, though it often sells a narrow version of it.

Take an everyday situation: you prepare carefully for a job interview and don’t get the job. A Stoic approach is to judge your preparation and conduct separately from the hiring decision. You can learn from the outcome without treating it as a verdict on your worth. That’s a reasonable habit, especially if you tend to replay setbacks for days.

It also encourages you to question your first interpretation of events. “My friend hasn’t replied” is an observation; “my friend doesn’t care about me” is a judgment you’ve added. You can notice the hurt without immediately accepting the story behind it. Related techniques appear in cognitive behavioral therapy, whose early developers drew partly on Stoic ideas.

The reservations matter, though:

- The strict ancient view is demanding. It holds that virtue is the only true good; health, wealth, and even the survival of people you love don’t determine whether you have a good life. Stoics could strongly prefer and work to protect those things, but many people reasonably find that account of loss unconvincing.
- “Focus on what you control” can become an excuse to tolerate something you could help change. You don’t control your employer or government, but you can still organize with others and influence them. Ancient Stoic ethics included duties to other people, which motivational posts often leave out.
- Constantly monitoring your judgments can turn into another reason to criticize yourself. If you’re grieving or depressed, feeling awful doesn’t mean you’ve failed at philosophy. The popular instruction to “choose your response” can exaggerate how much immediate control people have over their emotions.

I’d treat Stoicism as a set of practices worth trying before committing to its stronger claims. Pause before acting on anger. Put effort into things you can influence. Ask whether you behaved fairly, even when behaving fairly cost you something.

Then judge it by what happens in your life. If it helps you handle disappointment while remaining affectionate and willing to ask for help, it’s doing you some good. If you start feeling ashamed of having needs, or use “acceptance” to avoid a difficult conversation, reconsider how you’re applying it.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair, especially when Stoicism gets presented as advice for employees, people struggling financially, or anyone with little power. “You can’t control what happens, only your response” can become a convenient message for whoever benefits from things staying as they are.

The problem is that “outside your control” covers very different things. You can’t undo a bad decision someone made yesterday. You may be able to prevent them from making it again. And although you can’t personally guarantee better working conditions, joining other people to demand them can make a difference. If you treat everything you can’t guarantee as something you should simply accept, you give up much of your ability to act.

Ancient Stoicism has resources for resisting that conclusion. Justice was one of its central virtues, and Stoics understood people to have duties to one another. Marcus Aurelius had substantial political power; his reminders to endure difficulties existed alongside responsibilities to govern. Still, that doesn’t establish that Stoicism reliably produces good politics. Its emphasis on maintaining your character under any circumstances can make the circumstances themselves seem less urgent than they should be.

There’s also a difference between advice you choose for yourself and advice someone gives you about your suffering. “I’ll try not to let this consume me while I work out what to do” can be helpful. A boss telling an overworked employee to practice acceptance deserves much more suspicion. The employee may need fewer hours or more support.

My test would be: **does this use of Stoicism help someone act, or give them a reason to stop trying?** Staying calm might help you document mistreatment, confront someone, or sustain a long campaign whose outcome is uncertain. But anger can also tell you that something needs attention. A philosophy becomes limiting when it teaches you to dismiss that signal before you’ve examined what caused it.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something right about everyday life: we often make ourselves miserable trying to control outcomes we can only influence. Learning to pause, question our assumptions, and live with uncertainty can spare us a lot of needless distress. But the advice goes wrong when acceptance becomes a reason to tolerate mistreatment or feel ashamed of grief and anger. Some situations deserve resistance, and some feelings deserve time. Stoicism is useful when it helps us face a difficult situation and decide what to do; it becomes limiting when staying calm takes priority over changing what’s wrong.

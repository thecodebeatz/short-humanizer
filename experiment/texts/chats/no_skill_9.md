## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

It’s not just you. Stoicism has become a kind of default philosophy for self-improvement culture—and it’s unusually well suited to both the stresses of modern life and the way ideas spread online.

A few forces have come together:

- **It offers a sense of agency in unstable times.** When politics, work, money, and the news feel out of your control, “focus on what you can control” is a compelling message. The pandemic likely strengthened that appeal, though the revival was already underway.

- **It’s practical without requiring religious belief.** You can read Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus and come away with something to try today: examine a judgment, anticipate a setback, or reconsider whether someone’s opinion deserves so much power over you. That makes it accessible to people who want guidance but aren’t drawn to religion or academic philosophy.

- **It fits the internet extremely well.** Short quotations about adversity, discipline, and mortality work beautifully as posts, podcast clips, and motivational videos. The nuance doesn’t always survive, but the memorable lines do.

- **A modern publishing and podcast ecosystem has promoted it.** Writers such as Ryan Holiday and William B. Irvine, along with entrepreneurs, athletes, and influencers, have packaged Stoic ideas for contemporary audiences. Once those audiences overlap, it can start to feel ubiquitous.

- **It meshes with productivity and fitness culture.** Discipline, resilience, and not being ruled by discomfort are easy to connect to training, leadership, and career ambition. There’s a tension here, though: ancient Stoicism treated virtue—not wealth, status, or maximum output—as the good life.

- **For some people, especially men, it offers an approachable language for emotional struggle.** Talking about judgment, courage, and self-command can feel more comfortable than talking about vulnerability. At its best, that opens a door to reflection; at its worst, it becomes a justification for bottling everything up.

That last distinction matters: **Stoicism isn’t simply “don’t feel anything.”** The ancient philosophy asks you to examine your judgments and act wisely and justly, not become indifferent to people or tolerate every bad situation.

So the short answer is: it meets a real need for steadiness, it’s easy to turn into practical advice, and it has a very effective modern marketing machine. And once you engage with a little of it, recommendation algorithms can make it look like the whole world has started reading Marcus Aurelius.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It’s a genuinely useful philosophy for ordinary life—and also a self-help trend.** The trend draws on real strengths, but often strips away the parts that make Stoicism more than a collection of motivational quotes.

You don’t need to be an entrepreneur, soldier, or endurance athlete to benefit from it. Some of its best applications are mundane: getting criticism at work, sitting in traffic, caring for someone who’s ill, or resisting the urge to send an angry message.

Three ideas are especially useful:

- **Distinguish what you can influence from what you can’t dictate.** You can prepare for an interview; you can’t guarantee an offer. That distinction helps you act without making your peace of mind entirely dependent on the result.
- **Examine your first interpretation.** “My friend hasn’t replied” is an observation; “they don’t care about me” is a judgment. Stoicism encourages you to pause between the two—not to assume your concerns are always wrong.
- **Make character more important than applause.** Being fair, honest, and courageous is a more stable aim than being admired or successful. This is arguably its deepest teaching, and the one that productivity-focused versions tend to neglect.

But it has limits, especially when applied rigidly:

- **Acceptance can slide into resignation.** A bad workplace or unfair relationship isn’t necessarily something to endure more gracefully. You may not control the outcome, but leaving, organizing, protesting, or asking for help can still be the right action.
- **Emotional discipline can become emotional avoidance.** Grief, anger, and fear aren’t simply failures of reasoning. Sometimes you need to feel something, talk it through, or change your circumstances—not give yourself a lecture.
- **Self-command isn’t the whole good life.** People also need affection, play, belonging, and support. Ancient Stoics valued social duty, but some modern presentations make life sound like a solitary toughness contest.

There are also parts of ancient Stoicism—such as its account of a rationally ordered cosmos—that many modern readers won’t accept. You can borrow its practices without adopting the whole system, though then you’re using Stoic tools rather than embracing the complete philosophy.

**My take: Stoicism is a good guide to responding to difficulty, but a less convincing answer to every human need.** Use it to become less reactive and more principled, not less emotionally available or more willing to put up with harm. The test isn’t “Am I unbothered?” It’s “Am I handling this wisely while still caring?”

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is **fair about some modern Stoicism, but not about Stoicism as a whole**. The key question is: *what are you being asked to accept?*

There’s a big difference between:
- **Accepting that a situation is real**, so you can respond effectively.
- **Accepting that a situation is right or must continue**, so you shouldn’t challenge it.

Stoicism can support the first without requiring the second. You can acknowledge that your workplace is exploitative, that changing it will be difficult, and that you can’t guarantee success—while still organizing with coworkers or looking for another job. Acceptance of reality isn’t approval of it.

The classical emphasis on **justice and courage** matters here. If your calm comes at the cost of ignoring mistreatment, including mistreatment of other people, it’s hard to call that a fully Stoic success. Being unbothered isn’t the highest goal; acting well is.

**But the criticism identifies a genuine danger in how Stoicism gets marketed.** If every problem becomes “manage your reaction,” then understaffing becomes a resilience problem, discrimination becomes a mindset problem, and burnout becomes a failure of discipline. That shifts responsibility from institutions onto the people bearing the costs. Advice that helps someone get through a bad day can become an excuse not to fix the conditions producing bad days.

There’s also a misleading leap in the slogan “focus only on what you control.” Most worthwhile action involves things we can **influence but not control**: relationships, elections, workplace conditions, public health. If uncertain outcomes were a reason not to act, we’d do very little. A better formulation is: **take responsibility for your contribution without expecting to command the result.**

So I’d judge a version of Stoicism by what it encourages you to do:
- Does it help you face a problem clearly and choose a worthwhile response?
- Or does it make you feel morally superior for enduring something that ought to change?

Sometimes enduring is necessary, especially when someone has few safe options. But endurance is a coping strategy—not proof that the situation is acceptable. A useful Stoicism should help people preserve their agency, not teach them to surrender it.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we can’t dictate everything that happens to us, but we can work on how we judge situations and choose to act. That’s a useful foundation for resilience, perspective, and integrity. Where it goes wrong—especially in its modern self-help form—is when emotional discipline becomes emotional suppression, or acceptance becomes an excuse to tolerate injustice. Not every problem can be solved by changing your mindset; sometimes the circumstances need changing. Stoicism is at its best when it helps us act wisely despite uncertainty, not when it teaches us to endure everything without complaint.

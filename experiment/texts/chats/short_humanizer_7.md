## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits several pressures people feel right now, and it packages well for the internet.

A few things have helped it spread:

- A sense of having little control. Political turmoil, the pandemic and economic insecurity have made "focus on what you can control" an appealing piece of advice. Stoicism gives people a way to think about their own actions when larger events feel overwhelming.

- Exhaustion with constant reaction. Social media encourages people to take offense, compare themselves with others and respond immediately. Stoic advice about pausing before judgment offers some relief. There's an irony here: the same platforms that stir people up also circulate quotes about staying calm.

- A familiar self-help format with historical credibility. Marcus Aurelius was a Roman emperor writing about frustration, duty and difficult people. His circumstances give the advice weight, while his personal struggles make it recognizable. A short passage from *Meditations* can feel surprisingly relevant to an unpleasant workday.

- Successful modern promoters. Writers and podcasters, especially Ryan Holiday, have made Stoic ideas accessible through books, newsletters and daily exercises. That gives people an easy entry point without requiring them to work through ancient philosophy.

- Its fit with achievement culture. Entrepreneurs, athletes and productivity influencers can readily apply Stoic ideas about discipline and enduring discomfort. Those ideas work well in a culture that treats self-improvement as an ongoing project.

There's also a specifically male audience for some of this. Stoicism offers a language for discussing distress that emphasizes agency and self-respect. That can appeal to men who feel uncomfortable with the language of therapy. Some online versions push it toward emotional suppression or fantasies of invulnerability, though ancient Stoicism asks people to examine their judgments and includes duties toward other people.

The version circulating today is selective. Ancient Stoicism was a broad philosophy involving ethics, nature and our obligations to others. Online, it often becomes a handful of coping techniques or advice about being disciplined. That narrower version is easier to sell and share, and it's also easier to mistake for the whole philosophy.

As for why it feels *everywhere*: once you engage with a few posts about resilience, fitness or productivity, recommendation systems can serve you much more Stoic content. Its visibility comes partly from genuine interest and partly from how easily a quotation fits onto an image or into a short video.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

Yes, it's a good practical philosophy for ordinary life, though I would be cautious about adopting it wholesale.

Its most useful idea is to distinguish what you can choose from what you can only influence. You can prepare for a job interview; you can't decide whether someone hires you. That distinction can help you act without making your peace of mind depend entirely on the outcome. The Stoic habit of examining your first reaction is useful too: "They haven't replied" is an observation; "They don't respect me" is an interpretation you can question.

It also gives you a demanding ethical standard. For the ancient Stoics, a good life centered on acting with wisdom, justice, courage and moderation. A person could be calm, productive and wealthy while behaving badly by that standard. Much of the online material leaves this ethical side out.

There are a few places where I'd be careful:

- Emotions can become something to police. Ancient Stoic accounts of emotion are more complicated than "don't feel anything," but their ideal of freedom from destructive passions is demanding. For some people, practicing Stoicism becomes another reason to criticize themselves for feeling grief, anxiety or anger.
- Acceptance can slide into resignation. You may not control your workplace or relationship, but you can sometimes change its conditions, leave it or seek help. Applying "focus on yourself" too broadly can keep you in a situation you should challenge.
- Its view of loss is hard to accept fully. Strict Stoicism treats health, money and even the lives of loved ones as things that matter for our preferences but don't determine whether our lives are morally good. There's a coherent argument behind that position, but it can feel emotionally inadequate when someone is suffering.

My view is that Stoicism works well as a set of practices within a broader outlook. Use it to question a panicked interpretation or handle a setback. Keep room for grief, dependence on other people and efforts to change unfair conditions. If reading Marcus Aurelius helps you respond more thoughtfully to someone you love, that's a useful result. If it makes you ashamed of needing their support, I'd question how you're applying it.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair about some modern Stoic advice, especially when it turns every problem into a test of your attitude.

If your boss routinely mistreats you, "you control your response" can help you get through a difficult encounter. But it leaves practical questions unanswered: Can you document what's happening? Organize with coworkers? Find another job? Advice that keeps returning to your reaction can make the conditions themselves seem beyond challenge.

There's also a problem with who delivers the advice. When an employer tells stressed workers to practice resilience, the employer may be shifting responsibility onto people who have little power over their workload. The same exercise might genuinely help someone who chooses it for themselves. Who gets asked to adapt, and who has the power to change things, matters.

Ancient Stoicism has resources for resisting that passivity. Justice is a central virtue, and our duties extend to other people. Its distinction between our choices and external outcomes allows for determined action: you can work to stop an injustice while accepting that you can't guarantee success. Having limited control over an outcome doesn't mean you have no influence over it.

Still, ancient Stoicism does put extraordinary weight on a person's judgments as the basis of their well-being. That can understate how much safety, material conditions and relationships affect people's ability to live well. The criticism reaches beyond shallow social media quotes.

I'd judge the practice by what it helps you do. If accepting that a confrontation will be uncomfortable helps you raise a complaint, it's useful. If "acceptance" repeatedly means swallowing mistreatment and blaming yourself for being upset, the criticism applies.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: examining our judgments and focusing on the choices available to us can help us handle uncertainty and setbacks. Its emphasis on justice also gives us reasons to act responsibly toward others. But it puts too much weight on our attitude as the basis of well-being, underestimating how much our circumstances matter. In its thinner modern forms, it can make grief seem like a failure of discipline and encourage people to tolerate conditions they could challenge or change.

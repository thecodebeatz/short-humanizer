## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism fits a lot of what people have been feeling lately: overwhelmed, distracted, and unable to influence the things that worry them. Its advice to focus on your own judgments and actions is easy to grasp when the news, your job, or your social feed feels out of control.

A few things have helped it spread:

- **It gives uncertainty a manageable scale.** You can’t settle an election or guarantee that your job is safe. You can decide how to prepare and how to treat people. The pandemic made that distinction especially relevant, though the modern Stoic revival was already underway.

- **It fits the self-improvement market.** Writers and podcasters, especially Ryan Holiday, have made ancient Stoic ideas accessible through books, daily emails, and short videos. A passage from Marcus Aurelius is easy to turn into a daily exercise or a shareable quote. You don’t need to work through a whole philosophical system to get something useful from it.

- **It speaks to anxiety without requiring religious belief.** Stoicism offers guidance on suffering, mortality, and how to live. Although ancient Stoics had religious and metaphysical beliefs, modern versions often leave those out. That gives people a source of moral guidance they can use without joining a religion.

- **It overlaps with familiar psychological advice.** Examining your interpretations, questioning first impressions, and pausing before reacting resemble practices used in cognitive behavioral therapy. Some of CBT’s founders explicitly drew on Stoic ideas. That connection helps make Stoicism feel practical and credible.

There’s also a particular appeal in fitness, business, and male self-improvement circles. The language of discipline and endurance gives people a way to talk about distress that feels compatible with being strong or capable. Sometimes that opens a useful door; sometimes it turns into advice to suppress emotions or tolerate conditions they should try to change.

And “everywhere” is partly how feeds work. Stoic quotes are short, recognizable, and easy to post, so they circulate well. Once you engage with a few, recommendation systems can make a substantial niche look universal.

The version that travels best is often narrower than the ancient philosophy. Marcus Aurelius was concerned with duty to other people, fairness, and our dependence on one another, alongside self-discipline. Those obligations tend to get less attention than advice about staying calm and getting things done.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

I think it’s a good source of everyday guidance, though I’d be cautious about adopting it as a complete philosophy. The self-help trend draws on some genuinely useful ideas, while giving much less attention to their limits.

The most useful Stoic habit is asking what you can do about a situation. If you’re waiting for medical results or dealing with someone’s disapproval, separating your choices from the outcome can keep worry from taking over your day. You still prepare, ask questions, or have the difficult conversation. You accept that doing everything reasonably well doesn’t guarantee the result.

It’s also helpful to distinguish an event from your first interpretation of it. “My friend hasn’t replied” is an observation; “my friend doesn’t care about me” is a conclusion. Stoicism encourages you to examine that conclusion before acting on it. That’s useful even if you never read Marcus Aurelius.

Where I’d be more skeptical is its stricter account of a good life. Ancient Stoics held that virtue is the only true good, and that things such as health, wealth, and reputation don’t determine whether you live well. They could still prefer health to illness, but that’s a demanding position. Most people have good reason to think that bereavement, poverty, and illness can make life genuinely worse, even when someone faces them admirably.

The popular advice can also go wrong in ordinary ways:

- “Focus on what you control” can become an excuse to avoid trying to change something. You may not control your workplace, but you can still negotiate, organize with colleagues, or leave.
- Trying to correct every painful judgment can turn into judging yourself for feeling pain. Grief and anger don’t need to disappear on a philosophical timetable.
- Constant self-monitoring can become exhausting, especially if you already tend to blame yourself.

Ancient Stoicism includes duties to other people and participation in society, so passive resignation is a poor reading of it. Still, some modern versions make resignation sound virtuous.

For everyday use, I’d borrow its habits without accepting all its claims: pause before reacting, examine your assumptions, and judge your conduct partly by the choices you made given what you knew. Leave room for needing other people, grieving losses, and changing your circumstances. If practicing Stoicism helps you act with more patience and courage, it’s doing something worthwhile. If it mostly makes you ashamed of having feelings, I’d reconsider how you’re using it.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism is fair about some modern Stoic advice, especially when it treats every problem as a matter of attitude. A bad job, an unfair relationship, or financial insecurity can require changes that better emotional habits won’t accomplish.

“Focus on what you control” gets slippery here. You don’t control whether your employer improves working conditions. But you might influence that outcome by joining with coworkers, making a complaint, or negotiating. Most worthwhile action involves outcomes we can influence without controlling. If we only pursued guaranteed results, we’d give up on a lot of things worth doing.

There’s also a question of who is giving the advice. Someone choosing to endure a difficult situation while they work toward an exit may find Stoicism helpful. A boss telling an overworked employee to change their mindset has a convenient reason to leave working conditions out of the discussion. The advice can sound identical while serving very different purposes.

Ancient Stoicism gives grounds for challenging that use. Justice was one of its central virtues, and Stoics understood people as having obligations to one another. Accepting that something has happened doesn’t settle whether you should resist its continuation. Still, ancient Stoics lived within deeply hierarchical societies, and their philosophy wasn’t a modern program for structural reform. It would be too generous to pretend the tension is entirely a social-media distortion.

My test would be: **does practicing it help you respond to the situation, or does it keep you in it?** Calming yourself enough to confront someone, organize, or make a difficult exit seems useful. Repeatedly telling yourself that mistreatment shouldn’t bother you is a reason to question the advice. And when someone has few realistic options, finding a way to endure can be valuable without making the situation acceptable.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets a lot right about how we react to difficulty: our first interpretation isn’t always accurate, and worrying about an outcome won’t necessarily help us change it. Taking a breath and considering what we can do is often good advice. But the modern version can put too much responsibility on the person struggling, as though a better attitude could solve an unfair workplace or an unhealthy relationship. We can influence things we don’t control, especially by acting together. Stoicism is useful when it helps us face a problem; it becomes limiting when it gives us reasons to keep putting up with it.

## Turn 1

**User:** Why has stoicism gotten so popular in the last few years? It feels like it's everywhere.

**Assistant:**

A few things have converged: **Stoicism offers a simple, usable response to a world that often feels out of control—and it’s unusually easy to package for the internet.**

Here are the main drivers:

- **It fits an anxious, uncertain era.** Pandemic disruption, political conflict, economic insecurity, and constant bad news make “focus on what you can control” an appealing principle. It gives people something to *do* without promising they can fix everything.

- **It’s practical without requiring religious belief.** Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, and Seneca offer exercises for handling anger, setbacks, status anxiety, and mortality. For people who want guidance but aren’t drawn to religion—or to abstract academic philosophy—that’s an accessible entry point.

- **It overlaps with familiar mental-health ideas.** Examining your judgments, pausing before reacting, and distinguishing events from your interpretations resemble techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy, whose early developers drew partly on Stoic thought. That gives some of its advice a familiar, practical feel, though Stoicism isn’t a substitute for treatment.

- **A modern publishing and podcast ecosystem has promoted it effectively.** Writers such as Ryan Holiday and popularizers such as Tim Ferriss have brought it into business, sports, productivity, and self-improvement. Books, newsletters, podcasts, and daily quotations keep it in circulation.

- **It’s extremely compatible with social media.** A short quotation about discipline or adversity works well over a picture of a Roman statue. The format favors memorable maxims, even when they’re stripped of context—or misattributed. Once you engage with a few, recommendation algorithms can make it feel omnipresent.

- **It appeals to contemporary ideals of toughness and self-mastery.** That’s especially visible in male-oriented fitness and entrepreneurship spaces. Some of this is constructive: resilience, responsibility, less dependence on approval. Some of it turns into a much thinner message: suppress your feelings, work harder, never need anyone.

That last point is an important distinction. **Ancient Stoicism wasn’t simply “be unemotional and productive.”** It treated virtue as central and emphasized justice, social responsibility, and our obligations to other people. Much of today’s “pop Stoicism” selects the parts about personal control and leaves those commitments in the background.

So its popularity is both a genuine response to modern needs and a successful branding story. It gives people a sense of agency; the internet turns that into an easily recognizable lifestyle.

## Turn 2

**User:** Is it actually a good philosophy for normal people, or mostly a self-help trend?

**Assistant:**

**It can be a good philosophy for ordinary life—but it’s better as a thoughtful practice than as a “be invulnerable” lifestyle.** Its popularity doesn’t make it empty, and its ancient pedigree doesn’t make every part of it right.

The most useful parts address very ordinary problems:

- **Separating what you can influence from what you can’t.** You can prepare for an interview, but you can’t guarantee the offer. That distinction helps you put effort where it matters without treating every outcome as a verdict on your worth.
- **Questioning your first interpretation.** “My friend hasn’t replied” is a fact; “they don’t care about me” is an interpretation. Stoicism encourages a pause between the two.
- **Making character more important than status.** Being fair, honest, and dependable is a sturdier aim than always being admired or successful.
- **Accepting discomfort without immediately escaping it.** Disappointment, embarrassment, and uncertainty don’t always need to be eliminated before you can act well.

Those are substantial ideas, not just motivational slogans.

**Where it can go wrong is in what people take “acceptance” to mean.** Accepting that something has happened isn’t the same as approving of it or refusing to change it. You don’t control whether your boss behaves fairly, but you may be able to challenge them, organize with coworkers, or leave. “Not fully under my control” shouldn’t become “not my responsibility.”

Likewise, emotional steadiness can slide into emotional avoidance. Ancient Stoics didn’t simply tell people to feel nothing, but their ideal of freedom from certain passions is more demanding than modern advice to “notice your feelings.” You can reasonably borrow their techniques without accepting their whole account of emotion—or the ancient belief in a providentially ordered universe.

There’s also a legitimate philosophical objection: **Stoicism may understate how much a good life depends on things outside our control**, such as health, safety, affection, and supportive relationships. Its insistence that virtue alone is sufficient for happiness is inspiring to some people and implausibly austere to others.

My practical verdict: use Stoicism to become **less reactive, more courageous, and more considerate—not less emotionally available or more willing to tolerate mistreatment**. If it helps you face a difficult conversation, it’s doing useful work. If it helps you convince yourself you shouldn’t need love, support, or fair treatment, something has gone wrong.

You don’t have to become “a Stoic” to benefit from it. It’s perfectly reasonable to treat it as one source of wisdom rather than a complete operating system for life.

## Turn 3

**User:** Some people say modern stoicism just teaches people to accept bad situations instead of changing them. What do you think?

**Assistant:**

I think that criticism identifies a real danger, **but it isn’t the only—or necessarily the best—reading of Stoicism.** The key question is: *What are you being asked to accept?*

There are three quite different things people can mean:

- **Accept the facts:** “This injustice is happening; wishing otherwise won’t undo it.”
- **Accept the limits of your power:** “I can work to change it, but I can’t guarantee success.”
- **Accept the situation as something you shouldn’t challenge:** “There’s no point resisting.”

Stoicism has strong reasons for the first two. It doesn’t have to imply the third.

In fact, the classical emphasis on **justice and courage** can support resistance. If your workplace is unsafe, a Stoic response could be to document the problem, organize with colleagues, and demand changes—while recognizing that you can’t control the employer’s response. The aim would be to act well despite uncertainty, not to become comfortable with being mistreated.

But critics are right that **modern Stoicism often emphasizes managing your reaction much more than changing your circumstances**. “Your judgments are the problem” can be useful when you’re stewing over an insult. It’s much less adequate when you’re facing abuse, discrimination, or poverty. Those involve real harms, not merely unhelpful interpretations. Advice focused exclusively on mindset can shift responsibility from the people or institutions causing harm onto the person experiencing it.

There’s also a subtler issue: even advice that helps someone cope can leave the source of their distress untouched. An employer’s “resilience” workshop might genuinely help employees *and* serve as an excuse not to fix unreasonable workloads. Both can be true.

So I wouldn’t defend Stoicism by claiming that every passive interpretation is just a misunderstanding. **Its inward focus really does make it vulnerable to being used that way**, especially when detached from its ethical obligations to others.

My test would be: **Does this advice help you face reality and act more effectively, or does it teach you to stop asking whether your circumstances should change?** Learning not to be consumed by anger can support action. Learning to regard every grievance as a personal failure of composure can undermine it.

## Turn 4

**User:** Write me a short paragraph for my blog on what stoicism gets right and what it gets wrong.

**Assistant:**

Stoicism gets something important right: we live better when we distinguish what we can influence from what we can’t, question our first reactions, and value character over status. But its emphasis on inner freedom can understate how deeply well-being depends on relationships, material security, and fair treatment. Especially in its modern self-help form, it risks turning injustice into a mindset problem and acceptance into resignation. At its best, Stoicism helps us face hardship without losing our judgment; at its worst, it teaches us to endure conditions we ought to change. The goal should be steadiness in the service of action—not composure at any cost.
